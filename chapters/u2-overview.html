Unit 2 — Energy Flows & Matter Cycles

Unit 2 Overview

Every atom in your body was somewhere else a year ago, and will be somewhere else again. Every joule of energy keeping you alive arrived from the sun and will leave as heat. This unit follows both journeys, and shows why they are two different kinds of journey.

The title of this unit answers almost every question in it:

Energy flows. Matter cycles.

Energy arrives from outside, passes through, turns into heat and leaves for good, so it has to keep arriving. Matter never leaves. The same atoms are used again and again, rearranged into different molecules. If an explanation in this unit stops making sense, ask which of the two you are tracking. You are probably tracking the wrong one.

The phenomenon we keep coming back to

In 1960 a man called David Latimer put some compost into a ten-gallon glass carboy, a round bottle that had once held sulphuric acid, and lowered a spiderwort seedling in on a piece of wire. He added about a quarter of a pint of water. In 1972 he watered it once more, greased the bung, pushed it in tight and never opened it again.

The plant is still alive. It has filled the bottle. It has never been pruned, fed, watered or aired since the year the bung went in.

Think about what has to be true for that to work. The plant photosynthesises, so it is pulling carbon dioxide out of the bottle's air and building it into itself. It would eventually run out of carbon dioxide, except that it also respires, and so do the bacteria and fungi in the compost that break down its fallen leaves. Every carbon atom the plant fixes is eventually breathed back out by something, and the plant takes it up again. Water evaporates, condenses on the glass and runs back down. Nothing arrives and nothing leaves.

The only exceptions are light coming in and heat going out. Put the bottle in a dark cupboard and the plant would soon die. It would still have all its matter. It would die because the flow of energy had stopped.

Write down your best answers now. Rough answers are fine. Come back at the end of the unit and rewrite them.

  1. Where does the mass of a large tree actually come from? It is not the soil; a pot of soil loses almost no mass while a tree in it gains hundreds of kilograms.
  2. You eat a sandwich. Some of the carbon in it ends up as part of you. Where does the rest of it go? Say what it becomes, which route it takes and which part of your body it leaves by.
  3. A plant makes oxygen. Why does it also need oxygen?
  4. Why can the bottle recycle its atoms forever but not its energy?
  5. There are thousands of elk in Yellowstone and about a hundred wolves. Why so few wolves?

How the five standards fit together

Five NGSS performance expectations sit behind this unit. They look like five topics, but they build one argument in order, and each chapter takes the next step. The highlighted words show the skill each one asks for. Four ask you to use or build a model, and one asks for an explanation based on evidence. That is why most of the interactives in this reader ask you to build something and commit to it before they explain anything.

  • HS-LS1-5: How does photosynthesis turn light energy into stored chemical energy?
  • HS-LS1-7: How does breaking the bonds in food and oxygen, and making new ones, transfer energy?
  • HS-LS2-3: What changes when there is no oxygen?
  • HS-LS2-4: How much energy is left at each step up a food chain?
  • HS-LS2-5: How do photosynthesis and respiration move carbon between the air, the oceans, living things and the rocks?

Taken in order, they have a clear shape. The first two are a matched pair at the scale of a single cell: one process builds a fuel and the other burns it, and each is nearly the reverse of the other. The last two take the same pair of processes and ask what they do at the scale of a whole planet. Nothing new is introduced between 2.6 and 2.7. You simply zoom out.

Learning targets for the unit

These are the unit's "I can" statements, grouped by topic. Use them as a checklist. The number after each one is the chapter that teaches it. Practice marks a science and engineering practice, a skill scientists use. Crosscutting marks a crosscutting concept, an idea that applies across all of science.

Energy, matter and the bonds between

  • I can differentiate between energy and matter.
  • I can explain the idea of bond energy and differentiate between types of energy.
  • I can develop a model to illustrate transformations of energy and cycling of matter.
  • I can explain that energy cannot be created or destroyed; it only moves between one place and another place, between objects and/or fields, or between systems.

Cellular respiration

  • I can explain the importance of glucose.
  • I can state the inputs and outputs of cellular respiration.
  • I can describe cellular respiration using the chemical formula to show the inputs and outputs.
  • I can describe the role of mitochondria in carrying out cell respiration.
  • I can explain that breaking chemical bonds and creating new bonds to form new compounds results in energy transfer.
  • I can develop a model based on evidence to illustrate the relationships between systems or components of a system.

Energy through a food web

  • I can describe the movement of energy within a food web, justify the amount available at each trophic level.

What you will actually do

Each chapter has at least one interactive, and that is where most of the thinking happens. Each one asks you to commit to an answer before it explains anything, because you learn more from a model you got wrong first than from one you were handed.

  • 2.1 Model five everyday phenomena as inputs, outputs and one energy transformation.
  • 2.2 Label a leaf in cross-section, then say what every layer is for.
  • 2.3 Route nine molecules through a chloroplast and work out the totals per glucose.
  • 2.4 Design the leaf-disc experiment, then meet the real class data.
  • 2.5 Build the respiration model on a blank mitochondrion and total the ATP yourself.
  • 2.6 Run the yeast fermentation lab, build a CER, and find the balance point in an aquarium.
  • 2.7 Classify a Yellowstone food web, break it, and draw a pyramid to scale.
  • 2.8 Walk one carbon atom from a giraffe into a tree, and sort the reservoirs into spheres.
