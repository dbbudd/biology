Unit 4 — Reproduction

4.4 Same DNA, Different Cell

Chapter 4.3 ended on a puzzle it could not solve: every cell in your body carries an identical genome, and yet a neuron, a red blood cell and a skin cell could hardly be more different. This chapter resolves it — and the resolution turns out to be one of the most important ideas in biology, because it is also how a single fertilised egg builds a whole person.

Set the puzzle up properly first

Take the two facts separately, because each one is worth pausing on.

Fact one: every cell in your body has the same DNA. Not similar — the same. Mitosis makes exact copies, so the genome in a neuron in your spinal cord is a copy of a copy of a copy of the genome in the original zygote, and so is the genome in a cell lining your gut. That is why a single hair root can identify you, and why a cheek swab and a blood sample give the same result.

Fact two: your body contains around 200 distinguishable cell types, and they are not minor variations on a theme. A neuron may be a metre long and never divide again. A red blood cell has no nucleus at all and lives four months. A muscle fibre has many nuclei. A white blood cell can crawl.

Put them side by side and the problem is sharp:

If the instructions are identical, the difference cannot be in the instructions.

So it has to be in which instructions are being followed. That single move is the answer to this chapter, and the rest of it is working out what that means and what it costs.

Gene expression: which instructions are being followed

A gene is a stretch of DNA that codes for a protein, and a cell only makes that protein if the gene is being transcribed. When a gene is being transcribed and its protein made, the gene is said to be expressed — switched on. When it is not, the gene is still there, still perfect, and simply not being read.

So a cell type is defined by its gene expression pattern: the particular subset of its 20,000-odd genes that it keeps switched on.

  • A red blood cell expresses the haemoglobin genes at enormous rates. The insulin gene is present in the same cell and is switched off.
  • A pancreatic beta cell expresses insulin. Its haemoglobin genes are present and switched off.
  • Both cells contain both genes. Neither cell has lost anything.

Build a few cells and the idea stops being abstract.

Housekeeping genes are the ones every cell needs whatever else it does — the enzymes of respiration, the proteins of the cytoskeleton, the machinery for making more proteins. They stay on in every cell type in your body. Differentiation switches off the genes a cell has no use for; it never switches off the ones that keep it alive.

Differentiation: how a cell ends up with one pattern rather than another

Differentiation is the process by which a cell becomes specialised — the progressive switching on of some genes and switching off of others until the cell has a settled identity and a job.

Two features of it matter more than the definition.

It is directed from outside the cell

A cell does not decide what to become. What it becomes is determined by where it is — which chemical signals reach it from neighbouring cells, and how strong they are. A cell transplanted early in development to a different part of an embryo becomes what its new neighbourhood makes, not what it would have become where it started.

This is a genuine answer to a question that sounds unanswerable. There is no plan of the body written down anywhere. There is a chain of local signals, each cell responding to what is next to it, and the body is the result.

It narrows, and it is usually one-way

Follow one lineage forward and the options shrink at every step. A fertilised egg could become any cell. A cell in the early embryo could become any cell in the body. A blood-forming cell in your bone marrow can become any kind of blood cell, and nothing else. A mature red blood cell cannot become anything at all.

Stem cells, and what "potency" measures

A stem cell is an unspecialised cell with two properties at once:

  1. it can divide to produce more stem cells — it renews itself; and
  2. its descendants can differentiate into one or more specialised cell types.

Both halves are required. A cell that divides but cannot specialise is not a stem cell, and neither is a cell that specialises but cannot renew its own supply.

Potency is simply a name for how far down the narrowing in Figure 4.16 a stem cell already is — how many different cell types it can still become.

Why anyone cares: stem cells and repair

Most cells in your body cannot be replaced once they are gone. Heart muscle damaged by a heart attack is replaced by scar tissue, not by new heart muscle. Neurons lost to spinal injury are not replaced at all. That is what "differentiation is one-way" costs you.

Stem cells are interesting because they are the exception — and medicine has been trying for decades to use them.

  • Bone marrow transplants are the established case, and have been standard treatment for decades. Multipotent blood stem cells from a donor rebuild a patient's entire blood and immune system.
  • Skin grafts grown from a patient's own skin stem cells are used for severe burns.
  • Embryonic stem cells are pluripotent, so in principle they could repair anything. In practice they raise two serious problems: they are obtained from embryos, which many people object to on ethical grounds, and being from a different individual they can be rejected by the immune system.

Induced pluripotent stem cells

In 2006 Shinya Yamanaka showed something that had been thought impossible: an ordinary specialised adult cell — a skin cell — could be driven backwards into a pluripotent state by switching a small set of genes back on. The result is an induced pluripotent stem cell, or iPS cell. The work won the Nobel Prize in 2012.

Notice what this proves about the whole chapter. The skin cell had not lost the genes needed to be any other kind of cell — it still had all of them, which is exactly what this chapter has been claiming. Differentiation switched them off, and switching a few master genes back on undid it.

iPS cells also solve both problems with embryonic stem cells at once: no embryo is involved, and the cells are made from the patient's own tissue, so there is nothing for the immune system to reject.

Be careful how far you take this. iPS cells are a major research tool and are used to grow tissue for testing drugs and studying disease. Turning them into routine transplant treatments is much harder — cells that will divide indefinitely and can become anything are, by that description, uncomfortably close to the cells in Chapter 4.3, and controlling that risk is one of the central problems in the field.

Check your understanding

Why is a neuron different from a skin cell?

  1. Neurons contain genes that skin cells have lost
  2. Skin cells contain a different version of the genome
  3. Neurons have more chromosomes
  4. They express different subsets of the same set of genes

Both carry the identical genome, because both descend from the same zygote by mitosis. What differs is which genes are being transcribed. Nothing has been deleted from either cell.

A red blood cell pours out haemoglobin and makes no insulin. What is the state of the insulin gene inside that red blood cell?

  1. It was deleted as the cell specialised
  2. It is present but has been damaged so it cannot work
  3. It is present and intact, and is simply not being transcribed
  4. It is transcribed as normal, but the insulin is destroyed afterwards

Every cell of your body holds both genes. A red blood cell and a beta cell differ only in which of the two they read, which is why the same genome can specify 200 cell types.

A bone marrow stem cell can make every type of blood cell but cannot make a neuron. What is it?

  1. Totipotent
  2. Multipotent
  3. Pluripotent
  4. Not a stem cell at all

Multipotent means it can still become a limited family of related types. Pluripotent would mean any body cell; totipotent would include the placenta.

A cell taken from the inner cell mass of a blastocyst renews itself in culture and can be driven to form neurons, muscle and gut lining, but never placenta. What is it?

  1. Totipotent
  2. Multipotent
  3. A specialised cell of the embryo
  4. Pluripotent

Neurons, muscle and gut lining come from the three different germ layers, so this cell has not yet narrowed to one branch. The crossed-out placenta is the line between pluripotent and totipotent.

What do induced pluripotent stem cells demonstrate about differentiation?

  1. A specialised cell still contains all its genes, and switching some back on can reverse its specialisation
  2. Specialised cells regain genes they had previously lost
  3. Differentiation cannot be reversed under any circumstances
  4. Adult cells contain a second, hidden genome

Nothing is regained, because nothing was lost. Reprogramming works precisely because the genes were only switched off, which is the central claim of this chapter.

Suppose differentiation had worked by deleting the genes a cell does not need. What would that have meant for Yamanaka's experiment?

  1. Nothing — switching the master genes on would have rebuilt the missing ones
  2. It could not have worked, because a skin cell would no longer carry the genes the other cell types need
  3. The skin cell would have become some other specialised type instead
  4. It would have worked, but only with genes supplied from a donor embryo

An experiment can test a claim by what it makes possible. Reprogramming succeeds only in a body where specialisation is a matter of switches, so its success is evidence about every cell in you, not only about skin.

What determines which cell type an unspecialised embryonic cell becomes?

  1. The genes it happened to inherit
  2. Random chance, with no external influence
  3. Chemical signals from the cells around it — that is, where it is
  4. A master plan stored in the nucleus

Position decides fate. Move a cell early in development and it becomes what its new neighbours make it, which is how a body is built with no plan written down anywhere.

Early in development, a cell that would have formed skin is moved to the part of the embryo that forms gut lining. What does it become?

  1. Gut lining, because it now receives the signals its new neighbours are sending
  2. Skin, because its fate was already fixed when it formed
  3. Nothing, because it cannot differentiate away from its original position
  4. A mixture of the two, since it carries instructions from both regions

This transplant is the experiment behind the claim that fate is positional. It also explains how a body is built without a plan: each cell only has to answer the cells touching it.

What is a housekeeping gene?

  1. A gene that is switched off in every differentiated cell
  2. A gene needed by every cell whatever its specialisation, so it stays switched on everywhere
  3. A gene that repairs other genes
  4. A gene only found in stem cells

Respiration enzymes, cytoskeleton proteins and the protein-making machinery are needed by every living cell. Differentiation switches off the genes a cell has no use for, never these.

Which of these genes would you expect to find switched on in a neuron, a skin cell and a red blood cell as it is being made?

  1. The haemoglobin gene
  2. The insulin gene
  3. The genes Yamanaka switched on to make iPS cells
  4. A gene for one of the enzymes of respiration

Housekeeping genes are recognised by what they are for, not by what they are called: if a cell cannot stay alive without the protein, the gene stays on. Specialised genes are the ones that vary from row to row on the switchboard.

Where this goes next

That completes the first half of this unit. You can now account for the whole top row of Figure 4.1: one cell divides by mitosis into 37 trillion identical copies, those copies differentiate into 200 types by expressing different genes, and a control system decides which of them may divide and when.

What none of it explains is the bottom arrow. Every cell in that story is diploid — 46 chromosomes, copied exactly. To close the loop and make a new individual, your body has to produce cells with 23, and it has to produce them in a way that does not simply copy you. That requires a completely different kind of division, and Chapter 4.5 is about how it works.
