Unit 4 — Reproduction

4.8 Loops That Keep You Steady

Chapter 4.7 built two organ systems and left them with no way of deciding when to do anything. This chapter supplies the missing half: the hormone loops that time everything — sperm production, ovulation, the lining of the uterus, and how tall you grow. It also covers the other thing this standard asks for, which is how you would design an investigation to show a loop is real.

The shape of a feedback loop

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a steady internal environment — body temperature, blood glucose, water balance, hormone levels — despite everything changing outside. It is not achieved by anything being naturally stable. It is achieved by correction, constantly, and the mechanism that does the correcting is negative feedback.

Every negative feedback loop has the same five parts, and once you can name them you can take any loop apart.

Take the promise made in Chapter 4.7 and cash it in, because the scrotum is the simplest loop in this unit and it has no hormones in it at all.

Two completely different systems, one shape. That is the point of learning the shape rather than the examples.

A test you can apply to any loop: follow the arrows all the way round and count the minus signs. An odd number means the loop opposes change — negative feedback, and the level is held steady. An even number (including zero) means the loop amplifies change — positive feedback, and the level runs away.

Almost every loop in your body is negative feedback, because staying alive means staying within limits. The few positive feedback loops are the ones with a definite end point in view, and you will meet two of them in this chapter.

Follow the loop, then break it

The reproductive systems in Chapter 4.7 are run by a loop between three organs: the hypothalamus in the brain, the pituitary gland just below it, and the gonad. It is called the HPG axis, and the chain runs like this:

  1. The hypothalamus releases GnRH — gonadotrophin-releasing hormone.
  2. GnRH tells the pituitary to release two hormones into the blood: FSH and LH.
  3. FSH and LH travel to the gonad and switch it on — making gametes, and making sex hormones.
  4. Those sex hormones travel back to the hypothalamus and pituitary and switch them down. That is the negative feedback, and it is what makes the whole thing a loop rather than a chain.

Three real clinical and sporting scenarios. Each one breaks the loop somewhere different. Work out what happens from the arrows in Figure 4.31 before you open the answer — write down a direction (up, down or unchanged) for every hormone named.

Anabolic steroids. Testosterone-like molecules are taken from outside the body, so the blood is full of them. Predict what happens to GnRH, to LH, and to sperm production. Most people get the last one wrong.

GnRH falls, LH falls, and sperm production falls too.

Follow the two dashed minus arrows back from testosterone. The hypothalamus and the pituitary cannot tell a steroid from the real thing, so they read the blood, conclude there is far too much testosterone, and shut down. And sperm production sits at the end of the chain that starts with LH — so when the pituitary goes quiet, the testis is never switched on.

More testosterone in the blood, less sperm. The full version is worth reading below, because it comes back at the end of this chapter.

Damaged testes. The testes can no longer respond, so testosterone falls. Predict what happens to LH. Then explain why a doctor who measures both testosterone and LH can tell whether the problem is in the gonad or in the pituitary, when measuring testosterone alone cannot.

LH rises. With testosterone low, the negative feedback signal disappears — and the hypothalamus and pituitary, hearing nothing coming back, call louder. GnRH and LH both climb.

That is exactly what makes the pair diagnostic. Low testosterone with high LH means the fault is in the testis: the pituitary is plainly still sending the signal, and nothing is answering. Low testosterone with low LH means the opposite — the signal never arrived, so the fault lies above the gonad, in the pituitary or hypothalamus.

Testosterone on its own reads identically in both cases. It is the second measurement that separates them, and the only reason it can is that the loop runs in both directions.

Cut the feedback link. Every organ is healthy and every hormone can still be made. The only change is that the two dashed arrows are gone — testosterone no longer reports back. Describe what happens to the whole axis, and use it to answer what this standard actually asks: what is your evidence that a feedback mechanism maintains homeostasis?

With nothing reporting that testosterone has arrived, GnRH never comes down. LH never comes down. Testosterone climbs, and goes on climbing.

That contrast is the evidence. Nothing was removed except the return arrows: the same hypothalamus, the same pituitary, the same testis, all working. Yet the level that used to hold steady now runs away. So the steadiness was never a property of the organs being present — it was produced by the information travelling back. A feedback mechanism is the thing doing the maintaining, and cutting it is what proves it.

This is also why the argument has to be built from a comparison. A system that simply looks stable is not evidence of anything; a system that stops being stable when you remove one specific arrow is.

A fourth case — what happens when the LH surge fails — needs the ovarian cycle before it makes sense, so it waits for the section below.

The steroid result is worth stating in full, because it is counter-intuitive and it comes back at the end of this chapter.

Anabolic steroids are testosterone-like molecules. The hypothalamus and pituitary cannot tell them apart from the real thing, so they read the blood, conclude there is far too much testosterone, and shut GnRH and LH down. But sperm production needs the testis to be switched on — it needs FSH, and it needs testosterone at the very high concentration found inside the testis, which only LH can produce. With the pituitary silenced, the testis stops working. Sperm production falls and the testes shrink.

More testosterone in the blood, less sperm. Nothing malfunctioned: the feedback loop did precisely what it is built to do.

What starts it, and what stops it

The HPG axis is present from birth, and for the first decade or so of life it is almost silent. The hypothalamus releases very little GnRH, the pituitary releases very little FSH and LH, and the gonads sit essentially switched off — which is why children do not make gametes.

Puberty is the hypothalamus turning the axis on. It begins releasing GnRH in pulses, the pituitary responds with FSH and LH, and the gonads start working: making gametes, and making sex hormones in quantity for the first time. Everything people describe as the changes of puberty is downstream of that one switch, because sex hormones act on tissues all over the body, not only on the reproductive organs.

The two systems then behave very differently for the rest of life, and the difference traces straight back to Chapter 4.7.

  • Sperm production, once started, simply continues. The axis settles into a steady state and holds testosterone roughly level for decades, because the stem cells that feed spermatogenesis keep dividing. There is no fixed stock to run out of.
  • Egg release has an end. The stock of primary oocytes was finite from before birth, and it declines throughout life. When too few remain to respond to FSH, the ovary stops producing much oestrogen, cycles become irregular and then stop. That is menopause.

Menopause is worth one more sentence, because the hormone pattern is a direct prediction of the model you built. With the ovary no longer responding, oestrogen falls — so the negative feedback signal disappears, and the pituitary responds by raising FSH and LH. Persistently high FSH is exactly what a blood test for menopause looks for.

Notice that this is the same reasoning as the damaged-testis case in the interactive: a gonad that cannot respond produces a low sex hormone and a high pituitary hormone at the same time. One model, two situations, same prediction.

The ovarian and menstrual cycles

The same HPG axis runs in everyone. In people who ovulate it does something extra: instead of holding a hormone steady, it runs a cycle, about 28 days long, and it runs two linked loops at once.

  • The ovarian cycle is what happens in the ovary — a follicle matures, an egg is released, and what remains becomes the corpus luteum.
  • The menstrual cycle is what happens in the uterus — the lining is rebuilt, maintained, and then either kept or shed.

They are driven by the same four hormones, which is why they stay in step.

Follow one cycle through, in four movements.

Days 1–5: the lining is shed

Progesterone has collapsed at the end of the previous cycle, so the endometrium — which progesterone was maintaining — breaks down and is shed. That is a period. With oestrogen and progesterone both low, nothing is suppressing the pituitary, so FSH begins to rise.

Days 5–13: a follicle grows and the lining is rebuilt

FSH stimulates several follicles in the ovary; one takes the lead. The growing follicle releases oestrogen, and oestrogen does two things at once: it rebuilds the endometrium, and it suppresses FSH. That suppression is why the other follicles stop developing — the leading follicle is, in effect, switching off the signal its rivals depend on. It is negative feedback doing something useful and specific: making sure one egg is released rather than several.

Day 14: ovulation, and a sign change

Once oestrogen passes a threshold, the response of the pituitary flips. High oestrogen stops suppressing LH and starts driving it, and the result is the LH surge — the tall narrow spike in Figure 4.32. That surge triggers ovulation: the follicle ruptures and releases the egg.

That sign change is a genuine switch from negative to positive feedback, and it is the clearest example of positive feedback in the body after childbirth and blood clotting. More oestrogen causes more LH, which causes more oestrogen — the loop amplifies itself until something ends it. What ends it is ovulation itself: the follicle bursts, and the thing producing the oestrogen is no longer a follicle.

That is the signature of positive feedback everywhere. It is unstable by design, and it is used when the body wants to commit to an event and finish it, rather than to hold a level steady.

Days 14–28: the lining is maintained, or not

What is left of the follicle becomes the corpus luteum, which releases progesterone. Progesterone maintains the thickened endometrium and suppresses both FSH and LH, so no new follicle starts while a possible pregnancy is being waited for.

Then everything depends on one thing.

  • If no embryo implants, the corpus luteum breaks down after about ten days. Progesterone collapses, the lining it was maintaining breaks down, and the cycle starts again at day 1.
  • If an embryo implants, it releases a hormone called hCG — human chorionic gonadotrophin — which keeps the corpus luteum alive. Progesterone stays high, the lining is not shed, and no new cycle begins.

Two everyday facts fall straight out of that last pair of bullets. A missed period is a consequence of the corpus luteum not breaking down. And a pregnancy test detects hCG — the very hormone that prevented it — which is why the test works so early and why it is looking for a hormone made by the embryo rather than by the parent.

The same shape somewhere else: growth hormone

Nothing in this chapter is specific to reproduction. The HPG axis is one instance of a pattern the body uses everywhere, and the fastest way to see that is to look at a completely different axis and find the same diagram.

The chain is: hypothalamus → GHRH → pituitary → growth hormone → liver → IGF-1 → bone, muscle and fat cells. And, exactly as before, the last hormone in the chain feeds back to switch the first ones down: IGF-1 suppresses GHRH.

The anchor guide asks you to add two more hormones to that diagram, and both are worth understanding rather than just placing:

  • Ghrelin is released by an empty stomach. It stimulates growth hormone release. This is one reason growth hormone peaks during the night and during fasting.
  • Somatostatin is released by the hypothalamus and does the opposite of GHRH: it tells the pituitary to stop releasing growth hormone. It is a second brake, and IGF-1 increases it.

  3. Run "liver disease — IGF-1 cannot be made". Growth hormone goes up and growth goes down. Explain how a hormone level can be high while the effect it should produce is absent.
  4. Run "somatostatin blocked". Notice that the axis does not run away completely. Which arrow is still holding it, and what does that tell you about how systems that matter are built?

This is the point of including a second axis at all. Your standard, HS-LS1-2, asks you to develop and use a model of interacting systems — and a model is only worth having if it transfers. Planning an investigation

HS-LS1-3 has two halves, and everything above has only been the first. The standard reads: plan and conduct an investigation to provide evidence that feedback mechanisms maintain homeostasis. Explaining a loop is not evidence. Designing something that could have come out otherwise is.

So here is the design problem, which is also the one on the last pages of your anchor guide.

Anabolic steroids are synthetic versions of testosterone, sometimes taken without medical advice to increase muscle mass. As you established earlier in this chapter, they suppress the HPG axis, and sperm production falls with it. Once someone stops taking them, sperm production usually recovers — but how long recovery takes seems to vary, and longer periods of use may need longer recovery.

The scientific question: how does the duration of steroid use affect the recovery time of sperm count?

Volunteers are recruited who are currently using steroids and want to stop. They are grouped by how long they have been using: short-term, under 6 months; moderate-term, 6 months to 1 year; long-term, over 1 year.

Work through the design in the order below. This is the shape of every investigation plan you will be asked for.

ElementWhat it meansFor this investigation
HypothesisA testable prediction, phrased so that data could contradict it. "If… then… because…" is a reliable format.If the duration of steroid use is longer, then the time taken for sperm count to return to normal will be longer, because the HPG axis has been suppressed for longer and takes longer to restart.
Independent variableThe one thing you change or select for.Duration of previous steroid use — the three groups.
Dependent variableThe thing you measure, which may respond.Time taken for sperm count to return to a normal range. (Measured how? Sperm count per millilitre, at fixed intervals, by the same method every time.)
Controlled variablesEverything else that could plausibly affect the result, held the same across groups.Age range; general health; the specific steroid used and its dose; whether any other treatment is given; the laboratory and method used to count sperm; the time of day samples are taken; how "normal range" is defined.

Without them you cannot tell recovery from normal fluctuation.
Sample and repetitionEnough subjects that one unusual individual cannot decide the result.Many volunteers per group, not one. Individual biology varies enormously, so a single person's recovery tells you almost nothing.
Data tableDrawn before collecting anything, with the independent variable on the left and units in the headings.Rows: each participant, grouped. Columns: sperm count (million per ml) at 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 18 and 24 months. A final column for time to recovery.

"Controlled variables" and "control group" are not the same thing, and confusing them is the most common error in this kind of question.

Controlled variables are the conditions you keep identical across every group — age, method, dose. The control group is a group of subjects who do not get the treatment, there so you have something to compare against. An investigation can have many controlled variables and still be worthless if it has no control group.

Two limitations are worth naming, because recognising them is part of the skill.

First, the volunteers selected themselves into their groups — nobody was assigned to use steroids for two years. So the groups may differ in other ways too: someone who has used steroids for years may also differ in age, dose or general health. That weakens any claim that duration caused the difference in recovery.

Second, and more obviously: you could not ethically run this the other way round. Deliberately giving people steroids to see what happens to their fertility is not an experiment anyone may perform. A great deal of human physiology has to be studied through natural experiments like this one, and the design has to be honest about what that costs.

Check your understanding

What does "negative" mean in negative feedback?

  1. The effect is harmful
  2. The response acts in the opposite direction to the change
  3. The level always falls
  4. The hormone concentration becomes very low

Negative describes the direction of the correction, not its value or its usefulness. Too hot triggers cooling; too much hormone triggers less of it. The result is a level held near its set point.

The testes get too cold, and muscle in the scrotum wall pulls them closer to the body until they warm up. Why does this count as negative feedback?

  1. Because getting too cold is harmful to sperm production
  2. Because the temperature is pushed downwards towards a lower value
  3. Because the response — warming — acts in the opposite direction to the change that triggered it
  4. Because no hormones are involved anywhere in the loop

The sign of a loop is read off the return arrow, not off the example. Follow the arrows round and count the minus signs: an odd number means the loop opposes change, whether the parts are nerves or hormones and whether the stimulus is dangerous or trivial.

Someone takes anabolic steroids for a year. What happens to their LH and their sperm production?

  1. LH rises and sperm production rises
  2. LH rises and sperm production falls
  3. LH falls and sperm production rises
  4. LH falls and sperm production falls

The pituitary reads high testosterone in the blood and shuts down, so LH falls. Sperm production depends on the testis being switched on by LH and FSH, so it falls too. More testosterone in the blood, less sperm.

A tumour damages the pituitary so that it releases almost no LH or FSH. Every other organ in the axis is healthy. What happens to testosterone and to sperm production?

  1. Both fall
  2. Testosterone falls, but sperm production carries on unaffected
  3. Testosterone rises, because the testis is no longer being suppressed
  4. Testosterone falls, and GnRH falls with it

Anything downstream of a broken link goes quiet, and sperm production is downstream of LH and FSH even though it is not a hormone. Anything upstream does the opposite: with the returning signal gone, the hypothalamus pushes harder.

A patient has low testosterone and high LH. Where is the problem?

  1. In the testis — the pituitary is clearly still signalling, and nothing is answering
  2. In the pituitary — it is producing too much LH
  3. In the hypothalamus — it is producing too little GnRH
  4. There is no problem; this is normal

High LH means the pituitary is trying, and it is trying hard precisely because the negative feedback from testosterone has disappeared. Low testosterone with low LH would point the other way — the signal never arrived.

A different patient has low testosterone and low LH. Where is the problem?

  1. In the testis — it has stopped responding to LH
  2. Nowhere — low testosterone always brings LH down with it
  3. In the body tissues, which have stopped responding to testosterone
  4. Above the gonad — in the pituitary or hypothalamus, because the signal never arrived

One hormone measured alone cannot locate a fault in a loop, because the same low testosterone appears whichever end has failed. The pair separates them: sent and unanswered reads high, never sent reads low.

What triggers ovulation?

  1. A fall in progesterone
  2. A rise in FSH at the start of the cycle
  3. A surge of LH, driven by high oestrogen through positive feedback
  4. The shedding of the endometrium

Once oestrogen passes a threshold the pituitary's response flips from negative to positive, and LH amplifies itself into a surge. The surge ruptures the follicle. Ovulation then ends the positive feedback, because the follicle is gone.

The LH surge builds by positive feedback, which amplifies itself. What stops it?

  1. Progesterone rises and suppresses LH
  2. Ovulation itself — the follicle ruptures, so the oestrogen driving the surge is no longer being made
  3. A rise in FSH switches LH off
  4. Oestrogen suppresses the pituitary, as it did earlier in the cycle

Positive feedback has no brake built into it, so something outside the loop has to end it. Here the loop destroys its own source: the event it was committing to is the thing that stops it, which is exactly why this arrangement is used for events rather than for holding a level steady.

Why does a period not happen if an embryo implants?

  1. Oestrogen stops being produced
  2. hCG from the embryo keeps the corpus luteum alive, so progesterone stays high and maintains the lining
  3. The uterus lining stops responding to hormones
  4. FSH rises and rebuilds the lining immediately

A period is what happens when progesterone collapses. hCG prevents the collapse by keeping the corpus luteum going — and a pregnancy test detects that same hCG.

No embryo implants, so the corpus luteum breaks down about ten days after ovulation. What follows?

  1. Oestrogen rebuilds the lining immediately, so no period occurs
  2. hCG holds the lining in place until the next cycle starts
  3. FSH falls, so no new follicle can begin to grow
  4. Progesterone collapses, the lining it was maintaining breaks down and is shed, and FSH begins to rise

The endometrium is maintained rather than permanent — it lasts only as long as progesterone is being supplied to it. The same collapse lifts the suppression on the pituitary, which is why the lining is shed and the next follicle starts growing at the same moment.

In the steroid investigation, which of these is the control group?

  1. The short-term users
  2. Keeping the age range the same across groups
  3. Measuring every sample in the same laboratory
  4. Volunteers of the same age range who have never used steroids

The control group is a group of subjects who do not get the treatment. Options 2 and 3 are controlled variables — conditions held the same — which is a different idea entirely.

In the same steroid investigation, which of these is a controlled variable?

  1. How long each volunteer had been using steroids
  2. Taking every sperm sample at the same time of day
  3. Including volunteers who have never used steroids
  4. The time taken for sperm count to return to a normal range

A controlled variable is a condition kept identical everywhere, so that it cannot be blamed for any difference you find. Every plan needs all four roles named separately — one variable changed, one measured, the rest pinned down, and a group with no treatment to compare against.

Someone cannot make IGF-1 because of liver disease. What happens to their growth hormone level?

  1. It rises, because IGF-1 is the brake and the brake is gone
  2. It falls, because there is nothing for it to act on
  3. It stays the same, because growth hormone is not part of the loop
  4. It rises and then falls to zero

IGF-1 normally suppresses GHRH and raises somatostatin. Remove it and both brakes are released, so growth hormone rises — and the tissues still do not grow, because growth hormone does not act on bone and muscle directly.

Growth hormone is injected from outside, so the level in the blood is high. What happens to the body's own GHRH?

  1. It rises, because the hypothalamus matches whatever level it detects
  2. It is unchanged, because injected growth hormone never reaches the hypothalamus
  3. It falls — the liver releases more IGF-1, and IGF-1 suppresses GHRH
  4. It falls, and somatostatin falls with it

This is the anabolic steroid result in different clothing. Supplying the end of an axis from outside quietens the start of it, because the loop cannot tell where a hormone came from — only how much of it there is.
