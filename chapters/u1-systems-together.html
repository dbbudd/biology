Unit 1 — Biochemistry & Homeostasis

1.5 No System Works Alone

Chapter 1.4 ended with an explanation that could not stay inside the digestive system for a single sentence. This chapter makes that the point: the digestive system is physically incapable of finishing its own job, and so is every other system in your body.

You have absorbed a meal. In 1.3 you followed it through six compartments; in 1.4 you saw why the absorbing surface is folded and how water and salt cross it. At the end of that process, glucose, amino acids and fatty acids are inside the cells of a villus.

And that is as far as the digestive system can take them. A villus cell is a few micrometres across. Your big toe is a metre away.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to

  • Name the major organ systems and say what each contributes.
  • Cite specific interactions between the digestive system and at least five other systems, naming what crosses each boundary.
  • Explain why glucose and fat leave the small intestine by two entirely different routes, and what follows from that.
  • Distinguish egestion from excretion, and say which system does each.
  • Describe the gut–brain axis and give an example of the digestive system acting as a sense organ.

Eleven systems, one body

An organ system is a group of organs that co-operate on one large task. Human biology conventionally recognises eleven of them, and the number is a convention rather than a fact of nature — the division is something we impose to make the body thinkable, and the body does not always respect it.

SystemWhat it doesWhat it needs from others
DigestiveBreaks food down and absorbs itBlood to carry the products away; nerves and hormones to time it

Read the third column again. Every system in that table needs at least one other system to do its job, and most need several. This is not a nice observation about co-operation — it is a structural fact. There is no system in your body that could be lifted out and still work.

Two doors out of a villus

The clearest single illustration of system interaction in the whole of human biology is what happens at the end of absorption. Two nutrients from the same mouthful, absorbed by the same cell at the same moment, leave by completely different routes and arrive in different places.

Why does that difference exist? Because of size and solubility, decided at the membrane, exactly as Chapter 1.1 would predict. Glucose is small and water-soluble, so it dissolves in plasma and fits through a capillary wall. A chylomicron is a droplet of fat wrapped in protein, roughly a thousand times wider than a glucose molecule and not soluble in water at all. Capillary walls will not pass it; lacteals, which are leakier, will.

This has a practical consequence you may have met. Some medicines are destroyed by the liver almost immediately — the "first-pass effect" — so swallowing them is useless. Fat-soluble drugs that ride the lymphatic route partly avoid this, and drugs given under the tongue or as a patch avoid it entirely by never going near the gut. Anatomy that looks like a curiosity turns out to decide how a medicine is designed.

Egestion is not excretion

The urea route in that simulation makes a distinction that exams test relentlessly and students blur constantly.

EgestionExcretion
What leavesMaterial that was never absorbed — fibre, dead gut lining, bacteriaWaste produced by your own cells' chemistry — urea, carbon dioxide, excess salt and water
Was it ever inside your body?No. It passed through a tubeYes. It was made inside a cell
Which systemDigestiveUrinary (urea, salt, water), respiratory (carbon dioxide), skin (a little)
RouteRectum and anusKidney → ureter → bladder → urethra; or lungs

Both routes are in the model at the top of this chapter. Switch the digestive and urinary systems on together and look at where each one ends: the large intestine runs to the outside without its contents ever having been inside you, while the kidneys sit behind it, taking their load out of the blood. The two routes are not variations on one idea. They start in different places.

The test is simple: did it ever cross a cell membrane into your body? If not, getting rid of it is egestion. Faeces are mostly egested; the small amount of dead gut lining and bile pigment in them is genuinely excreted, which is why they are brown.

The gut is also a sense organ

So far the digestive system has been described as a tube that things happen to. That is a serious understatement, and the last twenty years of research have made it look increasingly silly.

Your gut wall contains a network of somewhere around 500 million nerve cells — more than your spinal cord — arranged in two layers running the whole length of the tube. It is called the enteric nervous system, and it can run peristalsis, secretion and local blood flow entirely on its own, with the brain disconnected. Biologists sometimes call it the second brain, which overstates it, but not by as much as you would expect.

It is also in constant conversation with the actual brain. The route is called the gut–brain axis.

Switch the nervous and digestive systems on together in the model at the top of this chapter and the thing worth noticing is the distance. The brain is a long way from the gut, and the traffic between them is mostly upward. The model can't show the vagus nerve itself, so Figure 1.21 above draws that connection instead. How it feels from the inside

You already have direct experience of this system, so it is worth naming the experiences.

  • Feeling full about twenty minutes after eating, not immediately. Hormone cells in the intestine release CCK and GLP-1 when fat and protein reach them. The delay is the signal travelling and the hormone accumulating. It is also why eating fast leads to eating more — you finish before the message arrives.
  • Butterflies before an exam. Stress signals travelling downwards change blood flow and movement in the gut. The sensation is real and the cause is in your head, in the most literal possible sense.
  • Nausea from a smell. A pathway from the brain to the vomiting centre, triggered by something that never entered your gut at all.

And the bacteria

Your large intestine holds a population of microorganisms roughly comparable in number to your own cells. Collectively they are your microbiome, and they are best thought of as a metabolic organ you were not born with and did not build.

  • They digest fibre that you have no enzyme for, releasing short-chain fatty acids that your colon cells then use as their main fuel.
  • They make vitamin K and several B vitamins that you cannot synthesise.
  • They occupy space and consume resources that harmful bacteria would otherwise use — which is part of why a course of antibiotics can be followed by a gut infection.
  • They produce chemicals that reach the nervous system, and animals raised entirely germ-free behave differently from ordinary ones.

Be careful with this topic, because it is heavily oversold. That gut bacteria affect health is beyond doubt. That a particular yoghurt will change your mood is not established, and much of what is sold on that basis runs far ahead of the evidence. This is a good place to practise the habit Chapter 1.7 is about: asking what the study actually measured, in whom, and compared with what.

Where this goes next

Look back at what the last three chapters have built. A tube, folded to give an enormous surface. Systems that carry things away as fast as they arrive. Signals running in both directions between the gut and the brain, reporting what has been eaten and how much.

That is a great deal of machinery, and it is fair to ask what it is all for. The answer is the subject of Chapter 1.6, and it is remarkably simple: staying in range. Your blood glucose has to sit near 5 mmol/L, your core temperature near 37 °C, your blood pH near 7.4 — not because anything wants them there, but because your enzymes stop working if they drift. Everything you have met so far exists to hold those numbers steady.

Check your understanding

Explain why glucose and fatty acids absorbed from the same mouthful, by the same cell, end up taking completely different routes to the rest of the body. What difference does it make?

The reason is size and solubility. Glucose and amino acids are small and dissolve in water, so they pass easily through the wall of the capillary inside the villus and travel in the plasma. Digested fat is rebuilt inside the epithelial cell into triglycerides and wrapped in protein as a chylomicron — a droplet roughly a thousand times wider than a glucose molecule and insoluble in water. It cannot get through a capillary wall, so it enters the lacteal, a lymph vessel with a leakier wall.

The consequence is the interesting part. Blood from the gut goes to the liver first, along the hepatic portal vein, so the liver inspects and regulates every gram of sugar and amino acid before the body receives it. Lymph does not go to the liver; it drains up the thoracic duct and joins the blood in a vein near the collarbone. So absorbed fat reaches your muscles and fat stores before the liver has had any say, while absorbed sugar never does.

Cite five specific interactions between the digestive system and other systems. For each one, name what actually crosses the boundary.

Any five of these, and the marks are in the second half of each sentence — naming what crosses is what turns a list into an explanation.

  • Circulatory: glucose and amino acids cross from the villus capillary into the blood, and are carried to the liver in the hepatic portal vein.
  • Lymphatic: chylomicrons cross into the lacteal and travel in lymph to the thoracic duct.
  • Respiratory: oxygen crosses from the alveoli into the blood, and is required before any of the absorbed glucose can be used; the carbon dioxide made in the process leaves the same way.
  • Urinary: urea, made in the liver from surplus amino acids, crosses from the blood into the kidney tubule and leaves as urine. The digestive system cannot remove anything it has already absorbed.
  • Endocrine: insulin crosses from the pancreas into the blood after a meal, and instructs liver and muscle cells to take glucose up; CCK and GLP-1 cross from gut hormone cells into the blood to signal fullness.
  • Nervous: signals cross from stretch and chemical receptors in the gut wall along the vagus nerve to the brain, and instructions cross back down to change movement and secretion.
  • Muscular: smooth muscle in the gut wall performs peristalsis; skeletal muscle movement is what pushes lymph along, since the lymphatic system has no pump.
  • Skeletal: calcium and phosphate absorbed in the small intestine cross into the blood and are deposited in bone — a process that itself requires vitamin D.
An old piece of advice says not to swim straight after a large meal. Using system interactions, explain what real physiology sits behind it — and be honest about how strong the effect is.

After a large meal the circulatory system diverts a substantial share of blood flow to the digestive system, by widening the arterioles supplying the gut. That blood is needed there: absorption depends on the capillaries carrying nutrients away fast enough to keep the concentration gradient alive, which you met in 1.3. During hard exercise the same circulatory system is asked to send a large share of its output to skeletal muscle instead. The two demands compete, because the heart's output is finite.

So the underlying interaction is real: digestive and muscular systems compete for a shared resource that the circulatory system has to allocate. The observable result is that hard exercise soon after a big meal tends to feel unpleasant — cramping, stitch, sluggishness — because neither system is getting what it asked for.

The honest part: there is no good evidence that this causes drowning, which is what the traditional advice claims. That is a useful reminder that a real mechanism does not automatically justify the conclusion someone has attached to it — which is the subject of the next two chapters.

Your pancreas belongs to the digestive system and to the endocrine system. What does that tell you about the eleven systems?

  1. That the pancreas is an unusual special case
  2. That the eleven systems are a mistake and should be abandoned
  3. That the division is a convention we impose on one body, not eleven separate machines sitting side by side
  4. That there are really two pancreases doing two different jobs

Figure 1.19 draws all eleven inside the same body outline, and that is the honest part of it. Every system in the table needs at least one other to do its job — there is no system that could be lifted out and still work.

Your diaphragm contracts to pull air in, and it is skeletal muscle. Which two systems does it belong to, and what does that show?

  1. Only the respiratory system — calling it muscular is loose talk
  2. Muscular and respiratory at once, which shows the eleven systems are overlapping descriptions of one body
  3. Neither — it has been counted twice by mistake
  4. Only the muscular system, since respiration happens in the lungs

The pancreas and the diaphragm are not awkward exceptions to be explained away — they are the rule showing through. The eleven systems are a convenience we impose to make the body thinkable, and the body was never built to respect them.

Glucose and fat are absorbed by the same villus cell at the same moment, and then leave by completely different routes. What decides that?

  1. Size and solubility — glucose dissolves in plasma and fits through a capillary wall; a chylomicron does neither
  2. The body sends the more useful nutrient by the faster route
  3. Fat is denser, so it sinks into the lacteal below the capillary
  4. They are absorbed by two different kinds of cell

A chylomicron is a droplet of fat wrapped in protein, roughly a thousand times wider than a glucose molecule and insoluble in water. Capillary walls will not pass it; lacteals, which are leakier, will. The decision is made at a membrane, exactly as Chapter 1.1 would predict.

A drug company designs a new medicine as a large, fat-soluble molecule. Which route will it take out of the small intestine?

  1. The blood capillary, because all absorbed material enters the blood
  2. Whichever route the body decides it needs most urgently
  3. Neither — a large molecule cannot be absorbed at all
  4. The lacteal, because it is too large and not water-soluble enough to cross a capillary wall

The same two properties decide it every time: size, and whether it dissolves in water. That is a membrane question, which is why Chapter 1.1 could have predicted this before you knew anything about villi.

Absorbed fat reaches your muscles and fat stores before the liver has seen any of it, while absorbed sugar never does. Why?

  1. Because lymph travels faster than blood
  2. Because the liver cannot process fat, only sugar
  3. Because the body chooses to send fat where it is needed most
  4. Because blood from the gut goes to the liver first along the hepatic portal vein, while lymph rejoins the blood near the heart

The liver inspects every gram of sugar and amino acid before the rest of the body receives it. Lymph bypasses that check. This has a real consequence: medicines destroyed by the liver on first pass are useless swallowed, which is why some are given under the tongue or as a patch instead.

Some swallowed medicines are largely destroyed before they reach the rest of the body, so they have to be given under the tongue or as a patch instead. Which route are those medicines taking, and what is the effect called?

  1. The lymph route, called the first-pass effect
  2. The blood route, and the drug is destroyed by stomach acid
  3. The blood route — the hepatic portal vein takes it to the liver first, which is called the first-pass effect
  4. Neither route matters; the drug is simply too weak

Everything absorbed into the blood from the gut goes to the liver before anywhere else. For a nutrient that is quality control; for a medicine it can mean the dose never arrives. Anatomy that looks like a curiosity decides how a drug is designed.

Which of these is excretion rather than egestion?

  1. Fibre leaving in the faeces
  2. Urea leaving in the urine
  3. Gut bacteria leaving in the faeces
  4. A meal being vomited back up

The test is simple: did it ever cross a cell membrane into your body? Urea was made inside your liver cells from surplus amino acids, so it is genuine waste from your own chemistry and the urinary system removes it. Everything else on this list only ever travelled through a tube.

Which of these is egestion rather than excretion?

  1. Dead gut lining and fibre leaving in the faeces
  2. Urea leaving in the urine
  3. Carbon dioxide leaving in your breath
  4. Excess salt leaving in the urine

The test is one question: was it ever actually inside your body? Fibre and dead gut lining passed through a tube and were never absorbed, so losing them is egestion. Urea, carbon dioxide and surplus salt were all handled by your cells, so losing them is excretion.

About nine tenths of the fibres in the vagus nerve run from the gut to the brain rather than the other way. What does that asymmetry suggest?

  1. That the brain has no control over the digestive system at all
  2. That the gut works entirely independently of the brain
  3. That the connection is mainly for reporting — the gut is telling the brain what is happening, more than the brain is issuing orders
  4. That signals from the brain to the gut are unimportant

Your gut wall holds around 500 million nerve cells — more than your spinal cord — and it is largely reporting upwards. Feeling full twenty minutes after eating is that system at work: hormone cells release CCK and GLP-1 when food reaches them, and the delay is the message travelling.

After a meal, hormone cells in your gut release CCK, which travels in the blood and makes you feel full twenty minutes later. Which description fits what is happening?

  1. The brain instructing the gut to stop digesting
  2. A fast electrical signal travelling up the vagus nerve
  3. The gut excreting a waste product that happens to affect mood
  4. The gut reporting upwards to the brain — a slower, hormonal channel alongside the fast vagus one

Three channels run upwards: the vagus nerve in milliseconds, hormones in the blood over minutes, and chemicals from gut bacteria over hours. The delay you feel after a large meal is the middle one arriving — which is also why eating quickly overshoots.

An advertisement claims a yoghurt "balances your gut bacteria and lifts your mood." How should you treat that claim?

  1. Sceptically — gut bacteria clearly affect health, but that does not establish what one product does to one person's mood
  2. Reject it outright, since gut bacteria have no effect on the body
  3. Accept it, since there is a real mechanism connecting gut and brain
  4. Accept it, since germ-free animals behave differently

Both halves are true at once: the gut–brain axis is real, and this topic is heavily oversold. The habit to build is the one Chapter 1.7 is about — ask what the study actually measured, in whom, and compared with what.

A headline reads: "Gut bacteria cause depression." It is based on a study showing that germ-free mice behave differently from ordinary mice. What is the strongest objection?

  1. That there is no known way gut bacteria could affect a brain
  2. That a behavioural difference in mice raised without any bacteria at all is a long way from depression in people
  3. That studies on animals can never tell us anything about humans
  4. That the study shows nothing at all and should be ignored

Notice that the objection is not "this is nonsense". A real mechanism exists and the result is real. What has gone wrong is the distance between what was measured — mice with no gut bacteria behaving oddly — and what was claimed. That gap is where most health headlines live.
