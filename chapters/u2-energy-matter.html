Unit 2 — Energy Flows & Matter Cycles

2.1 Energy, Matter and the Bonds Between

The unit overview left you with a sealed bottle in which atoms go round forever and energy passes straight through. This chapter is about why those two behave so differently — and about the one place where energy is actually kept: inside a chemical bond.

Two things that get muddled constantly

Matter is stuff. It has mass, it takes up space, and it is made of atoms. You can weigh it. A leaf, a lungful of air, a puddle, a wolf: all matter.

Energy is not stuff. It has no mass and takes up no space, and you cannot put a sample of it on a balance. Energy is a property — a number describing what a system is able to do. You never observe energy directly; you observe what it does. A hot pan, a fast car and a charged battery are all matter, and each has a lot of energy associated with it, but the energy is not a separate ingredient mixed in.

The confusion is completely understandable, because in everyday speech energy sounds like a substance. We say food "contains" energy, we "burn" it, we "run out" of it. Those phrases are not wrong exactly, but they are a shorthand, and this unit is where the shorthand starts to cost you marks.

"Plants get their food from the soil." Almost everyone believes this at some point, and it is wrong in an instructive way. A tree gains hundreds of kilograms over its life while the soil around it loses a few grams. Weigh the pot before and after and you will find the mass did not come out of it.

It came out of the air. Carbon dioxide is a gas, and gases weigh something — one cubic metre of air contains roughly 750 mg of CO₂, and a tree is quietly filtering enormous volumes of it. The confusion happens because we cannot see gas, so we do not count it as matter. Fixing that habit is most of the work of this unit.

Kinds of energy, and the only division that matters

Textbooks list many forms of energy — light, thermal, electrical, chemical, mechanical, sound, nuclear. The list is useful for labelling, but it hides the one distinction you actually need.

Kinetic energy is energy something has because it is moving. A running person, a moving car, vibrating molecules in a hot pan, light travelling from the sun. Thermal energy is really kinetic energy at molecular scale: hot means the molecules are moving fast.

Potential energy is energy something has because of where it is or how it is arranged. A diver on a high board, water held behind a dam, a stretched spring, a charged battery — and, crucially for us, a molecule whose atoms are arranged in a high-energy pattern.

Look hard at that third panel, because it is the whole idea. Nobody moved the molecule anywhere. Nothing fell. All that happened is that the atoms were rearranged — and rearranging them released energy. That means the energy was in the arrangement.

Before reading on, decide which of each pair has more potential energy, and say why.

  1. A diver standing on the ten-metre board, or the same diver in the water below.
  2. A phone at 100% charge, or the same phone at 5%.
  3. One molecule of glucose plus six of oxygen, or six molecules of carbon dioxide plus six of water containing the same atoms.

The third one is the same question as the first two. If that is not obvious yet, the next section is the one that makes it obvious.

Where the energy in a sugar molecule actually sits

Ask most people where the energy in food is and they will point at the food. Ask which part of the food, and the answer usually runs out. So here is the answer.

A chemical bond is not a stick. It is an arrangement of shared electrons between two atomic nuclei, and every such arrangement sits at a particular energy level. Some arrangements are stable and low-energy — the atoms are content where they are, and it takes a hard push to separate them. Others are less stable and higher-energy.

Bond energy is the amount of energy needed to break a particular bond apart. And here is the piece that trips almost everyone up:

Breaking a bond always takes energy in. Forming a bond always gives energy out.

Always, with no exceptions. A reaction releases energy overall when the bonds it makes are stronger — lower in energy, more stable — than the bonds it breaks. The energy released is the difference between the two, not the energy "stored in" any single bond.

So when we say glucose is a high-energy molecule, what we mean is precise: the C–C and C–H bonds that hold glucose together are relatively weak and high in energy, while the C=O bonds in carbon dioxide and the O–H bonds in water are exceptionally strong and low in energy. Take glucose and oxygen apart and put the same atoms back together as CO₂ and H₂O, and you end up in a much lower-energy arrangement. That drop is the energy a cell collects.

Two things follow from that diagram, and they are worth saying out loud.

First, photosynthesis and respiration are the same slope walked in opposite directions. That is not a coincidence or a neat teaching analogy; it is the reason the two processes fit together, and it is why the rest of this unit works.

Second, the energy was never "in the glucose" on its own. It is in the difference between glucose-plus-oxygen and carbon-dioxide-plus-water. Glucose in a jar with no oxygen has nothing to give. This is why the equation has oxygen on the left, and why a diagram of respiration that forgets the oxygen has lost the point.

Downhill and uphill: exergonic and endergonic

Reactions come in two kinds, and biologists have names for them.

  • An exergonic reaction releases energy overall. It runs downhill. Respiration and burning are exergonic. So are digestion and, in general, any reaction that takes big molecules apart — which is why chemists call those reactions catabolic.
  • An endergonic reaction absorbs energy overall. It runs uphill, and something has to supply the energy. Photosynthesis is endergonic. So is building a protein out of amino acids, or a starch grain out of glucose — anabolic reactions, from the same root as anabolic steroids.

Living things do endergonic things constantly. Growing, moving, thinking, pumping ions across a membrane against a gradient — all uphill. The trick every cell uses is to couple an uphill reaction to a downhill one, so the energy released by the downhill reaction pays for the uphill one.

The molecule that does that coupling is ATP. You met it in Unit 1 and it comes back properly in 2.5, but the idea is simple enough to state now: respiration is a downhill reaction that charges ATP; ATP then runs downhill itself to pay for whatever uphill job the cell needs done. It is a rechargeable battery, and the cell recycles the same molecules thousands of times a day.

Frying an egg. A raw egg white is clear and runny; a cooked one is white and solid, and it never goes back. What happened?

No new atoms arrived. The heat did not add anything. What it did was shake the long protein chains hard enough that the weak bonds holding each chain in its folded shape gave way, and the unfolded chains then tangled together into a solid mesh. That mesh is a lower-energy, more stable arrangement than the neatly folded one — which is exactly why the change is a one-way trip. Cooling the pan does not refold a protein any more than cooling a burnt log turns it back into wood.

This is denaturing, and it will matter again in 2.4 when you boil some leaf discs and find they no longer photosynthesise at all.

Both are conserved. Only one is reusable.

Two laws sit underneath everything in this unit, and students often learn them as a matched pair without noticing that they say quite different things.

Matter is conserved. Atoms are not created or destroyed in any chemical or biological process. Count the carbons on the left of an equation and you will find exactly the same number on the right, or the equation is wrong. This is why matter can cycle indefinitely: there is always precisely as much of it as there ever was.

Energy is conserved too — the first law of thermodynamics. It cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred or transformed. So the energy in your food does not disappear when you use it.

But then the second law adds something the first does not: every transfer or transformation degrades some energy into heat, and heat spreads out into the surroundings where nothing can gather it back up. The total is unchanged; the useful fraction always goes down.

That asymmetry is the entire reason the unit has the title it has.

Matter cycles because it is conserved and reusable — a carbon atom is exactly as good after being respired as it was before.

Energy flows because it is conserved but not reusable — every step degrades some of it to heat, and heat leaks away to space. So the supply has to be topped up continuously, forever, from outside. That top-up is sunlight.

Put the sealed bottle from the overview in a cupboard and it dies. Not because it lost any atoms, but because the flow stopped.

You can see the second law in ordinary things. A phone charger gets warm — that warmth is energy you paid for and did not get into the battery. A car turns maybe a quarter of the chemical energy in its petrol into motion; the rest is heat out of the radiator and the exhaust. A power station burning coal loses most of the energy up the cooling towers. Nothing is 100% efficient, and nothing ever will be.

Living things are no exception, and in 2.7 you will find out how expensive that turns out to be when you stack organism on top of organism.

Where this goes next

You now have the idea that light energy can be pushed into a molecular arrangement and stored there — that a sugar is, in a real sense, a charged battery. What you do not yet have is any idea how a plant manages it. Trapping light and using it to force carbon dioxide uphill into sugar is a genuinely difficult piece of chemistry, and it needs specialised equipment.

That equipment is a leaf. Chapter 2.2 takes one apart, layer by layer, and asks a question that turns out to have a good answer for every single layer: why is it built like that?

Quick check

Auto-marked. Get one wrong and it will point you back to the section that covers it.

A tree in a large pot gains 90 kg of wood over ten years. The soil in the pot loses about 50 g. Where did the 90 kg come from?

  1. Minerals taken up from the soil
  2. The water it was given
  3. Carbon dioxide taken from the air
  4. Sunlight, converted directly into wood

Gases weigh something. We just do not count them as matter because we cannot see them, and that habit is what makes this question hard. A tree is filtering enormous volumes of air and keeping the carbon.

A log weighing 4 kg is burnt completely and leaves 80 g of ash. Where did the rest of the mass go?

  1. It was converted into the heat and light of the flame
  2. It left as carbon dioxide and water vapour — gases that weigh something but cannot be seen
  3. Its atoms were destroyed by the heat of the fire
  4. It is all still in the ash, which is simply more tightly packed

The habit of not counting gases as matter costs you in both directions — a tree gaining mass out of thin air, a log losing mass into it. Weigh the gases and nothing has gone missing either time.

A hot frying pan and a sugar cube both "have energy". How do the two differ?

  1. The pan's is kinetic — fast-moving molecules. The sugar's is potential, held in the arrangement of its atoms
  2. Both are kinetic, because heat and chemical energy are the same thing
  3. Both are potential, because you can get energy out of either one
  4. The pan contains energy as a substance; the sugar only stores a record of it

Cool the pan and its energy is gone. Cool the sugar and nothing changes, because its energy is in where the atoms sit relative to one another. That is the only division of energy you really need.

Water held behind a dam and sunlight arriving on a leaf both "have energy". How do the two differ?

  1. Both are potential, because each one could be used to do a job later
  2. Both are kinetic, because the water will move the moment the gate opens
  3. The water's is kinetic, because it is under pressure; sunlight's is potential until it is absorbed
  4. The water's is potential — energy held by position. Sunlight's is kinetic — energy carried by something travelling

The long list of forms — light, thermal, electrical, chemical — is only a labelling exercise. Every one of them is either energy of motion or energy of arrangement, and that is the division worth carrying around.

A student writes: "Respiration releases energy by breaking the bonds in glucose." What is wrong with that sentence?

  1. Nothing — breaking bonds is exactly how energy is released
  2. The energy actually comes from the oxygen, which is the high-energy reactant
  3. The bonds in carbon dioxide and water are weaker, which is why energy comes out
  4. Breaking bonds takes energy in — the energy comes out when the stronger bonds of CO₂ and water form

A reaction releases energy overall when the bonds it makes are stronger than the bonds it breaks. The energy released is the difference between the two, which is why the answer is never "the energy stored in a bond".

Forming a bond gives energy out. Photosynthesis forms the bonds of a glucose molecule — so why does it need an energy input at all?

  1. The bonds it breaks in carbon dioxide and water are stronger than the ones it forms in glucose, so the reaction costs more than it repays — and light makes up the difference
  2. Forming the bonds of glucose takes energy in, because glucose is a high-energy molecule
  3. Light is only needed to get the reaction started, the way a flame starts paper burning
  4. The energy ends up locked inside the new bonds themselves, and light is what is put there

Photosynthesis and respiration are one slope walked in opposite directions. Going up costs exactly what coming down releases, and the only reason the climb is possible is that light keeps arriving to pay for it.

Building a protein from amino acids is endergonic — it runs uphill. How does a cell manage it?

  1. It waits until the cell is warm enough for the reaction to run on its own
  2. The enzyme supplies the missing energy
  3. It couples the uphill reaction to a downhill one — ATP running down pays for the protein going up
  4. The cell creates the extra energy it needs

Coupling is the trick behind everything living things do that looks impossible — growing, moving, pumping ions against a gradient. ATP is the molecule that does the coupling, and the cell recharges the same molecules thousands of times a day.

A cell breaks a starch grain down into glucose, and then uses some of that glucose to build a new protein. How are the two reactions classified?

  1. Both are anabolic, because both are jobs the cell is actively carrying out
  2. The first is catabolic and exergonic — running downhill; the second is anabolic and endergonic — running uphill
  3. The first is endergonic, because breaking the starch apart takes energy in
  4. Both are exergonic, since the cell ends up with energy out of the glucose either way

The two labels always arrive as a pair: reactions that take things apart run downhill, reactions that build run uphill. A cell spends its entire life using the first sort to pay for the second, with ATP passing the energy between them.

Matter and energy are both conserved. So why does an ecosystem need a permanent supply of sunlight, but no supply of atoms?

  1. Some energy is destroyed at every step and has to be replaced
  2. Living things can make atoms, but not energy
  3. Sunlight supplies the atoms that plants need
  4. Energy degrades to heat at every transfer and leaks away, while a carbon atom is exactly as good after being respired as before

Conserved and reusable means matter can cycle forever. Conserved but not reusable means energy has to be topped up from outside, forever. That asymmetry is the whole reason the unit is called what it is called.

A car turns roughly a quarter of the chemical energy in its petrol into motion. What becomes of the other three quarters?

  1. It is still there, degraded into heat and spread through the radiator and exhaust into surroundings that nothing can gather it back from
  2. It is destroyed by friction between the moving parts of the engine
  3. It stays in the petrol unburnt and is lost when the exhaust leaves
  4. It is stored in the hot engine and returned as useful energy when the car cools

The first law says the total never changes; the second says the useful fraction always falls. That is why no engine, no power station and no living thing has ever reached 100% efficiency, and why nothing ever will.

You charge a phone. No matter enters it and none leaves. Does that break the rule that matter is conserved?

  1. Yes — matter has to move for it to be conserved
  2. No — energy moved, not matter, and the atoms in the battery were simply rearranged
  3. No — electricity is a very light form of matter, so a little does enter
  4. Yes — the battery gains mass as it charges

This is the case that tests whether you have really separated the two ideas. Energy crossed the boundary; matter did not. Both laws hold, and they held independently of each other.

Filling in the organizer for a leaf in sunlight, a student lists carbon dioxide, water and sunlight together as the inputs. What needs correcting?

  1. Nothing — all three enter the leaf, and the organizer asks what goes in
  2. Water should come out, because a leaf makes its own water
  3. Sunlight should come out of the inputs — it has no mass, so it belongs under the energy transformation instead
  4. Carbon dioxide should come out, because a gas is not matter

The organizer earns its keep by holding the two questions apart: what matter went in and came out, and then separately what the energy did. Anything with no mass answers the second question, never the first.
