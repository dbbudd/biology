Unit 2 — Energy Flows & Matter Cycles

2.3 Two Halves of One Process

In 2.2 you found two compartments inside a chloroplast: stacked thylakoid membranes, and the fluid stroma around them. Two compartments, because photosynthesis is two processes. One catches light. The other builds sugar. Neither can run without the other, and this chapter is about what they hand across.

The equation, and everything it hides

Here is the equation you will be asked to write down.

That equation is correct, and as an explanation it is nearly worthless, because it answers no interesting question. Look at what it will not tell you:

  • The oxygen released — did it come from the CO₂ or from the water? The equation cannot say; both contain oxygen.
  • What is the light physically doing? "Providing energy" is not a mechanism.
  • How does a carbon atom get out of a gas molecule and into a ring of sugar?
  • Why do plants need light at all, if the sugar-building part turns out not to use light directly?

The rest of this chapter answers all four. The short version: the equation is a summary of the accounts at the end of the year, and we are about to look at the transactions.

The light reactions — what light actually does

The light reactions happen in the thylakoid membranes. Here is the chain of events, in order.

  1. A photon of light hits a chlorophyll molecule and is absorbed. The energy is transferred to one of the chlorophyll's electrons, which jumps to a higher energy level. This is the moment light energy becomes something a cell can use, and it takes about a millionth of a millionth of a second.
  2. That excited electron is grabbed by a neighbouring protein before it can fall back, and is passed down a chain of carriers embedded in the membrane, releasing energy at each step.
  3. That released energy is used to pump hydrogen ions across the thylakoid membrane, building up a concentration gradient inside the thylakoid.
  4. The ions flood back out through an enzyme called ATP synthase, and the flow drives it round like a turbine. Each turn makes ATP from ADP and phosphate.
  5. At the end of the chain, the electron is loaded — along with a hydrogen ion — onto a carrier molecule called NADP⁺, making NADPH.

Which leaves an obvious problem. The chlorophyll has just given away an electron. Give away enough electrons and it stops working. Where does the replacement come from?

Photolysis — and where the oxygen comes from

From water. A cluster of manganese atoms next to the chlorophyll splits water molecules apart to replace the lost electrons. The process is called photolysis — literally, splitting by light.

Written as an equation: 2 H₂O → 4 e⁻ + 4 H⁺ + O₂. Four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms in, four electrons, four hydrogen ions and one oxygen molecule out — balanced for atoms and for charge.

Every oxygen molecule you have ever breathed came out of a water molecule that was split inside a thylakoid.

Not out of carbon dioxide. The oxygen released by a plant is not the oxygen it took in as CO₂ — the CO₂ oxygen ends up in the sugar and in the water the Calvin cycle makes. This was settled in the 1930s and 40s by growing algae in water containing a heavy isotope of oxygen, ¹⁸O, and finding the heavy label came out in the O₂ gas.

Oxygen is a waste product. Photosynthesis does not exist to make it; the plant is simply throwing away the leftovers from getting at water's electrons. That the entire aerobic biosphere runs on that waste is one of the more remarkable accidents in the history of life.

If you want to see what the machinery really looks like, here it is — two photosystems, a cytochrome complex, a mobile carrier or two, and an ATP synthase, all sitting in the thylakoid membrane. You are not expected to learn the protein names. Look instead at the shape of the thing: electrons entering on the left from water, moving right along the membrane, and hydrogen ions being pushed into the space below and let back out through the turbine on the far right.

What gets handed across

The light reactions produce three things, and it is worth being precise about where each goes.

ProductWhat it isWhere it goes
ATPA charged energy carrierTo the Calvin cycle, a few nanometres away, in the stroma
NADPHA carrier loaded with hydrogen and electronsTo the Calvin cycle
O₂Waste from splitting waterOut of the chloroplast, out of the cell, out through the stomata

Two of those three never leave the chloroplast. This matters, because students often draw ATP travelling around the plant. It does not. ATP is made where it is spent — in this case, across a gap smaller than the wavelength of the light that made it.

And the traffic runs both ways. Once the Calvin cycle has spent an ATP it has an empty ADP and a loose phosphate; once it has unloaded an NADPH it has an empty NADP⁺. Both go straight back to the thylakoid to be recharged. A chloroplast holds only a small stock of these carriers and cycles the same ones continuously — the same recycling logic as the atoms in the sealed bottle, but running thousands of times a second.

"NADPH carries energy." Only loosely. What NADPH actually carries is hydrogen atoms — protons and electrons, and it is the electrons that are at a high energy level. That distinction matters here because the Calvin cycle has a specific chemical problem to solve: carbon dioxide contains no hydrogen at all, and glucose is half hydrogen. Every hydrogen atom in a sugar molecule was delivered by NADPH, and came originally out of a water molecule. So the hydrogen in your breakfast was, quite recently, part of some water.

The Calvin cycle — building carbon into sugar

The Calvin cycle happens in the stroma. It uses no light directly. It is sometimes called the "dark reactions", which is a bad name, because it does not run in the dark — it stops within minutes of the light going off, since the ATP and NADPH it lives on stop arriving. "Light-independent" is more accurate: independent of light, dependent on the light reactions.

It has three stages, and it is a cycle because the molecule it starts with is rebuilt at the end.

  1. Carbon fixation. An enzyme called rubisco attaches a CO₂ molecule to a five-carbon molecule called RuBP. The six-carbon product is unstable and immediately splits into two three-carbon molecules. This is the moment inorganic carbon becomes organic carbon — the single most important reaction in the biosphere, and it is happening in the leaves outside your window at a rate of about a hundred billion tonnes of carbon a year.
  2. Reduction. ATP and NADPH are spent on those three-carbon molecules, adding energy and hydrogen, turning them into G3P — a small sugar, and the actual product of photosynthesis.
  3. Regeneration. Most of the G3P is rearranged, using more ATP, back into RuBP so the cycle can go again. Only a little leaves.

Follow the carbon and the numbers become obvious

Count the carbon atoms through one set of three turns, taking in 3 CO₂:

WhereMoleculesCarbon atoms

Every row balances, because atoms are conserved. Three carbon atoms went in as gas; three carbon atoms left as sugar; the fifteen that were already in the cycle are still in the cycle.

Glucose has six carbons, so it takes two G3P to build one — and therefore two sets of turns, taking in 6 CO₂. Which is exactly the coefficient in the equation at the top of this chapter. The mechanism and the summary agree, as they must.

The loop, and one thing the equation gets slightly wrong

Set the two processes side by side and the symmetry is hard to miss.

PhotosynthesisCellular respiration
InCO₂, H₂O, lightGlucose, O₂
OutGlucose, O₂CO₂, H₂O, ATP
EnergyStores it — endergonicReleases it — exergonic
WhereChloroplastCytosol and mitochondrion
WhoPlants, algae, some bacteriaEverything, plants included

That last row is the one to underline. Plants respire. All day, all night, in every living cell. A plant is not a machine that only makes oxygen — it is a machine that makes oxygen faster than it uses it, whenever there is enough light. In the dark the balance reverses and a plant is a net consumer of oxygen, exactly like you. You will measure that reversal directly in 2.6.

The sim's totals show that the light reactions split 12 water molecules per glucose, not 6. Both figures are right, and here is why.

12 NADPH are needed, each carrying 2 electrons, so 24 electrons are needed, so 12 water molecules must be split. But the Calvin cycle then produces 6 water molecules of its own as it assembles the sugar. Net water consumed = 12 − 6 = 6.

The familiar equation reports the net figure. The fuller version — 6 CO₂ + 12 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ + 6 H₂O — reports the gross one, and it is the version that makes the isotope experiment come out right. Both balance. Check them.

Where this goes next

You can now say what photosynthesis needs: light, water, carbon dioxide, working enzymes, and a leaf built to deliver all four. Which immediately raises a testable question — if you take one of those away, or supply more of it, does the rate change?

Quick check

Auto-marked. Get one wrong and it will point you back to the section that covers it.

Sunlight is written on the left of the photosynthesis equation, but it is not counted when you check that the equation balances. Why not?

  1. Light is not matter — it has no atoms and no mass, so there is nothing to count
  2. Because light is used up completely and leaves nothing behind
  3. Because the equation is only an approximation
  4. Because light is a catalyst, and catalysts are never written into equations

The equation is an atom count, and energy has no atoms. This is the same distinction as 2.1: matter is what balances, energy is what drives. Notice how much the equation still refuses to tell you — where the oxygen came from, what the light physically did, how a carbon got into a ring.

A student counts the atoms in Figure 2.10, finds 18 oxygen on each side, then asks where the light has gone in that tally — after all, the light was used up. What should you tell them?

  1. The light left the tally as heat, which is why the counts still match
  2. A tally counts atoms, and light has none — its energy is consumed and ends up in the bonds of the sugar, where no atom count can show it
  3. The light is carried away inside the oxygen molecules, so it is already counted among the 18
  4. The counts match only roughly, so a small amount of light need not be accounted for

Matter and energy are accounted for separately. Atoms are conserved, so they can be counted; energy is transferred, so it has to be traced another way — which is what the rest of this chapter does.

Algae are grown in water made with the heavy oxygen isotope ¹⁸O, and given ordinary carbon dioxide. Where does the heavy label turn up?

  1. In the sugar, because the water's oxygen is built into glucose
  2. In both equally, since both reactants contain oxygen
  3. In the oxygen gas, because the O₂ comes from splitting water
  4. Nowhere — isotopes behave differently and are not taken up

Every oxygen molecule you have ever breathed came out of a water molecule split inside a thylakoid. The plant is not making oxygen on purpose; it is throwing away the leftovers from getting at water's electrons.

Run the isotope experiment the other way round: ordinary water, and carbon dioxide made with the heavy oxygen isotope ¹⁸O. Where does the heavy label turn up now?

  1. In the sugar, and in the water the Calvin cycle makes — carbon dioxide's oxygen goes into what the stroma builds
  2. In the oxygen gas, since every oxygen atom released came from a reactant
  3. In the oxygen gas and the sugar equally, since the label cannot be steered
  4. Nowhere — rubisco will not accept carbon dioxide carrying a heavy isotope

Each reactant's oxygen has a fixed destination: water's oxygen leaves as gas, carbon dioxide's oxygen stays in the organic products. Running the label in both directions pins down both halves of the answer.

A student draws ATP made in a leaf travelling down to the roots to power growth there. What is wrong with the drawing?

  1. Nothing — that is how a plant moves energy about
  2. Roots do not need ATP, so nothing needs to be sent
  3. ATP is too large a molecule to travel in phloem
  4. ATP is made where it is spent — here, a few nanometres away in the same chloroplast. Sugar is what travels

Two of the light reactions' three products never leave the chloroplast at all. ATP and NADPH cross a gap smaller than the wavelength of the light that made them, hand over their load, and go straight back to be recharged.

A student draws NADPH made in the thylakoids leaving the chloroplast to deliver hydrogen to the rest of the cell. What is wrong with the drawing?

  1. Nothing travels, because NADPH is destroyed the instant it is made
  2. NADPH carries only energy, so it has no hydrogen to deliver anywhere
  3. NADPH hands its hydrogen to the Calvin cycle a few nanometres away in the stroma, then goes back to the thylakoid as NADP⁺ — only sugar and oxygen leave the chloroplast
  4. NADPH is too large a molecule to cross the chloroplast's double membrane

A chloroplast keeps a small stock of carriers and runs the same ones round thousands of times a second. Carriers shuttle; products travel. Confusing the two is what puts ATP in the phloem.

The Calvin cycle uses no light directly. So why does it stop within minutes of the lights going out?

  1. Because it is switched off at night and restarts in the morning
  2. Because it runs on the ATP and NADPH the light reactions make, and those stop arriving
  3. Because rubisco needs light to work
  4. Because carbon dioxide stops entering the leaf in the dark

"Dark reactions" is a bad name for exactly this reason. "Light-independent" is better: independent of light, entirely dependent on the light reactions.

A lit leaf is plunged into darkness, and for a few seconds afterwards the Calvin cycle goes on fixing carbon. How is that possible, if it depends entirely on the light reactions?

  1. Rubisco stores light energy while the light is on and releases it in the dark
  2. The stroma absorbs a little light of its own, which keeps the cycle turning
  3. The carbon dioxide still dissolved in the leaf supplies the energy for those last turns
  4. ATP and NADPH already delivered to the stroma are still there to be spent, and the cycle runs on until that small stock is gone

Light-independent does not mean self-sufficient. The cycle runs on a supply line, and a supply line takes a moment to empty — which is why the stop is measured in seconds and minutes rather than happening the instant the light goes.

Three turns of the Calvin cycle fix 3 CO₂ and make 6 G3P — but only 1 G3P leaves. Why must the other 5 stay behind?

  1. They are rearranged back into the 3 RuBP the cycle started with — 15 carbons out, 15 carbons back
  2. They are stored as a reserve for the night
  3. They are waste and get respired away
  4. Only one in six G3P molecules is the right shape to leave

It is called a cycle because the molecule it starts with is rebuilt at the end, and rebuilding costs carbon. Count the atoms and the numbers stop being something to memorise: 3 in as gas, 3 out as sugar, 15 still going round.

Building one glucose molecule takes in 6 CO₂, not 3. Which count explains the 6?

  1. Six is the coefficient in the balanced equation, so the cycle has to match it
  2. One turn of the cycle fixes all six CO₂ together, so a single turn does it
  3. Three turns fix 3 CO₂ and release one G3P of three carbons; glucose has six carbons, so two G3P are needed — two sets of three turns, six CO₂
  4. Half the fixed carbon is respired away again, so twice as much must be taken in

The mechanism and the summary have to agree, and they do. The 6 in front of the CO₂ is not a convention someone chose — it is six carbon atoms arriving one at a time, three turns at a time.

A plant sits in a sealed jar of pure nitrogen with plenty of light and water. It makes no sugar at all. Why not?

  1. Without oxygen the plant cannot respire, so it cannot photosynthesise
  2. The nitrogen poisons the chlorophyll
  3. There is no carbon dioxide, so the Calvin cycle has no carbon to fix — and every carbon in a sugar comes from CO₂
  4. The light reactions cannot run without carbon dioxide

This is the routing sim as a thought experiment. Light and water feed the thylakoids; carbon dioxide feeds the stroma. Take away either supply and the whole chloroplast stops, but it stops in a different order and for a different reason each time.

A leaf in bright light with plenty of CO₂ has its water supply cut off. Which half of the chloroplast stalls first, and why?

  1. The Calvin cycle, because water is one of its reactants
  2. The light reactions, because photolysis has no water to split — no replacement electrons, so no ATP and no NADPH for the stroma
  3. Neither, because the Calvin cycle makes its own water and feeds it back to the thylakoids
  4. The light reactions, because chlorophyll cannot absorb light in a leaf that is short of water

Trace the two supply lines separately and the order of failure follows. Light and water feed the thylakoids; carbon dioxide feeds the stroma. Cut either line and the whole chloroplast stops, but it stops from a different end each time.

A student says: "Plants make oxygen and animals use it — that is the deal." What is the correction?

  1. Plants do not respire, because photosynthesis does that job for them
  2. Plants respire only at night, when photosynthesis has stopped
  3. Animals photosynthesise a little too, which balances the exchange
  4. Plants respire as well, in every cell, day and night — they only produce oxygen on balance because photosynthesis outruns it in the light

Look at the last row of the comparison table: photosynthesis is done by plants, algae and some bacteria; respiration is done by everything. In the dark a plant is a net consumer of oxygen, exactly like you — and you will measure that reversal in the aquarium in 2.6.

A healthy plant is sealed in a jar and left in complete darkness for several hours. What happens to the oxygen in the jar?

  1. It falls, because the plant goes on respiring and there is no photosynthesis running to replace what it uses
  2. It falls, because the plant switches over to respiration once the light goes
  3. It rises slowly, because the Calvin cycle carries on making sugar and oxygen in the dark
  4. It stays constant, because a plant neither makes nor uses oxygen in the dark

Photosynthesis and respiration run side by side in a lit plant, and only one of them stops in the dark. A sealed jar never measures either rate on its own — it measures the difference between them.
