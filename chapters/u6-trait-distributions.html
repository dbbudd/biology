Unit 6 — Evolution

6.3 Reading Trait Distributions

6.2 gave you the mechanism. This chapter is where you prove it happened — not by retelling the story, but by reading the numbers. Two of the four summatives in this unit are Investigating, and both of them look like this.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to

  • Define microevolution, and describe it as a change in the distribution of a trait or in allele frequency.
  • Analyse and interpret data to explain how a trait distribution changed over time.
  • Distinguish stabilizing, directional and disruptive selection from a graph.
  • Decide whether a dataset actually shows natural selection — and say what is missing when it does not.
  • Write a claim supported by evidence and reasoning.

This chapter is the reading that goes with the six data-analysis case studies in anchor guide 6.2 (pages 10–21) and the tuskless elephant study in anchor guide 6.3. Work through it alongside them rather than instead of them — the graphing and the written analysis are the assessed skills.

Where we have got to

6.1 established that populations have changed over time. 6.2 explained the process that changes them: variation, inheritance, differential survival and reproduction, adaptation.

Both of those are arguments made in words. A scientist would now ask an awkward question: can you show me?

That is this chapter. Everything here is about turning the story into numbers you can check — and, just as importantly, learning to spot when a set of numbers does not show what someone claims it shows.

From Unit 5 you have already worked with probability and the distribution of traits in a population — how a characteristic is spread across many individuals rather than being the same in all of them.

That is precisely the tool you need here. In this chapter you take two of those distributions, measured at different times, and work out what happened in between.

A word on why this chapter matters more than its length suggests. Two of the four summatives in this unit are assessed under Investigating, and both ask you to analyse data or design an investigation. The skills here are the ones being marked.

What change actually looks like in data

Microevolution is change in the heritable characteristics of a population over a small number of generations — evolution on a timescale you can measure rather than infer.

Because you cannot watch a population evolve directly, you measure it in one of two ways:

  • The distribution of a trait. Measure a characteristic — beak depth, path length, body mass — across many individuals, and plot how many fall at each value. Then measure again later and compare the shapes.
  • Allele frequency. Find what proportion of the population carries a particular allele, and see whether that proportion changes.

Both measurements are of the population, never of an individual. No finch's beak grew during the drought. What changed was which finches were still alive to breed, and therefore what the distribution of beak depths looked like in the next generation. If your written answer describes an individual changing, you have described the wrong thing.

The standard asks for statistics and probability

HS-LS4-3 says organisms with an advantageous heritable trait "tend to increase in proportion". Both of those words carry weight:

  • Proportion, not number. A population can shrink dramatically while the proportion carrying an advantageous allele rises. Reporting raw counts without proportions is one of the commonest ways to get this wrong.
  • Tend to. Selection is a statistical claim about averages, not a guarantee about individuals. A well-camouflaged individual can still be eaten. The pattern shows up across a population and across generations, which is exactly why you need data rather than an anecdote.

Three patterns of selection

Whatever the organism, a distribution under selection changes in one of three recognisable ways. Being able to name the pattern from a graph is a fast, reliable mark.

PatternWhat is selected againstWhat the graph doesExample
Stabilizing selection Both extremes Same centre, narrower peak Human birth mass — very small and very large babies both historically had lower survival
Directional selection One extreme The whole curve shifts Finch beak depth after a drought; antibiotic resistance
Disruptive selection The intermediate One peak becomes two Beak size where only very small and very large seeds are available

Interactive

Work through these in order. Reset between each one.

  1. No selection. Run 30 generations. Does the mean stay exactly still? Explain what makes it wobble — you met the answer in 6.4's terms, but you can describe it now.
  2. Only deep beaks can crack the seeds. Run until the mean stops moving. Record the mean every 5 generations. Why does it slow down and then stop, rather than rising forever?
  3. Both extremes do badly. Watch the spread rather than the mean. What happens to it, and what does that cost the population later?
  4. Only very small and very large seeds. Run 40 generations. What happens to the middle of the distribution — and what would happen next if the two groups stopped interbreeding?
  5. Set variation to none and try any pressure. Explain what you see using the four factors.

The mean and spread under the graph are your data. Reading a shift off them and writing what caused it is exactly the task in the six case studies below, and in the Investigating summatives.

A quick test that catches most errors: cover the labels and ask two questions of the graph — did the middle move? and did the spread get wider or narrower? Middle moved: directional. Middle stayed, narrower: stabilizing. Middle stayed, split in two: disruptive.

Disruptive selection is not "both extremes are selected for" as a separate goal — it is the intermediate being selected against. The distinction matters because it tells you what to look for in the environment: usually two distinct resources with nothing usable in between.

The six case studies

Each case study below gives you a real dataset. In every one you do the same thing: graph the data, identify the difference, give reasoning for it, and predict what happens next. That last step is the one that separates a description from an explanation. The anchor guide has the full task for each station.

Station 1 — Galápagos finch beak depth

The finches of the Galápagos have been studied to see how a change in environmental conditions affects the frequency of beak depth in a population. Medium ground finches (Geospiza fortis) were studied by measuring the beak depth of their offspring in 1976, before a major drought hit the island, and again in 1978, after the drought.

Finches on the Galápagos eat a variety of seeds. When there is a drought, the easy-to-crack, softer seeds are eaten first and only the larger, harder-shelled seeds are left as a food source. A drought is a lack of rain over a long period, and it can affect the entire ecosystem, so some individuals end up more successful than others.

The drought is the selection pressure. Only birds with deep enough beaks can crack what is left. Compare the 1976 and 1978 distributions and the pattern should be clear — and it should have a name from the section above.

The final question asks what you would expect years after the drought ends. Think carefully: does the mean stay where the drought pushed it? The answer depends on whether deep beaks are still advantageous once soft seeds return — which is the difference between a permanent change and a temporary excursion.

Station 2 — A cline in mummichog fish

Some variation in populations can be seen as a geographic cline — a graded change in a characteristic along a geographic axis. Some clines are produced by a gradation in an environmental variable, such as latitude. At higher latitudes, towards the north pole, the water is cold (about 6 °C). At lower latitudes, towards the equator, the water is warmer (about 21 °C).

In mummichog fish (Fundulus heteroclitus), the frequency of the Ldh-Bb allele changes in fish sampled from Maine down to Georgia. That allele codes for a form of the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase-B, which works in metabolism. This version of the enzyme functions better in cold water than the other versions do, and individuals carrying it can swim faster in cold water than individuals with other alleles.

Station 3 — E. coli and citrate

Twelve genetically identical populations of E. coli bacteria were grown for 35,000 generations in a low-glucose environment that also had citrate present. E. coli use glucose as an energy source but cannot use citrate. Up to 33,000 generations the bacteria could only metabolise glucose, so every 24 hours the population consistently produced an optical density of 0.03 (a value closer to zero indicates a low population density). After 33,000 generations the bacterial population that could be supported had an optical density of 0.25. Biochemical analysis showed that the strain now had the enzymes to metabolise citrate as well. This strain is referred to as Cit+.

For 33,000 generations the optical density sits flat at 0.03 — the population is limited by the scarce glucose. Then it jumps eightfold in a few hundred generations, because a strain has appeared that can also metabolise the citrate that was in the flask all along.

This is the strongest possible answer to "mutations are always harmful" and "you cannot observe evolution". Twelve identical populations were run in parallel; the innovation appeared in one. That tells you the mutation was rare and random — if the environment had caused it, all twelve would have developed it at once.

Station 4 — Foraging behaviour in fruit flies

The fruit fly (Drosophila melanogaster) gives us a way to study how foraging behaviour might have evolved depending on population size. A gene called forager (for) dictates the food-search behaviour of fruit fly larvae. On average, larvae with the forR ("Rover") allele travel nearly twice as far while feeding as larvae with the forS ("sitter") allele. Experiments have shown that the enzyme produced by the forR allele in larvae is more active than in forS larvae. Both alleles are naturally present in fruit fly populations.

So what circumstances might favour one allele over the other, making one of them more common? An experiment was set up to answer that. Two groups of flies were watched for many generations: one kept at low density and one at high density. (Population density is the number of individuals in a certain area.) Each population was given the same amount of food, and the distance the larvae moved to forage was measured.

Note the design: three replicate populations at each density, not one. The three low-density values cluster tightly (2.6–3.1) and the three high-density values cluster well above them (5.7–6.8), with no overlap. That separation is what lets you claim the difference is caused by density rather than by chance.

The trait here is a behaviour, and it is still heritable — the forager gene determines it, and the enzyme it produces differs between the two alleles. Behaviour evolves by natural selection exactly as anatomy does, which is a point the unit returns to with sexual selection in 6.4.

Station 5 — Guppy colouration and predation

The guppy (Poecilia reticulata) is a small freshwater fish, originally from north-west South America but now found all over the world. Male guppies come in a variety of colourings: some are a rather drab pale pink, others are brightly coloured with orange spots. One of the guppy's natural predators is the blue acara (Andinoacara pulcher), a small fish of the cichlid (pronounced "sick-lid") family.

In an aquarium experiment, male guppies of varying colouration — drab or brightly coloured — were presented to a cichlid. The background of the aquarium was tan with a gravel substrate. Researchers recorded which sort of guppy the cichlid attacked first, and how many of those attacks succeeded, recorded as "initial captures".

This one has a subtlety the others do not. Two quantities are measured — the percentage of attacks directed at each type, and the percentage of those attacks that succeeded (captures). Bright males are attacked more, which is a straightforward cost. But compare the two columns: bright males take 80% of the attacks and only 69% of the captures. Being attacked more often did not translate into being caught at the same rate, so something about being bright is also helping them escape.

Hold that result until 6.4. If bright colouration raises your chance of being attacked, why has it not been eliminated? The answer is that survival is not the only route to fitness — and that is the whole idea behind sexual selection.

Station 6 — Tuskless elephants

Poaching for ivory removes tusked elephants from the population. In heavily poached populations the proportion born without tusks has risen sharply. This is directional selection with a human selection pressure — and it is the case study the argumentation task is built on.

"Tusklessness is beneficial to elephants" needs care. It is beneficial while poaching continues. Tusks are used for digging for water, stripping bark and competing for mates, so tuskless elephants pay a cost in every other respect. A trait's value is always relative to the current environment, never absolute — and if poaching stopped, the selection pressure would reverse.

Is it actually natural selection?

The activity is called "Is it natural selection?" for a reason — the answer is not always yes, and knowing what would be missing is as valuable as recognising when it is.

For a dataset to demonstrate natural selection, you need all four of these. Check them explicitly:

RequirementWhat to look for in the data
Variation existedA range of values in the starting population, not a single value
The trait is heritableA named gene or allele, or offspring resembling parents. Not a trait acquired during life
Differential survival or reproductionA selection pressure, and evidence that it affected some variants more than others
Change across generationsMeasurements at two or more time points, or across populations under different pressures

Two cases that are not natural selection, worth being able to explain:

"I am pale in winter and more tanned in summer." Real variation, real environmental cause — but not heritable. Your children do not inherit your tan. This is an individual responding within its lifetime.

"Some animals shed their fur in summer." Also not selection. The ability to shed seasonally is an inherited adaptation that evolved by natural selection — but an individual shedding its coat this July is that adaptation being used, not evolution happening.

The distinction is the same one both times: a population changing across generations versus an individual changing within its life.

Writing the argument

Analysing the data earns you nothing until it is written down as an argument. The structure assessed here is claim, evidence, reasoning.

PartWhat it isWhere students lose marks
Claim A direct answer to the question, in one sentence Hedging, or restating the question instead of answering it
Evidence Specific data from the study — numbers, units, both time points "The graph went up." Which value, from what to what?
Reasoning Why that evidence supports that claim, using the biology Omitted entirely. This is the most common single failure

Reasoning is where the mechanism goes. It is not enough to say the proportion of tuskless elephants rose from X% to Y%; you must say why that follows from selection — poachers removed tusked individuals, tusklessness is heritable, so survivors passed the allele on and its frequency rose. Evidence without reasoning is a description. Reasoning is the explanation.

A test for your own writing: could a reader who has not seen the graph follow your argument from your words alone? If your evidence sentence contains no numbers, or your reasoning contains no biological mechanism, the answer is no.

Where this leaves us

The mechanism from 6.2 now has evidence behind it that you can check yourself. Finch beaks deepened in a drought. A cold-water allele grows commoner the further north you sample. Bacteria gained an ability none of their ancestors had. Fruit flies changed how far they travel to feed. Elephants are being born without tusks.

Different organisms, different timescales, different researchers — and every one of them fits the same four-step process.

Every change here was within one population: a trait shifting, an allele spreading. Real, measurable, but modest.

Yet the world is full of things that seem far too big for that — an eye, a wing, a mussel that lures a fish. And the tree in 6.1 does not just show populations changing; it shows them splitting, one lineage becoming two.

6.4 asks whether the small changes you have just measured are enough to account for both.

Check your understanding

Two parts. The quick check marks itself and points you back to anything shaky. The written questions after it are the harder practice, because explaining in your own words is what the summatives ask for.

Quick check

A population is measured twice. The mean is unchanged but the spread has clearly narrowed. Which pattern is this?

  1. Directional selection
  2. Disruptive selection
  3. Stabilizing selection
  4. No selection at all

Ask two questions of any distribution graph: did the middle move, and did the spread widen or narrow? Middle stayed, spread narrowed — both extremes were selected against.

In the beak-depth model the only seeds available are very small or very large, with nothing usable in between. After many generations, what should the distribution of beak depths look like?

  1. One peak in the same place, but narrower
  2. One peak, shifted towards deep beaks
  3. Unchanged, because the two seed sizes cancel each other out
  4. Two peaks, with a dip at intermediate beak depths

Disruptive selection is the intermediate being selected against, so in the environment you look for two distinct resources with nothing usable between them. If the two groups later stopped interbreeding, the split you can see in the graph is where a second species would begin.

Why does the standard say advantageous traits increase in proportion rather than in number?

  1. Because proportions are easier to measure
  2. Because a population can shrink while the share carrying the trait still rises
  3. Because the number of individuals never changes
  4. Because proportion and number mean the same thing here

A drought can halve a finch population while the share with deep beaks rises sharply. Reporting raw counts without proportions can make selection look like the opposite of what happened.

A drought cuts a finch population from 400 birds to 180. The number with deep beaks falls from 120 to 90. Has the drought favoured deep beaks?

  1. No — fewer deep-beaked birds are left, so the trait was selected against
  2. No — a population that is shrinking cannot be evolving
  3. Yes — the share with deep beaks rose from 30% to 50%
  4. Yes — the harder seeds caused the surviving birds' beaks to deepen

Divide before you compare. Any count taken from a shrinking population will fall, so counts alone can make selection look like the reverse of what happened — which is why the standard is written in terms of proportion.

Which of these is not evidence of natural selection?

  1. Bacteria in a flask evolving the ability to use a new food source
  2. Beak depth increasing in a finch population after a drought
  3. The share of tuskless elephants rising where poaching is heavy
  4. People who train developing bigger muscles over a year

The trait must be heritable. Muscle built by training is acquired during a life and is not passed on, so selection cannot act on it. There is also no data across generations and no differential reproduction.

Some animals shed their thick winter coat each summer. Why is one animal shedding its coat this July not natural selection happening?

  1. Because the ability to shed a coat is not inherited
  2. Because it is an inherited adaptation being used within one lifetime, not a population changing across generations
  3. Because shedding gives the animal no survival advantage
  4. Because the coat grows back each winter, so nothing has really changed

Separate the adaptation from its use. Selection built the seasonal moult over many generations; an individual moulting is that finished adaptation working, in the same way that a tan is an individual responding within its life rather than a population evolving.

Twelve identical E. coli populations were grown in the same conditions, and only one evolved the ability to use citrate. Why does that strengthen the conclusion?

  1. It shows the mutation was random rather than caused by the conditions
  2. It shows the experiment was poorly controlled
  3. It shows citrate is harmful to bacteria
  4. It shows evolution needs exactly 33,000 generations

All twelve had identical conditions. If the environment caused the useful mutation, all twelve should have acquired it. Only one did — so it arose by chance and was then selected because it happened to help.

The fruit fly study kept three separate populations at each density rather than one. What does that replication buy the conclusion?

  1. It lets the researchers supply more food overall
  2. The three low-density means (2.6–3.1 cm) sit well below the three high-density means (5.7–6.8 cm) with no overlap, so the difference is unlikely to be chance
  3. It proves that the forR allele makes larvae travel further than forS
  4. It guarantees every population began with the same allele frequencies

One population giving an unusual result tells you very little. Three that agree with each other, and disagree with three run under the opposite condition, is what lets you attribute the difference to the condition rather than to chance — the same logic as running twelve E. coli flasks instead of one.

A student writes: "The proportion of tuskless elephants rose from 18% to 51% over ten years. This shows natural selection." What is missing?

  1. The claim
  2. The evidence
  3. The reasoning
  4. Nothing — this is a complete answer

Reasoning is where the mechanism goes: poachers removed tusked elephants before they bred, tusklessness is heritable, so survivors passed the allele on and its frequency rose. Leaving it out is the most common single failure in these answers.

A student writes: "Beak depth increased because the drought left only hard seeds, birds with deeper beaks could crack them and survived to breed, and beak depth is inherited, so their chicks had deeper beaks too." What is missing?

  1. The evidence
  2. The claim
  3. The reasoning
  4. Nothing — this is a complete answer

Reasoning is the part most often dropped, but an answer can fail the other way. Name the measurement, the units and both time points — 1976 and 1978 mean beak depth here — so a reader who has never seen the graph can still check the claim.

Explain it yourself

Write your answer before opening the response. Comparing what you wrote with a full answer is where most of the learning happens.

A population of snails is measured twice, fifty years apart. The mean shell thickness is unchanged, but the range has narrowed markedly. Name the pattern and suggest what could cause it.

Stabilizing selection. The centre has not moved, so it is not directional; the distribution has one peak that got narrower, so it is not disruptive.

A plausible cause: both extremes are disadvantaged. Very thin shells give poor protection from crabs; very thick shells cost more calcium and energy to build and make the snail heavier and slower. Snails near the middle survive and reproduce best, so each generation the extremes are trimmed away.

Note that a narrowing range also means less variation — which leaves the population less able to respond if conditions change later.

In the E. coli study, twelve genetically identical populations were grown under identical conditions, but only one evolved the ability to use citrate. Why does that strengthen the conclusion rather than weaken it?

Because it shows the mutation was random rather than induced by the environment. All twelve populations experienced exactly the same citrate-rich, glucose-poor conditions. If the environment caused the useful mutation, all twelve should have acquired it — the fact that only one did shows the mutation arose by chance and then was selected because it happened to be useful.

The replication is also what makes it a controlled experiment. With a single population you could not tell an evolutionary innovation from contamination or an experimental error; with twelve you have eleven controls.

A student offers this as evidence of natural selection: "People who exercise develop bigger muscles, so over time humans are becoming more muscular." Identify every problem.

The trait is not heritable. Muscle developed by training is acquired during a life and is not passed to offspring. This is the Lamarckian error — inheritance of acquired characteristics.

There is no differential reproduction. No evidence is offered that more muscular people leave more offspring, which is what selection requires.

There is no data across generations. A claim about change over time needs measurements at two or more points, and none is given.

Individuals, not the population. The observation is about individuals changing within their lifetimes, which is not evolution.

What would be needed instead: a heritable genetic variant affecting muscularity, evidence that carriers leave more surviving offspring, and allele frequency measured in at least two generations.

Improve this answer: "The tuskless elephants increased because of poaching. This shows natural selection."

It has a claim but no real evidence and no reasoning.

Claim: Heavy poaching caused the proportion of tuskless African elephants to increase in the affected populations.

Evidence: cite the actual figures from the case study — the proportion of tuskless individuals before and after the poaching period, with the time span over which it changed.

Reasoning: tusklessness is a heritable trait already present at low frequency. Poachers targeted elephants with tusks, so tusked individuals were removed from the population before reproducing while tuskless individuals survived and bred. Because the trait is inherited, a larger share of the next generation was tuskless, and the proportion rose. The environmental change did not create tusklessness — it changed which existing variant survived to reproduce.
