Unit 1 — Biochemistry & Homeostasis

Unit 1 Overview

You eat food that was recently part of another organism, and somehow it becomes part of you. Meanwhile your temperature, your blood sugar and the water in your body barely change. This unit explains how both happen at once.

One sentence holds this unit together. It is worth writing on the inside of your folder:

Structure makes function possible → systems interact → the body stays in range.

Each chapter takes one step along that line. If an explanation stops making sense, work out which arrow you are on. Most questions in this unit ask you to move along one of them.

The case we keep coming back to

In 1971, in refugee camps on the border between India and what is now Bangladesh, cholera was killing about three people in every ten who caught it. Treating it properly needed a drip, a sterile needle and a trained nurse, and there were tens of thousands of patients. A doctor named Dilip Mahalanabis ran out of intravenous fluid and started mixing salt and sugar into clean water in dustbins, telling families to make the patients drink it. The death rate fell to about three in a hundred.

That mixture is now called oral rehydration salts. The World Health Organization's recipe has just four ingredients:

IngredientAmount per litreWhy it is there
Sodium chloride75 mmol/L sodiumReplaces the salt lost in the stool
Glucose75 mmol/LThis is the part that needs explaining
Potassium chloride20 mmol/LReplaces potassium lost from cells
Trisodium citrate10 mmol/LCorrects the acidity of the blood

This unit answers one question: why does the sugar have to be there? A litre of the mixture holds about 13.5 g of glucose. That is roughly three teaspoons, and far too little to matter as food for a sick child. Yet if you take the glucose out, the mixture stops working.

Before you start, write down your best answer to each question. Rough answers are fine. Come back after 1.8 and rewrite them.

  1. Where in the body does the water in a drink actually cross into the blood, and what does it cross?
  2. Why would adding a little sugar make water cross faster?
  3. A healthy adult puts about 9 litres of fluid into their own gut every day and loses about 0.1 litres of it. Where does the rest go?
  4. Why is losing water dangerous, when you could simply drink more?

By the end of the unit you will be able to answer all four. The key to the second is a membrane protein called SGLT1, which only carries sodium and glucose into a gut cell together. Water follows them. The medical journal The Lancet called oral rehydration therapy potentially the most important medical advance of the twentieth century. It is a sachet of salt and sugar, and it works because of one protein in one kind of cell.

How the three standards fit together

This unit is built on three NGSS performance expectations. Each one looks at the body at a different scale, and each builds on the one before. The highlighted words show the skill each standard asks you to use.

  • HS-LS1-2: How do the parts of a body fit together, and how does each structure make its job possible?
  • HS-LS1-3: How does a body hold itself steady, and how would you gather evidence that it does?
  • HS-LS1-6: How do the atoms in your food become the molecules you are made of?

Taken in order, they zoom in. HS-LS1-2 works at the scale of organs and systems: what a villus is for, why the gut is folded, and which system passes nutrients to which. HS-LS1-3 asks what all that machinery is for, and the answer is keeping the body in range. HS-LS1-6 goes down to atoms: what food is made of, and where each atom ends up.

Learning targets for the unit

These are the unit's "I can" statements, grouped by standard. Use them as a checklist. The number after each one is the chapter that teaches it. Practice marks a science and engineering practice, a skill scientists use. Crosscutting marks a crosscutting concept, an idea that applies across all of science.

Develop and use a model

  • I can describe the characteristics of a living thing.
  • I can describe the hierarchy of organization from atom to organism related to functions carried out in organisms.
  • I can explain how different components (tissues, muscles, organs) of the digestive system interact to achieve the digestion of food and absorption of nutrients.
  • I can cite various interactions between the digestive system and other body systems.
  • I can distinguish between the accuracy of a model and the actual body systems and functions it represents.
  • I can translate data from a graph into a table.
  • I can model energy and matter inputs/outputs in a living organism and describe the processes involved (metabolism).
  • I can model the digestive tract and describe the processes.
  • I can use a model (e.g., physical, mathematical, computer models) to simulate systems and interactions within and between systems at different scales.
  • Given new data and evidence, I can develop a model to illustrate the relationships between systems or components of a system.

Plan and conduct an investigation

  • I can explain homeostasis and how it ensures survival.
  • I can describe negative homeostatic feedback mechanisms using digestion as an example.
  • I can describe positive homeostatic feedback mechanisms using childbirth contractions, vomiting, sneezing, blood clotting as examples.
  • I can manipulate variables to alter the body mass of a human through diet and exercise.
  • I can plan and conduct an investigation to produce data to serve as the basis for evidence, and in the design: decide on types, how much, and accuracy of data needed to produce reliable measurements and consider limitations on the precision of the data (number of trials, time, data collection methods), and refine the design accordingly.
  • I can explain that feedback (negative or positive) can stabilize or destabilize a system.

Construct and revise an explanation

  • I can describe the basic structure and functions of carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids.
  • I can analyze the breakdown of glucose and the use of the individual atoms in creating other essential carbon based molecules.
  • I can construct and revise an explanation based on valid and reliable evidence obtained from a variety of sources (including students’ own investigations, models, theories, simulations, peer review).
  • I can describe changes of energy and matter in a system in terms of energy and matter flows into, out of, and within that system.

What you will actually do

Each chapter has an interactive where you practise the skill instead of reading about it. Most ask you to commit to an answer before they explain anything, because writing a prediction down makes you think it through.

  • 1.1 Move a cell between solutions and watch osmosis do it.
  • 1.2 Build the hierarchy from atom to organism, then find three places it breaks.
  • 1.3 Follow one sandwich through six compartments, deciding what happens where.
  • 1.4 Run the agar-cube lab in software, then apply the ratio to a gut and a cell.
  • 1.5 Trace five things across the body: sugar, fat, oxygen, waste and a signal.
  • 1.6 Build six feedback loops and run them, including three positive ones.
  • 1.7 Design an experiment and see the data it would really produce. Then model how diet and exercise change body mass.
  • 1.8 Follow the six carbon atoms of one glucose molecule into a fat and into an amino acid.
