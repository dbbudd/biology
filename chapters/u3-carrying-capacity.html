Unit 3 — Ecosystem Dynamics

3.2 Carrying Capacity and What Limits a Population

Chapter 3.1 ended with a proof: exponential growth cannot continue, because if it did, one bacterium would outweigh the Earth in under two days. Something stops it. This chapter is about what that something is, how it bends the J-curve into an S, and why on St Matthew Island it arrived far too late.

The ceiling has a name

The carrying capacity of an environment is the maximum number of individuals of a population it can support over the long term. It is written K.

Three things in that definition are worth arguing with, because students lose marks on all three.

  • "Of a population." K is not a property of a place on its own. The same island has one carrying capacity for reindeer and a completely different one for arctic foxes. Asking "what is the carrying capacity of this island?" is not a complete question.
  • "Over the long term." An island can hold 6,000 reindeer for a season, the way you can hold your breath for a minute. That does not make 6,000 the carrying capacity. K is the number it can support indefinitely, which means the resource must be able to regrow at least as fast as it is eaten.
  • "Maximum number." K is not fixed and it is not a law of nature. A wet year raises it; a fire lowers it; overgrazing can lower it permanently. K is a summary of the current conditions, and conditions change — which is what the second half of this unit is about.

Once there is a ceiling, growth cannot stay exponential. As the population climbs towards K, each individual finds slightly less food, slightly less space, slightly more competition. Birth rates fall, death rates rise, and the population's per-individual growth rate shrinks. When the population reaches K, births balance deaths and net growth is zero.

Growth of that shape is called logistic growth, and it makes an S-curve.

The most useful single feature of an S-curve is where it is steepest. Not at the start, when there are almost no breeders. Not at the end, when there is no food. Halfway up, at about K/2 — enough individuals to reproduce fast, still enough resource to go round.

That is not a curiosity. It is the reason fisheries are managed to hold stocks near half their unfished level rather than letting them recover to maximum: a population at K/2 replaces the most fish per year. Whether that is a wise thing to aim for is a different argument, and a good one.

An S-curve in the wild

Northern fur seals were hunted almost to nothing on the Pribilof Islands in the Bering Sea during the nineteenth century. In 1911 an international treaty stopped the killing of seals at sea, and the population was left largely alone. Biologists then counted breeding males on St Paul Island for the next several decades.

Compare this against the reindeer curve in Figure 3.1 and you have the two possible endings of the same story. Both start with a protected population and abundant resources. Both grow exponentially for years. One bends over and settles. The other does not.

The seals had something the reindeer did not: their food — fish and squid — moved, reproduced independently of the seals, and could not be eaten down to nothing in a single winter. A seal that cannot find a fish this week goes hungry and may not breed, and that feedback reaches the population while there are still fish. That is the whole difference, and the sandbox below lets you demonstrate it.

Overshoot, and the reindeer

If you worked through the sandbox you have already found the answer. Overshoot — a population growing past its carrying capacity — happens only when the population grows past that ceiling before the shortage registers — and that can only happen if there is a delay between the resource running short and the population responding.

Delays are everywhere in biology. A reindeer cow that conceives in October gives birth the following May: seven months during which the herd keeps growing on a decision made when there was still food. A population of long-lived adults keeps eating for years after it has stopped successfully raising young. Fat reserves let animals breed through one bad season on last season's food.

On St Matthew Island the delay was compounded by the food itself. The reindeer lived largely on lichen, which grows a few millimetres a year and takes decades to recover once grazed off. The herd was living on a bank balance that had been accumulating undisturbed since the last ice age, and it spent it in twenty years. When Klein returned in 1966 the lichen mat was gone, the winter of 1963–64 had been exceptionally severe, and the surviving animals were visibly starved.

Overshoot does not just cause a crash. It can lower K itself. The island after 1966 could support fewer reindeer than the island in 1944, because the lichen that was the carrying capacity had been eaten faster than it could regrow. This is the difference between using a resource and consuming the machinery that makes it.

Hold onto this. In Chapter 3.5 you will meet the formal version: changing conditions can produce a new ecosystem rather than a recovered one. St Matthew Island is the simplest example in the whole unit.

Two kinds of limiting factor

Every limiting factor falls into one of two categories, and telling them apart is one of the most heavily assessed skills in this unit. The test is a single question:

Does this factor hit harder when the population is crowded?

If yes, it is density-dependent. If it would do the same damage to ten individuals as to ten thousand, it is density-independent.

Density-dependent — mostly bioticDensity-independent — mostly abiotic
Predation. Predators find dense prey more easily and gather where prey is thick. Weather. A hard winter is just as cold for a sparse herd as a dense one.
Competition for resources — both intraspecific (between members of the same species) and interspecific (between different species). Natural disasters — typhoons, volcanic eruptions, fire, flood.
Accumulation of waste. More individuals, more waste, in the same space. Pollution — a chemical spill kills the same proportion regardless of numbers.
Disease and parasites. A pathogen needs to reach a new host, and crowding makes that easy. Habitat destruction by human activity.

"Biotic means density-dependent" is a useful rule of thumb and not a law. A living thing can act density-independently: a fungal spore blown in from outside kills whatever it lands on regardless of how many neighbours there are. And a non-living factor can act density-dependently — think about oxygen in a pond, where the number of fish determines how fast it runs out. Use the crowding test, not the biotic/abiotic label, and you will always be right.

The two kinds do different jobs. Density-dependent factors regulate. They act as a thermostat: too many individuals, the factor bites harder, numbers come down; too few, it eases off, numbers recover. That negative feedback is what produces a stable population and an S-curve at all.

Density-independent factors do not regulate — they just knock. A typhoon can halve a population, but it does not care whether the population was above or below K, and it will not push it back towards K next time. In many real populations, density-independent events keep numbers so low that density-dependent factors rarely become important at all.

Three datasets: classify before you read on

Here are three real studies. For each one, decide before reading the commentary whether the pattern is being driven by a density-dependent or a density-independent factor — and, harder, say what question the researchers were asking.

The horizontal axis is a measure of crowding, and reproduction falls steadily as crowding rises — from around five offspring per female at low density to about one at high density. The fitted line through those points has a slope of about −0.06 offspring per extra female. The researchers' question was: does the sparrows' own density control their reproduction? The answer is clearly yes, so this is density-dependent. Note what is being limited: not survival, but birth rate. Regulation does not have to work through death.

The same shape upside down: the more crowded the plot, the higher the proportion killed — from about a quarter at low density to nearly all of them at high density. Also density-dependent, this time acting through death rate rather than birth rate, and the mechanism is named in the paper's title: predators concentrate where prey is dense, and dense schools run out of places to hide.

This one is different, and the difference is the point of including it. There is textbook exponential growth from April to August — the J-curve from Chapter 3.1 — and then the population falls off a cliff within a few weeks. Nothing about the shape suggests a gentle approach to a ceiling.

A collapse that fast and that complete, at the same time every year, is the signature of a density-independent factor: the season ended. Cold and the death of the host plant's soft growth kill the aphids whether there are three thousand of them or thirty. Many small, short-lived organisms live like this permanently — booming, crashing, and never once approaching a carrying capacity.

Be careful with this graph, though, because it is the kind of question that separates a good answer from a lazy one. The crash is density-independent. But the timing of the peak, and how high it got before the season ended, will have been shaped by density-dependent things too — predators such as ladybirds, parasitic wasps, and the aphids' own crowding on the plant. Real populations are almost never controlled by one kind of factor alone. As your anchor guide puts it: population sizes are controlled by a complex balance of both.

Quick check

Six thousand reindeer once lived on St Matthew Island. Why is "the carrying capacity of the island is 6,000" still a wrong answer?

  1. The count of 6,000 was inaccurate
  2. K is the number that can be supported indefinitely, and the lichen was being eaten far faster than it regrew
  3. A population can never rise above its carrying capacity
  4. Carrying capacity is set by the island's area, which had not changed

An island can hold 6,000 reindeer the way you can hold your breath for a minute. "Over the long term" is the load-bearing phrase in the definition: the resource has to regrow at least as fast as it is consumed.

A shallow pond is stocked with 500 carp in spring. All 500 are alive in September; by March most are dead and the pond weed has been grazed down to the mud. A student writes that the pond's carrying capacity for carp is 500. What is wrong with that?

  1. The count should have been taken in March, once the die-off was over
  2. Carrying capacity applies to a whole pond, not to one species living in it
  3. The pond weed, not the carp, is the population with a carrying capacity here
  4. 500 is the number the pond fed for one season, not the number it can feed indefinitely — the weed was eaten faster than it grew back

The test for K is never how many individuals were once present. It is whether the resource can renew at the rate it is being used. A figure that lasts only until the resource runs out is a peak, not a ceiling.

Fisheries are often managed to hold a stock near half its unfished size rather than letting it recover to K. What is the reasoning?

  1. A population at K grows fastest, so half of K leaves a safety margin
  2. Fish grow to a bigger size when the stock is near K
  3. An S-curve is steepest around K/2, so that is where the stock replaces the most fish per year
  4. Half the stock has to be left for other predators

At K/2 there are enough adults to breed fast and still enough resource to go round. Whether aiming for that is wise is a separate and very good argument — it leaves almost no margin for a bad year.

A reserve manager wants a deer herd to produce as many surplus animals as possible each year for release elsewhere. At roughly what size should the herd be held?

  1. As close to K as the habitat allows
  2. At about half of K, where the curve is steepest and the herd replaces the most animals per year
  3. As small as the reserve can keep viable, so that each deer has the most food
  4. It makes no difference, because the herd grows at the same rate whatever its size

Maximum growth in total and maximum growth per individual sit at opposite ends of the curve, and the useful figure sits between them. Whether it is wise to hold anything permanently at half its natural level is a separate argument — it leaves very little margin for a bad year.

Fur seals levelled off at a ceiling; reindeer sailed past theirs and crashed. Both were protected and both had abundant food. What was different?

  1. The seals still had predators keeping them in check
  2. The seals grew too slowly ever to reach their carrying capacity
  3. The 1911 treaty put a legal cap on seal numbers
  4. The seals' food swam, bred on its own and could not be eaten to nothing, so hunger reached the herd while there were still fish

A hungry seal fails to breed this year, while food remains. Lichen gave no such warning: it was eaten down to bare rock, and the signal arrived only when there was nothing left. Same mechanism, opposite outcome.

Suppose the Pribilof seals had fed not on fish and squid but on a slow-growing shellfish bed they dug out of the seabed. What would the count of breeding males after 1911 most likely have looked like?

  1. A climb straight past ten thousand and then a crash, because the shortage would register only once the bed had been stripped
  2. The same S-curve, since the treaty protected the herd either way
  3. A slower climb to the same ceiling, since shellfish are harder to catch than fish
  4. No growth at all, because a shellfish bed could not support ten thousand seals

What bends a curve over is a warning that arrives in time. Food that moves, breeds independently and cannot be gathered down to nothing delivers that warning; food that is a standing bank balance does not, whatever is eating it.

In the sandbox, with the delay switched off, no growth rate at all will push the population above K. What does that show?

  1. Overshoot needs a lag between the resource running short and the population responding
  2. Overshoot only happens above a certain growth rate
  3. Overshoot happens when the carrying capacity is set too low
  4. The model has a fault and the result should be ignored

Biology is full of such delays. A reindeer cow that conceives in October calves the following May — seven months of growth committed on a decision made when there was still food. Long-lived adults keep eating for years after they stop raising young successfully.

Leave the delay at 3 years and raise K from 500 to 900. The population still shoots past the new, higher ceiling. What does that show?

  1. That 900 is still too low a ceiling for the growth rate chosen
  2. That a population always overshoots whatever ceiling it is given
  3. That a higher ceiling moves the overshoot rather than preventing it, because the delay has not been touched
  4. That raising K raises the growth rate as well, so the population arrives faster

This is the bigger-bottle question from Chapter 3.1 in a new form. Enlarging the container buys time and changes nothing about the process, because the delay that causes the overshoot belongs to the organism rather than to the ceiling.

After the crash, St Matthew Island could support fewer reindeer than it had in 1944. Why?

  1. The surviving reindeer were weaker animals
  2. The climate had permanently turned colder
  3. The lichen that was the carrying capacity had been eaten faster than it can regrow, and it grows a few millimetres a year
  4. Carrying capacity always falls after a population crash

This is the difference between using a resource and consuming the machinery that makes it. Hold onto it — Chapter 3.5 turns it into the hardest idea in the unit: a disturbed ecosystem may come back as a different one rather than a recovered one.

Picture a second reindeer herd overshooting on an island where the food is fast-growing grass rather than lichen. The crash is just as severe. What happens to that island's carrying capacity afterwards?

  1. It falls just as far, because a crash this severe always damages the habitat
  2. It rises, because far fewer animals are now sharing the grass
  3. It stays the same only if the herd is removed, since grazing prevents any regrowth
  4. It stays close to where it was, because grass rebuilds at roughly the rate it was being eaten

A crash and a lowered ceiling are two separate events. Overshoot guarantees the first; only a resource that cannot rebuild carries the damage forward into what the island can support afterwards.

Oxygen in a pond runs out faster the more fish there are. Oxygen is non-living. Which kind of limiting factor is it here?

  1. Density-independent, because oxygen is abiotic
  2. Density-dependent, because it bites harder when the pond is crowded
  3. Density-independent, because it is a resource rather than a predator
  4. Neither — oxygen is not a limiting factor

Ask the one question that always works: does this hit harder when the population is crowded? It sorts a fungal spore blown in from outside (density-independent, though living) and pond oxygen (density-dependent, though non-living) correctly, where the biotic/abiotic label gets both wrong.

A fungal spore blows onto an island from the mainland and kills whatever it lands on. The fungus is alive. Which kind of limiting factor is it here?

  1. Density-dependent, because the fungus is biotic
  2. Density-dependent, because disease always spreads more easily in a crowd
  3. Density-independent, because it does the same damage whether it lands among ten individuals or ten thousand
  4. Neither, because a factor arriving from outside the population cannot be a limiting factor

The crowding test cuts across the biotic and abiotic line in both directions: pond oxygen is non-living and regulates, a wind-blown spore is living and merely knocks. Only the regulating kind can pull a population back towards K, which is why the distinction earns its place.

Woolly aphids grow exponentially from April to August, then collapse within a few weeks — at the same time every year. What best explains the crash?

  1. They reached carrying capacity and settled there
  2. Ladybirds and parasitic wasps built up until they regulated the population
  3. They ran out of space on the host plant
  4. A density-independent factor — the season ended, and cold kills three thousand aphids as readily as thirty

The shape is the evidence: a gentle bend means density-dependent regulation, a cliff at the same date every year means the calendar. Be careful with the follow-up, though — how high the peak got before the season ended was shaped by density-dependent things as well. Real populations are almost never controlled by one kind of factor alone.

In Figure 3.7 the mean number of offspring per female sparrow falls steadily as the number of females on Mandarte Island rises. What does that slope show?

  1. Density-dependent limitation acting through birth rate — crowding is cutting reproduction rather than survival
  2. Density-independent limitation, since conditions on the island varied between seasons
  3. Density-dependent limitation acting through death rate, as in the kelp perch plot
  4. That the island's carrying capacity for sparrows drops as the population grows

Regulation does not have to work through death. A factor is density-dependent if it bites harder when the population is crowded, whichever of births and deaths it reaches — and the fitted line, at about −0.06 offspring per extra female, is a direct measurement of how hard.

Where this goes next

You can now put a name to every force acting on the reindeer curve, and you can build the model that produces it. What you cannot yet do is trust a graph.

Every figure in this chapter was handed to you with its axes already chosen — a convenient window, a helpful scale, a line that tells a clear story. Chapter 3.3 takes that away. It gives you one real dataset, lets you choose the window yourself, and shows you how to get three completely opposite conclusions out of it without changing a single number. That is not a trick. It is the most common way people are misled by real data, and learning to catch it is a learning target in its own right.
