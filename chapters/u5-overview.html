Unit 5 — Genetics & Inheritance

Unit 5 Overview

Every cell in your body carries the same set of instructions, written in a four-letter alphabet in a molecule too small to see. This unit follows those instructions outward: from the molecule to the protein it builds, then to the cell, the whole person and the next generation.

One sentence holds this unit together. It is worth writing on the inside of your folder:

Changes to DNA → changes to proteins → changes to organisms.

If an explanation in this unit stops making sense, work out which arrow you are on. Most questions in this unit ask you to move along one of them.

The case we keep coming back to

Instead of meeting genetics as a list of rules, you will follow one condition through the whole unit: cystic fibrosis. It is a good case to learn from because every level of this unit shows up in it.

A gene on chromosome 7, called CFTR, codes for a protein that forms a channel in the cell membrane and lets chloride ions out of the cell. Water follows the chloride, and that water keeps the mucus lining your lungs and gut thin enough to do its job.

People with cystic fibrosis inherit two non-working copies of that gene, one from each parent. They make little or no working CFTR protein. Without it, cells cannot balance salt and water, the mucus turns thick and sticky, passageways clog, and bacteria that would normally be swept away stay put.

Read that again and count the steps. A change in a DNA sequence changes a protein. The changed protein changes what a cell can do. That changes a tissue, and the tissue changes the person. Nothing in the chain is mysterious, but explaining all of it takes every chapter of this unit. That is why we start here.

Before you go on, write down your best answer to each question. Rough answers are fine. Come back at the end of the unit and rewrite them.

  1. What is a gene, physically?
  2. How could a change to a molecule you cannot see change how a person breathes?
  3. Both of a child's parents can be perfectly healthy and the child can still have cystic fibrosis. How?
  4. Two people can have cystic fibrosis and be affected very differently. Why might that be?

How the four standards fit together

This unit is built on four NGSS performance expectations. They form one chain, built in order, and each chapter adds the next link. The highlighted words show the skill each one asks for: construct an explanation, ask questions, make and defend a claim, and apply statistics and probability. Knowing the vocabulary is where you start, not where you finish.

  • HS-LS1-1: How does the structure of DNA determine the structure of proteins?
  • HS-LS3-1: What role do DNA and chromosomes play in passing traits from parents to offspring?
  • HS-LS3-2: Where does inheritable genetic variation come from?
  • HS-LS3-3: How do probability and statistics explain the distribution of traits?

Two of them are about the molecule: what DNA is and what it builds. Two are about the family: how it travels between generations and how we predict what turns up. The unit moves from one to the other in 5.3, where a change in the code becomes a change you can inherit.

Learning targets for the unit

These are the unit's "I can" statements, grouped by standard. Use them as a checklist. The number after each one is the chapter that teaches it.

Structure of DNA and proteins

  • I can analyse the structure and function of DNA and evaluate how DNA's structure dictates how it is copied and how it is expressed.
  • I can illustrate the overall flow of genetic information from DNA to RNA to protein and where each process occurs in the cell, including transcription, post-transcriptional processes and translation. (The Central Dogma)
  • I can model the flow of DNA to RNA to protein given a section of DNA with an accompanying amino acid chart.
  • I can construct an explanation of the idea that regions of coding DNA are called genes.

DNA, chromosomes and inherited traits

  • I can illustrate the overall flow of genetic information from DNA to RNA to protein and where each process occurs in the cell.
  • I can distinguish between dominant and recessive traits in terms of homozygous and heterozygous alleles.

Where genetic variation comes from

  • I can explain and apply Mendel's laws of genetics (the law of segregation, the law of independent assortment and the law of dominance) to meiosis.
  • I can predict changes in DNA, RNA or amino acid sequence as a consequence of point and frameshift mutations (e.g. sickle cell anaemia).
  • I can explain how some viruses, such as HIV (a retrovirus), can change a cell's DNA.

Probability and the distribution of traits

  • I can analyse and interpret data to predict the outcome of crossing organisms with specific phenotypes and/or genotypes using Punnett squares, for both Mendelian and non-Mendelian traits: incomplete dominance, codominance, multiple alleles, pleiotropy, polygenic inheritance and sex linkage.
  • I can analyse and interpret data found in family pedigrees for inheritance patterns, including genetic disorders caused by a single gene (e.g. haemophilia, colour blindness).
  • I can evaluate the effects the environment (stress, diet, behaviour, toxins) has on traits, considering epigenetics.

What you will actually do

Each chapter has an interactive where you practise the skill instead of reading about it. They are the closest thing in this reader to practical work, and they are where most of the thinking happens.

  • 5.1 Build a DNA molecule base by base, then unzip it and copy it.
  • 5.2 Transcribe a gene into mRNA and translate it into a protein, using a codon chart.
  • 5.3 Introduce each type of mutation into a real sequence and see what survives.
  • 5.4 Set up any cross in a Punnett square and read off the probabilities.
  • 5.5 Run crosses that break the simple rules: blood types, colour blindness and blended coats.
  • 5.6 Work out genotypes backwards from a family tree, and build a dihybrid cross.
