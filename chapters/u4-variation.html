Unit 4 — Reproduction

4.6 Where Variation Comes From

Chapter 4.5 justified meiosis on arithmetic alone: something has to halve. But meiosis does far more than halve, and this chapter is about the two extra things it does — the two mechanisms that make every gamete you produce different from every other one, and that make you different from your siblings. It ends with what happens when the machinery misfires.

The question, stated properly

Two siblings have the same two parents. Every chromosome either of them has came out of the same pool: the 46 chromosomes their first parent had, and the 46 their second parent had. There is no extra genetic material anywhere. And yet they are unmistakably different people.

Set the mutations aside for a moment. Copying errors happen, but they are rare — a handful of new mutations per gamete — and they cannot possibly account for the scale of difference between two siblings.

So the difference must come from which of the available chromosomes each of them got, and in what combination. And that is decided during meiosis, by two mechanisms, plus a third at fertilisation.

Three sources of new genetic combinations, and this chapter deals with all three:

  1. Crossing over — homologous chromosomes exchange segments, so the chromosome you pass on is not even one of the two you received.
  2. Independent assortment — each of your 23 pairs decides which member goes into which gamete at random and separately from every other pair.
  3. Random fertilisation — any one of the millions of possible sperm can meet any one of the millions of possible eggs.

The first two happen inside meiosis. The third happens afterwards, and it multiplies the first two together.

Your standard, HS-LS3-2, names three sources of inheritable variation: new genetic combinations through meiosis, viable errors during replication, and mutations caused by environmental factors. This chapter is the first of the three, in full. The other two you met at the end of Chapter 4.1 and will meet again in Unit 5.

Source one: crossing over

You met crossing over in Figure 4.22, as one of the three things that make meiosis different from mitosis. Now look at what it does to the contents of a gamete.

In prophase I, homologous chromosomes pair up along their whole length. While they are pressed together, non-sister chromatids break at matching points and rejoin to the other chromosome. The point where they cross is called a chiasma, and there are typically one to three of them on every chromosome pair, every time meiosis runs.

Here is the consequence, and it is bigger than it first looks. Before crossing over, you had a chromosome 7 from one parent and a chromosome 7 from the other, and any gamete would receive one or the other. After crossing over, you have two chromosome 7s that are each a mixture of both — sections of one spliced into the other. The gamete receives a chromosome that has never existed in any cell, in any person, ever before.

This is why crossing over matters more than it appears to. Without it, an allele on chromosome 7 would be permanently stuck to every other allele on that same chromosome, and they would be inherited as one block forever. Crossing over breaks up those blocks, so alleles that arrived together can be passed on separately.

In Unit 5 this shows up as a rule about which genes obey Mendel's laws and which do not: genes that sit close together on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together, because a crossover is unlikely to fall in the short gap between them. The further apart they are, the more often a crossover separates them — which is how the first gene maps were made.

Source two: independent assortment

This is the larger of the two mechanisms, and it is a straightforward consequence of one detail of metaphase I.

At metaphase I, homologous pairs line up on the equator two chromosomes deep. Each pair has to choose which member faces which pole — and that choice is random, and it is made separately for every single pair. The chromosome you inherited from one parent does not "stick with" the others you inherited from the same parent. Pair 1 faces one way, pair 2 decides independently, pair 3 decides independently, and so on.

That is independent assortment, and it is worth a diagram before it is worth a number.

With two pairs there are 4 possible gametes. With three pairs there are 8. The pattern is not addition, it is multiplication: each pair doubles the number of possibilities, because whatever the other pairs are doing, this one can still go either way.

So with n pairs the number of possible gametes is 2n. Work up to 23 yourself.

Over eight million genetically different gametes, from one person, from independent assortment alone — before a single crossover is counted, and before anything has been mutated. And that is the number for each parent.

Source three: random fertilisation, and the total

The two mechanisms above act inside one person. The third acts between two.

Any one of the 8,388,608 possible sperm can fertilise any one of the 8,388,608 possible eggs, and nothing selects between them. So the number of genetically different children two particular people could produce is those two numbers multiplied together:

223 × 223 = 246 = 70,368,744,177,664

Seventy trillion. About eight and a half thousand times the number of people alive on Earth, from a single pair of parents.

And this is still an underestimate, because it ignores crossing over entirely. Crossing over means the 23 chromosomes a gamete receives are not even the ones the parent inherited — each is a new mixture, cut at points that vary every time. Add that in and the number of genetically distinct children two people could have is, for any practical purpose, unlimited.

"Siblings share 50% of their DNA" needs a careful reading. It is true on average, and it is a statement about expectation, not a guarantee. Each sibling independently receives a random half from each parent, so how much two siblings happen to share varies — typically somewhere between about 38% and 62%.

Identical twins are the exception that proves the rule. They come from one zygote that split, so they share 100% — and that is precisely because only mitosis was involved after fertilisation. Every mechanism in this chapter happened once, before the split, so both twins got the same result.

Sexual reproduction generates variation on a scale that mutation could never approach — and it does so every generation, automatically, without waiting for anything to go wrong.

When the shuffle goes wrong

Everything above depends on chromosomes separating cleanly — homologues at anaphase I, sisters at anaphase II. Usually they do. Sometimes they do not.

Nondisjunction is the failure of chromosomes to separate during meiosis. A pair stays stuck together, both members go to the same pole, and the resulting gametes end up with one chromosome too many or one too few.

Reading the result

If a gamete with 24 chromosomes is fertilised by a normal one with 23, the zygote has 47 — three copies of one chromosome instead of two. That is a trisomy. If a gamete with 22 is fertilised, the zygote has 45 — only one copy. That is a monosomy.

These are not mutations. Not one base of DNA has been changed. Every gene involved is a perfectly normal gene; there are simply too many or too few copies of it, and the resulting imbalance in how much protein is made is enough to disrupt development.

Two patterns in that table are worth extracting, because they are the actual biology rather than a list to memorise.

Trisomies of large chromosomes are not survivable; trisomies of small ones sometimes are. Read down the autosome rows: 21, then 18, then 13 — increasing in size and in the number of genes disrupted, and increasing in severity in exactly that order. There is nothing special about the number 21. It is just the smallest.

Sex chromosome conditions are much milder than autosomal ones. That is not a coincidence either. A cell with more than one X switches the spares off almost entirely, so the dosage problem largely fixes itself — and the Y carries so few genes that having an extra one changes little.

The chance of nondisjunction rises sharply with the age of the person producing the egg, and now you have the machinery to see why. From Chapter 4.5: every primary oocyte a person will ever have is already made and paused part-way through prophase I before they are born. An egg ovulated at 40 has been holding its chromosomes paired up, mid-division, for four decades. The proteins holding them together degrade over that time. Chapter 4.7 comes back to this.

Check your understanding

An organism has 4 homologous pairs. How many genetically different gametes can independent assortment alone produce?

  1. 8
  2. 16
  3. 4
  4. 256

Two choices per pair, and the pairs are independent, so the counts multiply: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 24 = 16. Not 4 × 2 and not 42.

Independent assortment alone can produce 32 genetically different gametes in a certain species. How many homologous pairs does that species have?

  1. 32
  2. 6
  3. 5
  4. 16

Each pair doubles the total, so the number of gametes is 2 raised to the number of pairs. Reading the rule backwards means asking which power of 2 gives 32 — and 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 is five doublings.

Which stage of meiosis is independent assortment a consequence of?

  1. Prophase I, when homologues pair up
  2. Anaphase II, when sisters separate
  3. Telophase I, when the cell divides
  4. Metaphase I, when each pair independently chooses which member faces which pole

The randomness is in the orientation of each pair on the metaphase I plate. Anaphase I then simply carries out whatever metaphase I set up.

In one cell at metaphase I, pair 1 has its maternal chromosome facing the left pole. What does that tell you about which way pair 2's maternal chromosome will face?

  1. Nothing — pair 2 makes its own choice, and either pole is equally likely
  2. It will face the left pole too, because chromosomes from the same parent travel together
  3. It will face the right pole, to keep the two poles balanced
  4. It depends on whether a crossover occurred in prophase I

The word doing the work in "independent assortment" is independent. Every pair decides separately, which is why the possibilities double with each pair rather than staying at two.

What does crossing over produce that independent assortment cannot?

  1. Individual chromosomes that are new mixtures of both parental versions
  2. Gametes with the wrong number of chromosomes
  3. Identical gametes
  4. Extra copies of a chromosome

Independent assortment shuffles whole chromosomes and never changes any of them. Crossing over rebuilds the chromosomes themselves, so alleles that were on the same chromosome can be separated.

Two genes sit some distance apart on the same chromosome. If crossing over never happened, how could those two genes be passed on separately?

  1. Independent assortment would separate them about half the time, as it does for genes on different chromosomes
  2. They could not — they would be inherited as one block every time
  3. They would separate at anaphase II, when the sister chromatids part
  4. Random fertilisation would separate them, because any sperm can meet any egg

This is the reason crossing over matters more than its small scale suggests. Every other mechanism in this chapter moves chromosomes about intact — only a crossover cuts and rejoins them, and only that can break a linked block apart.

Nondisjunction happens at anaphase I. What are the chromosome counts in the four resulting gametes?

  1. 24, 22, 23, 23
  2. 23, 23, 23, 23
  3. 24, 24, 22, 22
  4. 24, 24, 24, 24

At anaphase I there is still only one cell, so a failure there affects everything downstream — all four gametes are abnormal. The 24, 22, 23, 23 pattern is the signature of a failure at anaphase II, where only one of two already-separate cells goes wrong.

One meiosis produces four gametes holding 24, 22, 23 and 23 chromosomes. Where did it go wrong?

  1. At anaphase I, when the homologous pair failed to separate
  2. At prophase I, when the homologues paired up
  3. At fertilisation, when two gametes combined
  4. At anaphase II, in one of the two cells the first division produced

Count the damaged gametes and you have located the failure. Two normal gametes survive only if the cell had already divided when the mistake was made, which puts it in the second division.

Why is trisomy 21 survivable when trisomy of most other chromosomes is not?

  1. Chromosome 21 is not really needed
  2. Chromosome 21 is the smallest autosome and carries the fewest genes, so an extra copy disrupts the least
  3. The extra chromosome 21 is switched off completely
  4. Trisomy 21 involves no extra genetic material

The problem with a trisomy is dosage — too much protein from too many gene copies. The fewer genes on the chromosome, the smaller the disruption, and 21 is the smallest.

Triple X (XXX) often causes no noticeable effects, and many people never find out they have it. Why is an extra X so much milder than an extra autosome?

  1. The X chromosome carries almost no genes
  2. The extra X is discarded early in development
  3. A cell with more than one X switches the spares off almost entirely, so the dosage problem largely fixes itself
  4. Three X chromosomes is a normal karyotype, not an extra chromosome at all

Every one of these conditions comes down to dosage. An extra autosome is read in full and floods the cell with surplus protein; an extra X is switched off, so the cell ends up reading roughly the usual amount either way.

Why are identical twins genetically identical when ordinary siblings are not?

  1. They inherit the same alleles by chance
  2. Meiosis runs twice in exactly the same way
  3. They share a placenta
  4. They come from one zygote that split, so every shuffling mechanism happened once, before the split

Crossing over, independent assortment and fertilisation all happened once, producing one zygote. Everything after the split was mitosis, which makes exact copies.

Two children are carried in the same pregnancy, but they came from two separate eggs fertilised by two separate sperm. How alike are they genetically?

  1. Genetically identical, because they were conceived at the same time
  2. No more alike than any other pair of siblings — typically sharing somewhere between about 38% and 62% of their DNA
  3. Exactly 50% alike, because each parent contributes half to each child
  4. Identical to one parent each, since one egg carries one parent's chromosomes

What makes two people genetically identical is coming from one zygote. Two zygotes means two independent runs of the whole shuffle, and sharing a pregnancy does nothing to bring the two results closer together.

Where this goes next

You now have the complete answer to the question this chapter opened with, and it is a much better answer than "genes get mixed up". Two parents, seventy trillion possible children, and a mechanism for each stage of the shuffling.

What has been treated as a black box throughout is where meiosis happens. This chapter has said "in the gonads" and left it there. It has also assumed something odd without pointing it out: a single meiosis produces four gametes, and that is true for sperm — but a person producing eggs releases roughly one egg per month, about 400 in a lifetime, not four per meiosis multiplied by millions of cells.

Chapter 4.7 is about the two organ systems that run meiosis, and about why one of them makes four gametes from every cell and the other makes one.
