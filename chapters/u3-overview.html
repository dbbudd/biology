Unit 3 — Ecosystem Dynamics

Unit 3 Overview

Nothing in nature grows forever. Something always stops it, and this unit is about what stops it, when, and how hard. By the end you should be able to look at a graph of any population, name the forces shaping it, and say whether you are looking at a recovery, a collapse, or a completely different ecosystem.

One question runs through all six chapters:

What decides how many of something an ecosystem can hold, and what happens when that changes?

Every graph, case study and calculation in this unit answers part of that question. If a section stops making sense, ask which half it belongs to: the ceiling, or the change.

The case we keep coming back to

In August 1944 the United States Coast Guard released 29 reindeer onto St Matthew Island, a treeless island of tundra far out in the Bering Sea. It covers about 330 square kilometres of rock and lichen, with no town for hundreds of kilometres. The reindeer were meant as an emergency food supply for the nineteen men stationed there. The war ended, the station closed, the men left, and the reindeer were forgotten.

Nobody counted them again until 1957. There were 1,350. Six years later, in 1963, the biologist David Klein counted about 6,000. They were some of the fattest, healthiest reindeer ever measured.

He came back in 1966 and found 42, all but one of them female, and the island stripped of the lichen the animals lived on.

This is not really a story about reindeer. It is a pattern you will meet again in a bottle of bacteria, a bay full of crabs, a reef full of lionfish, and probably in this week's news. Notice what the graph does not tell you. It does not say why they grew or why they crashed. It does not say whether 6,000 was ever sustainable, or whether the island could support reindeer today. Reading those things from a curve is a skill, and this unit teaches it.

Write down your best answer to each question now. Rough answers are fine. Come back at the end of the unit and rewrite them.

  1. Why did the herd grow so slowly at first and then so fast? Nothing changed about the reindeer.
  2. What stopped it? Name the most likely thing, and say how you would test whether you were right.
  3. Why did the population overshoot, climbing past what the island could feed, instead of levelling off at the right number?
  4. If 29 more reindeer were released on St Matthew Island tomorrow, would the same thing happen again? Explain what would have to be true either way.

How the two standards fit together

This unit is built on two NGSS performance expectations. They form two halves of one argument, and the unit moves from the first to the second in 3.4.

  • HS-LS2-1: What sets the ceiling on a population, and how do you show it with real numbers, at different scales?
  • HS-LS2-6: When conditions change, does an ecosystem recover, or become a different one?

The first three chapters are about one population and its ceiling: how it grows, what holds it down, and how to read that off real data without being fooled. The last three are about everything else in the room: what one population is connected to, what happens when a connection is cut, and whether an ecosystem that has been knocked over gets back up as the same ecosystem.

Look closely at the highlighted words. HS-LS2-1 means doing arithmetic on real data, and 3.3 is there to make you good at it. HS-LS2-6 means evaluating claims, not describing or listing them. You will be given claims that are partly true and asked which parts hold up. That is harder than recall, and much more useful.

Learning targets for the unit

These are the unit's "I can" statements, grouped by standard. Use them as a checklist. The number after each one is the chapter that teaches it. Practice marks a science and engineering practice, a skill scientists use. Crosscutting marks a crosscutting concept, an idea that applies across all of science.

Populations, ceilings and calculations

  • I can differentiate between exponential and logistic growth.
  • I can describe multiple factors that affect the carrying capacity of an ecosystem.
  • I can use calculations to identify changes in populations.
  • I can analyze the effects on populations of density-dependent and density-independent factors.
  • I can interpret a population graph and determine the possible cause and effect relationships shown in graphical data.
  • I can recognize proportional relationships between different quantities as scales change, using math (trends, averages, histograms, graphs) of ecosystem factors to identify changes over time in the numbers and types of organisms in ecosystems of different scales.
  • I can analyze results from a computational simulation.

Stability, change and new ecosystems

  • I can explain how factors like hunting, seasonal flooding, sea level rise and volcanic eruptions will affect communities in an ecosystem.
  • I can evaluate or predict impacts of an invasive species to an ecosystem.
  • I can explain the process of ecological succession and analyze the factors that cause succession.
  • I can analyze conditions that affect stability and factors that control rates of change in natural systems.
  • I can evaluate and critique established scientific claims, evidence and reasoning.

What you will actually do

Each chapter has an interactive where you practise the skill instead of reading about it. Most ask you to commit to a prediction before they show you anything, because writing a prediction down makes you think it through.

  • 3.1 Doubling machine: feel how fast exponential growth actually is, and learn to read a log axis.
  • 3.2 Growth-model sandbox: set r, K and N₀ yourself, and find the one setting that produces overshoot.
  • 3.3 Scale slider: take one real dataset and get three opposite answers out of it.
  • 3.4 Pull a thread: remove a species, predict every consequence, then check.
  • 3.5 Trophic cascade builder: walk Borneo in the 1950s one link at a time, then judge the evidence.
  • 3.6 Succession timeline: rebuild an ecosystem from bare rock, and from burnt ground, and compare.
