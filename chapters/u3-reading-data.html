Unit 3 — Ecosystem Dynamics

3.3 Reading Population Data

Every graph in Chapter 3.2 came with its axes already chosen for you. This chapter takes that away. It is the mathematics chapter of the unit — percent change, rates, and the one skill that HS-LS2-1 names explicitly: recognising what happens to a conclusion when the scale changes underneath it.

Percent change, and the thing it is not

The formula your anchor guide gives you is this:

percent change = (P2 − P1) ÷ P1 × 100

where P1 is the population at the earlier time and P2 at the later one. Three things to get right, and each of them costs marks:

  • Divide by P1, always. The change is measured relative to where you started. Dividing by P2 is the most common error in this topic and gives a different, meaningless number.
  • Keep the sign. A negative answer means the population fell. Writing −78% as 78% turns a catastrophe into a boom.
  • Show the substitution. Your guide asks you to show your work, and in the summative the substituted line is worth marks on its own even if the arithmetic slips.

Now the correction, and it matters. That formula is a percent change. It is not a growth rate, even though it is often labelled one — including on some of the slides you will have seen.

A percent change answers "how much bigger or smaller, end to end?" A rate answers "how fast, per unit of time?" They are different questions and they need different units. Consider: a population falling 78% over 16 years and a population falling 78% over 2 years have the same percent change and are completely different emergencies. If a question gives you two years and two numbers and asks for a "growth rate", work out the percent change, then say clearly what period it covers — or divide it out to get a change per year. Being explicit is never wrong.

Worked example. African elephants: 1,300,000 in 1979 and 286,233 in 1995.

percent change = (286,233 − 1,300,000) ÷ 1,300,000 × 100 = (−1,013,767) ÷ 1,300,000 × 100 = −78.0%

Over 16 years. Averaged out, that is about −9.0% a year — the population shrinking by roughly a tenth of itself, every year, for sixteen years running. The two statements describe the same collapse; the second one tells you it was relentless rather than a single bad event. (The scale slider below shows both numbers for whatever window you choose.)

Where the change comes from: birth rate and death rate

A percent change tells you what happened. It says nothing about how. For that you need the two quantities underneath every population change in this unit.

  • Birth rate — the number of live births per 1,000 individuals, per year.
  • Death rate — the number of deaths per 1,000 individuals, per year.

Both are expressed per 1,000 on purpose. That makes them comparable between a population of two hundred and a population of two million, which raw counts are not. Two hundred deaths in a herd of five hundred and two hundred deaths in a herd of fifty thousand are not the same event.

That is a very different ecosystem from one where both rates are low, even though the graph looks identical. A flat line hides more than it shows.

Reading cause and effect off a graph

The most famous population dataset in ecology comes from an accounting ledger. The Hudson's Bay Company recorded how many snowshoe hare and lynx pelts it bought from trappers in northern Canada, year by year, for nearly two centuries. Charles Elton and Mary Nicholson gathered the records in 1942, and every biology course since has used them.

Three things are worth noticing, and your anchor guide asks you for exactly three:

  1. Both populations cycle, rising and crashing repeatedly rather than settling at a carrying capacity. Roughly a ten-year rhythm.
  2. The lynx peak comes after the hare peak, by a year or two. The hares peak in 1903 and the lynx in 1904; the hares peak again in 1913 and the lynx in 1915.
  3. The lynx line is much smaller. There are perhaps ten times as many hares as lynx — which is exactly what Chapter 3.4 will explain with the ten-per-cent rule.

The obvious story is a feedback loop: plenty of hares means well-fed lynx raising many kittens, so lynx numbers rise; more lynx means more hares eaten, so hares crash; fewer hares means lynx starve, so lynx crash; with few lynx the hares recover — and round again. Predation is a density-dependent factor, and here it is regulating both populations at once.

That story is too tidy, and the data cannot settle it. Field experiments since the 1990s have shown that the hare cycle is driven at least as much by food — the hares strip their winter browse and the plants respond by growing less palatable — as by lynx. When researchers fenced areas and excluded predators, hare numbers stayed higher but still cycled.

There is a second problem, and it is worth more than the first: these are not population counts. They are records of pelts sold. That number depends on how many animals existed, and also on how many trappers were working, how good the fur prices were, and how easily the company could reach a region. A graph is only ever as good as what was actually measured, and this one measures commerce as well as biology.

Neither point makes the figure useless. It makes it a good example of the difference between a pattern in the data and an explanation of it — which is precisely what HS-LS2-6 asks you to be able to evaluate.

The humpback whale, end to end

This is the case study where the deck makes its own strongest point, and it is worth following all the way through.

Commercial whaling in the twentieth century was industrial in scale. The catch was recorded, so unlike the hare and lynx data we know reasonably well how many animals were removed and when.

Now the population on the receiving end. The humpback whales that breed off Brazil and feed around South Georgia — the western South Atlantic population — are among the best-studied. Before whaling there were roughly 27,000 of them. By the late 1950s there were about 450.

That is a percent change of −98.3%. Fewer than two whales in every hundred were left.

Commercial whaling was banned in 1986. Before you look at what happened, do what your anchor guide asks: predict it. Sketch the line from 1986 out to 2030, and give a reason for the shape you drew.

By 2019 the population was estimated at about 24,900 — roughly 92% of its pre-whaling size. From 450 to 24,900 is a percent change of about +5,400%, one of the most complete recoveries of a large animal ever recorded.

Three questions the guide asks, answered properly:

  • How did the ban affect the population? Look at the two lines together. The catch line reaches zero and the population line, which had been falling for eighty years, turns and climbs. Notice the recovery does not start instantly — humpbacks take about ten years to mature and produce one calf every two to three years, so the population responds on a whale's timescale, not a policy's.
  • Is whaling density-dependent or density-independent? Density-independent. A factory fleet does not hunt harder because whales are crowded; it hunts to a quota, and it kept hunting as the population collapsed. That is exactly why hunting can drive a species to extinction where predation rarely does — a natural predator gives up when prey gets scarce, because searching stops paying.
  • What else could explain the increase? Be honest here rather than reflexive. Better protection of feeding grounds, and possibly reduced competition for krill because so many other whale species had also been removed. It is also worth asking whether the estimates themselves improved: aerial and photo-identification surveys got much better over this period, and a rising count can partly reflect a rising ability to count.

The summary in your guide, filled in:

Data and graphs can allow the reader to predict the cause and effect in a population change. The scale of data can greatly change the predictions that a reader can have. The story of the humpback whale vastly improved when given the full scale of data in this example.

A checklist for any population graph

Pull the chapter together into something you can actually use in an exam or on a news article. Before you accept what a population graph appears to say, ask:

  1. What is on the axes, exactly? Pelts sold is not hares alive. Sightings is not abundance. Reported cases is not infections.
  2. Where does the record start and stop? A window that begins at a low point will show recovery; one that begins at a peak will show decline. Ask what came before the left edge.
  3. Is the vertical axis at zero? A truncated axis makes a 4% wobble look like a collapse.
  4. Is it linear or logarithmic? A straight line means completely different things on the two.
  5. How many data points are there really? Two censuses joined by a straight line is not a trend; it is a line.
  6. What else changed at the same time? A change that lines up with an event is a hypothesis, not a conclusion.

Correlation is not causation, and the whale graph is a good place to practise saying why. The catch fell to zero in 1986 and the population rose afterwards. That is a correlation. What makes the causal claim credible here is not the graph — it is the mechanism: we know exactly how whaling reduces whale numbers, and we know the size of the effect because the catches were counted. Compare that with the hare-and-lynx graph, where the correlation is beautiful and the mechanism turned out to be more complicated than everyone assumed.

The test to apply: is there a plausible mechanism, does the size of the effect match, and has anything else been ruled out? A graph alone can never pass that test on its own.

Where this goes next

Everything so far has treated a population as if it were alone in the world — one line on one graph, with food and predators appearing as abstract "limiting factors". That was a useful simplification and it has run out.

Chapter 3.4 puts the other species back in. It asks what happens to everything else when one population changes, and it turns out that the answer is sometimes "almost nothing" and sometimes "the entire community collapses" — and that the difference between those two cases is one of the most important discoveries in modern ecology. It was made by one man, on one stretch of Washington coastline, throwing starfish into the sea.

Quick check

One population falls 78% over 16 years. Another falls 78% over 2 years. What does the percent change tell you about the difference between them?

  1. The second one has the larger percent change
  2. Both are shrinking at the same rate per year
  3. Nothing — the percent change is identical, so you need a rate to tell them apart
  4. The first is more serious because it went on for longer

A percent change answers "how much bigger or smaller, end to end?" A rate answers "how fast, per unit of time?" Different questions, different units. When a question hands you two numbers and two years and asks for a "growth rate", say clearly what period your answer covers — being explicit is never wrong.

The elephant collapse can be reported as −78% over 16 years, or as about −9.0% a year. What does the second version add?

  1. Nothing — it is the same calculation with a division written on the end
  2. The percent change for one particular year, 1994 to 1995
  3. A smaller number, which makes the collapse sound less severe
  4. How fast the loss happened — a change per unit of time rather than end to end

The two statements describe the same collapse. The per-year figure is the one that tells you it was relentless rather than a single bad event, which is why a question asking for a "growth rate" is really asking you to put time back into the answer.

A population sits exactly at carrying capacity and its size does not change from year to year. What can you say about births and deaths?

  1. They are equal — and both could still be enormous
  2. Both must be low, or the line would not be flat
  3. Almost nothing is being born
  4. Deaths must slightly exceed births

A population with a huge birth rate and an equally huge death rate is a completely different ecosystem from one where both are low — many short lives against a few long ones. The graph cannot tell them apart. A flat line hides more than it shows.

A population is falling steadily year after year. What does that line establish about its birth and death rates?

  1. The death rate is high — a falling line requires one
  2. Deaths outnumber births — though the line cannot tell you how large either rate is
  3. Almost nothing is being born
  4. Both rates are themselves falling

Direction comes from the gap between the two rates; magnitude does not show up on the line at all. That is why the rates are quoted per 1,000 individuals — it makes a herd of five hundred and a herd of fifty thousand comparable, which raw counts of births and deaths never are.

The same elephant dataset gives −78% for 1979–1995 and +38% for 1995–2016. Which figure is the honest one?

  1. The recent one, because it describes the situation now
  2. The longer one, because more years is always better
  3. Neither — one of them must contain a calculation error
  4. Both — and neither is usable without stating which years it covers

Two honest calculations from one dataset can support opposite conclusions, because the window does the arguing. A conservation charity would quote the first; an ivory lobbyist the second. Neither is lying, and both are incomplete.

A report on the western South Atlantic humpbacks could say "up about 5,400% since 1958" or "still slightly below the pre-whaling figure of 27,000". Which is the fair account?

  1. Both, each with its years attached — the recovery is real, and the baseline says what it is a recovery from
  2. The +5,400%, because 1958 is where the recovery begins
  3. The pre-whaling comparison, because the longer window is the honest one
  4. Neither, until the whales have been counted the same way each time

The window does the arguing here as much as it does with the elephants, and the direction of the claim reverses depending on which endpoint you start from. Quoting a percent change without its years is not a rounding error — it is the whole argument left unstated.

Shrink the window on the scale slider to one or two years and the percent change swings wildly as you slide it along. Why?

  1. The percent-change formula stops working over short periods
  2. Over a short window the year-to-year wobble is large compared with the underlying trend, so you end up measuring the noise
  3. The population genuinely reverses direction every year or two
  4. Fewer animals are alive during a shorter period

This is the mirror image of choosing too long a window. Too short and you report the wobble; too long and you hide a reversal inside it. There is no automatically safe choice, which is why a window has to be stated and justified rather than assumed.

Now open the slider to the full record, 1979–2016. The elephant data returns a single modest percent change. What has that window hidden?

  1. Nothing — the longest window uses every point, so it is the most reliable
  2. That the two endpoints were probably measured by different methods
  3. A collapse and then a partial recovery, which cancel each other into one unremarkable figure
  4. The percent change itself, which cannot be calculated over so long an interval

The blue dashed line between two endpoints is a line, not a trend — it describes the journey between them no better than a straight road describes a mountain pass. Two censuses joined up can conceal a reversal as easily as a short window can invent one.

Lynx numbers peak a year or two after hare numbers. A student concludes that lynx predation drives the hare cycle. What is the weakness?

  1. The student has the order of the two peaks backwards
  2. A two-year lag is far too long for predation to explain
  3. The timing fits that story, but it fits other stories too — hares fenced away from predators still cycled
  4. Nothing — a lag establishes which population is causing the other

When researchers excluded predators, hare numbers stayed higher and still cycled: the hares strip their winter browse and the plants respond by growing less palatable. The graph shows a pattern. It cannot, on its own, supply the explanation — which is precisely what HS-LS2-6 asks you to evaluate.

Which piece of evidence would do most to test the claim that lynx predation drives the hare cycle, rather than merely illustrate it?

  1. Another century of the same pelt record, showing the same lagged peaks
  2. Fencing predators out of an area and seeing whether the hare cycle then behaves any differently
  3. A calculation confirming there are about ten times as many hares as lynx
  4. A graph of hare numbers alone, showing how steep the crashes are

An observed pattern is consistent with several mechanisms at once; an experiment can rule some of them out. The fencing work did exactly that, and the answer it gave was not the tidy one — the hares kept cycling, which sent attention to their winter food.

The hare and lynx graph plots pelts sold to a fur company. Why is that not the same as counting animals?

  1. Because the animals were dead by the time they were counted
  2. Because the number also depends on how many trappers were working, what fur was worth, and where the company could reach
  3. Because lynx ate some of the hares before trappers could reach them
  4. Because the records only cover part of Canada

First question on the checklist, every time: what is on the axes, exactly? Pelts sold is not hares alive; sightings is not abundance; reported cases is not infections. This graph measures commerce as well as biology — which does not make it useless, but does mean you have to describe it accurately.

The bars in Figure 3.11 fall steeply after the 1960s. Read the axis: what exactly is falling?

  1. The number of whales left alive, since so few could still be found
  2. The effort the fleets put in, since catch is a measure of hunting effort
  3. The number of whales killed in each decade — that, and nothing more
  4. The rate at which whale populations were recovering

Figures 3.11 and 3.12 look like they tell one story, and they do only because the second one puts a population axis beside the catch. A catch record on its own can fall because the animals ran out, or because the fleets were stopped from taking them — and the bars do not choose between those.

Is commercial whaling a density-dependent or a density-independent factor?

  1. Density-independent — the fleets hunted to a quota and kept hunting as the population collapsed
  2. Density-dependent, because hunters find whales more easily when whales are common
  3. Density-dependent, because humans are living things, so the factor is biotic
  4. Neither — humans are outside the ecosystem

This is exactly why hunting can drive a species to extinction where natural predation rarely does. The western South Atlantic humpbacks fell from roughly 27,000 to about 450 — a change of −98.3% — with no easing of the pressure as they became scarce.

Lynx predation on hares is density-dependent; commercial whaling was not. What is the test that separates the two?

  1. Whether one living thing is acting on another
  2. Whether the factor is severe enough to collapse the population
  3. Whether the pressure eases as the population thins — a lynx stops finding hares worth hunting, and a quota does not notice
  4. Whether human beings are the ones involved

Density-dependent factors are self-limiting: they weaken as the population they act on shrinks, which is what makes them regulators rather than exterminators. A factor that keeps its grip all the way down has nothing to stop it, and the humpbacks came within about 450 animals of showing what that means.
