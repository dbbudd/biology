Unit 4 — Reproduction

4.3 When the Brakes Fail

Chapter 4.2 treated cell division as a good thing that organisms want more of. This chapter turns that around. Your body divides three to four million cells every second, and the difficult part is not making that happen — it is making it stop, in the right place, every single time. Cancer is what a body looks like when the stopping fails.

The problem nobody states out loud

Go back to the cut finger in Figure 4.2a. Cells at the edge of the wound divide until the gap closes — and then they stop.

Ask how they know. There is no supervisor. Each cell is a single cell with no view of the finger. Yet skin heals to exactly the thickness of skin, a liver regrows to the size of a liver and no further, and the twenty-odd cell divisions that rebuild a wound halt at the right moment across millions of independent cells.

So a cell must be able to answer questions like am I still needed? and is there room? and is my DNA fit to copy? — and must be able to refuse to divide when the answer is no. That refusal is the subject of this chapter.

State it as sharply as possible, because everything else follows from it:

Cell division is not the default that has to be triggered. In a multicellular body it is a licensed activity that has to be permitted, and most cells are refused permission most of the time.

Remember from Chapter 4.1 that most of your cells are sitting in G0 right now — alive, working, and not preparing to divide at all. That is not neglect. It is the normal, actively maintained state.

The cell cycle control system, and its three checkpoints

The cell cycle is regulated by a set of proteins that act like an inspection regime. At three points in the cycle the cell stops and checks something before it is allowed to continue. Those points are called checkpoints.

A checkpoint is not a repair mechanism and it is not a kill switch. It is an inspection, and it has three possible outcomes: pass and continue; hold in place while repair enzymes work; or, if the damage cannot be repaired, trigger apoptosis — a controlled, tidy self-destruction that dismantles the cell without spilling its contents.

Where in the cycle would you put those three inspections? You already know the wheel from Chapter 4.1. This time you add to it.

Three gates on the cell cycle

The G1 checkpoint asks about growth signals, and that is how a cell knows the tissue is full. Neighbouring cells release chemical signals when there is room and stop when there is not, and cells also detect direct contact with their neighbours — an effect called contact inhibition. A cell packed in on all sides receives no permission to divide, so it does not.

That is the answer to the cut finger. Nobody supervises the healing. Each cell divides while it has room and stops when it is surrounded, and the wound closes because the last cell to fill the gap is the last one that had permission.

Step 4 is the most important idea in this chapter. Each checkpoint catches something the others do not, and a cell that slips past a broken G2 is usually caught by G1 in the next generation. That redundancy is why a single mutation in a single cell almost never causes cancer, and why cancer risk rises so steeply with age: it takes time to accumulate several independent failures in the same lineage.

What cancer actually is

Now the definition, which should read as a summary of what you have just done rather than as new information.

Cancer is uncontrolled cell division. Cells that have lost the ability to respond to the signals that would normally stop them divide when they should not, and the mass of cells they produce is a tumour.

Cancer cells do not divide unusually fast. This surprises people. Many cancer cells divide at about the same rate as the healthy cells they came from — some divide more slowly. What is different is that they never stop. A normal cell divides a limited number of times under permission and then enters G0; a cancer cell keeps going. Over enough generations, "always" beats "fast".

Not every tumour is dangerous in the same way, and the distinction is about behaviour rather than size.

Compare that with what you saw in the model. Switching off G1 alone gave you uncontrolled growth with clean DNA — a mass of cells that will not stop but is not genetically unstable, which is roughly the benign case. Switching off G1 and G2 gave you uncontrolled growth in cells whose DNA is getting worse every generation. That instability is what eventually produces cells able to break out of the tissue and travel.

Where the failures come from

Checkpoints are run by proteins, proteins are built from genes, and genes can be mutated. So the causes of cancer are the causes of mutation, which you met at the end of Chapter 4.1 and in Unit 5:

  • Routine copying errors. Three billion bases, copied millions of times a second across the body. Some faults arise from nothing but arithmetic, which is why some cancer risk is unavoidable.
  • Mutagens. Anything that damages DNA raises the rate: ultraviolet light, tobacco smoke, some chemicals, ionising radiation. These do not create a new mechanism — they increase the number of chances.
  • Inherited alleles. Some people inherit a faulty copy of a checkpoint gene from a parent. They are not born with cancer; they are born one step closer to it, because one of the several failures needed has already happened in every cell of their body.
  • Some viruses. A few viruses interfere with checkpoint proteins directly. HPV is the clearest example, and it is why an HPV vaccine reduces cervical cancer.

Two categories of gene come up repeatedly:

Gene typeNormal jobWhat a harmful mutation doesAnalogy
Proto-oncogeneCodes for a protein that promotes division when it is neededBecomes stuck permanently on, so the cell is constantly told to divideAn accelerator jammed down
Tumour suppressor geneCodes for a protein that stops division when something is wrongStops working, so nothing applies the brakeA brake cable cut

The best known tumour suppressor is p53, which acts at the G1 checkpoint: it detects DNA damage, halts the cycle while repair is attempted, and triggers apoptosis if repair fails. Roughly half of all human cancers carry a mutated p53 — which is precisely the switch you flipped in the model.

This explains a pattern that otherwise looks like bad luck. Cancer becomes far more common with age, not because old cells are weak, but because several independent failures have to accumulate in the same cell lineage, and that takes decades of divisions. It also explains why cancers cluster in tissues that divide most — skin, gut lining, blood, lung — and are rare in tissues that have been sitting in G0 since childhood, like mature neurons.

Notice, finally, that this chapter has quietly answered a question from Chapter 4.1. Chemotherapy drugs work by attacking cells that are actively dividing — some by preventing spindle fibres forming, so cells arrest at metaphase. That is why they damage cancer cells preferentially, and also why their side effects are hair loss, nausea and a suppressed immune system: hair follicles, gut lining and bone marrow are exactly the healthy tissues with the highest mitotic index.

What does a checkpoint in the cell cycle actually do?

  1. Repairs damaged DNA
  2. Speeds up division when the cell is healthy
  3. Inspects a condition and stops the cycle if it is not met
  4. Destroys any cell that reaches it

It is an inspection with three outcomes: continue, hold while repair enzymes work, or trigger apoptosis. It does not do the repairing itself, and it does not kill everything that arrives.

A cell arrives at a checkpoint carrying DNA damage that the repair enzymes cannot put right. What is the outcome?

  1. Apoptosis — the cell is dismantled tidily, without spilling its contents
  2. It is held at that checkpoint indefinitely and simply never divides again
  3. The checkpoint repairs the damage itself and then releases the cell
  4. It passes, because a checkpoint records damage but cannot block a cell

The three outcomes are graded to how bad the damage is, not chosen at random. Apoptosis is the last of them, and it is controlled rather than messy — the cell is taken apart from the inside so the tissue around it is unharmed.

Which best describes a cancer cell?

  1. A cell that has lost the ability to stop dividing
  2. A cell that divides much faster than any healthy cell
  3. A cell with a completely different genome from the rest of the body
  4. A cell that has been infected by a bacterium

Speed is not the defining feature — many cancer cells divide at a normal rate. The defining feature is that they never stop, because the controls that would stop them have failed.

A tumour in the gut lining is measured and found to divide more slowly than the healthy gut lining beside it, yet the tumour keeps growing and the healthy lining does not. How is that possible?

  1. The measurement must be wrong, because only faster division can grow a mass
  2. The tumour cells are dividing by some process other than mitosis
  3. The healthy cells are dying off and leaving room for the tumour to fill
  4. The healthy cells stop when the tissue is full; the tumour cells carry on regardless

Growth is accumulation, not pace. A healthy cell divides a limited number of times under permission and then settles into G0, so its numbers hold steady; a cell that never settles keeps adding to the total every generation, however slowly it divides.

Why does a single mutation in a single cell rarely cause cancer?

  1. Single mutations are always repaired perfectly
  2. One cell is too small to matter
  3. Mutations only affect the cell they occur in, and that cell soon dies
  4. There are several checkpoints, and one that is still working can catch what another missed

The checkpoints back each other up. A cell that slips past a broken G2 is usually destroyed by G1 in the next generation, so several independent failures have to accumulate in the same lineage.

In the model, switching off G2 alone makes faulty cells appear, but the fault count settles rather than running away. What accounts for that?

  1. With G2 off, the damage that gets through is the mild kind, so it never builds up
  2. G1 is still inspecting, so it removes most of the faulty cells in the following generation
  3. Cells divide less often once a checkpoint is missing, so fewer faults are created
  4. The repair enzymes work harder to make up for the missing checkpoint

This is what makes the model worth running: flipping one switch changes what gets through, never how much damage occurs. The count only runs away once the inspection that would have caught the survivors is off as well — which is the case a real cancer has to reach.

A mutation causes a tumour suppressor gene to stop working. Using the car analogy, what has happened?

  1. The accelerator has jammed down
  2. The brake cable has been cut
  3. The engine has been replaced
  4. The car has run out of fuel

Tumour suppressors stop division, so losing one removes a brake. A proto-oncogene mutation is the jammed accelerator: a signal to divide that is stuck on.

A mutation leaves a proto-oncogene switched permanently on. Using the same car analogy, what has happened?

  1. The brake cable has been cut
  2. The handbrake has been left on, so the cell is stuck in G0
  3. The accelerator has jammed down
  4. The warning light has been disconnected, so faults go unnoticed

The two gene types fail in opposite directions: a proto-oncogene gains a signal it should never be sending, while a tumour suppressor loses one it should always send. Both end in the same place — a cell dividing without permission.

Why does chemotherapy commonly cause hair loss and nausea?

  1. The drugs are toxic to all cells equally
  2. Cancer cells release chemicals that cause these effects
  3. The drugs target dividing cells, and hair follicles and gut lining divide often
  4. The drugs damage the nervous system directly

These drugs discriminate by mitotic index rather than by being cancerous. Healthy tissues with a high mitotic index — hair follicles, gut lining, bone marrow — are hit alongside the tumour.

The same chemotherapy drugs leave a patient's mature neurons largely untouched. Why?

  1. Neurons run stronger checkpoints than other cells, so they resist the damage
  2. Neurons carry a different set of genes from the tissues that are damaged
  3. The drugs act only on cells that already carry mutations
  4. Mature neurons have been sitting in G0 since childhood, so there is nothing for the drug to interrupt

One rule predicts both the damage and the safety. Drugs that attack dividing cells sort tissues by mitotic index, so the tissue that divides least is the tissue least affected — and it is the same reason cancers are common in skin and gut lining but rare in neurons.

Where this goes next

Cancer is a failure of control over when a cell divides. There is a second kind of control that this unit has assumed without explaining, and it is just as remarkable: control over what a cell becomes.

Every cell in the tumours above carried the same genome as every healthy cell around them. Every cell in your body does. And yet a neuron, a red blood cell and a skin cell are so different that you would not guess they were related, let alone identical in their DNA. Chapter 4.4 explains how that is possible — and the answer will turn out to be the same kind of answer as this chapter's: not a difference in the instructions, but a difference in which instructions are being followed.
