Unit 1 — Biochemistry & Homeostasis

1.8 The Molecules of Life

Everything you are made of came out of your mouth as food. This chapter finds out what that food is made of, and then follows the six carbon atoms of a single glucose molecule all the way into a fat and into an amino acid.

You have been using these words since 1.3 without ever being told what they are. Starch was cut into maltose and then glucose. Protein was cut into peptides and then amino acids. Fat was emulsified and then split into fatty acids and glycerol. Chapter 1.7's energy-balance model quietly assumed that some of that could become part of you.

This chapter is where those assumptions get cashed in.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to

  • Describe the structure and function of carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids.
  • Explain how monomers are joined into polymers and taken apart again, and balance the equations.
  • Identify each food type using the standard chemical tests, and use the results as evidence.
  • Track the carbon atoms of a glucose molecule into a fatty acid and into an amino acid, and say where the nitrogen comes from.
  • Describe the flows of energy and matter into, out of and within a body.

Four families, one design idea

Almost everything in a living thing that is not water is one of four kinds of molecule. They do completely different jobs, but they are all built the same way: a small unit, repeated. The small unit is a monomer; the long chain built from them is a polymer; a very large polymer is a macromolecule.

A little more on each

Carbohydrates. The monomer is a monosaccharide — glucose, fructose or galactose, all with the formula C₆H₁₂O₆ and different arrangements. Two joined make a disaccharide: glucose + glucose is maltose, glucose + fructose is sucrose, glucose + galactose is lactose. Many joined make a polysaccharide: starch (how plants store glucose), glycogen (how you store it) and cellulose (what plant cell walls are built from). Starch and cellulose are both long chains of glucose; the only difference is the orientation of the link between units — and that tiny difference is why you can digest one and not the other.

Lipids. A triglyceride is one glycerol molecule with three fatty acids attached. Fatty acids are long hydrocarbon chains, and this is why fat carries more than twice the energy per gram of anything else: it is almost entirely carbon and hydrogen, with very little oxygen, so there is far more still available to be oxidised. In a saturated fat every carbon in the chain carries as many hydrogens as it can, so the chains are straight, pack together tightly and are usually solid at room temperature. An unsaturated fat has one or more double bonds between carbons, each of which puts a kink in the chain, so the molecules cannot pack closely and the fat is usually liquid. Shape decides state, exactly as it decided everything else in this unit.

Proteins. The monomer is an amino acid, and there are twenty of them. Every one has the same backbone — an amino group at one end, a carboxyl group at the other — and a different side group in the middle, and it is the side groups that give a protein its properties. A chain of amino acids folds into one specific three-dimensional shape determined by its sequence, and that shape is the function. This is why heat and extreme pH destroy an enzyme: they do not break the chain, they unfold it, and an unfolded enzyme has lost the shape that was doing the work.

Nucleic acids. DNA and RNA, built from nucleotides, and the subject of Unit 5. All that matters here is that they are the fourth family and that they need phosphorus and nitrogen.

Joining and splitting: two reactions, and they are opposites

Monomers are joined by removing a water molecule between them — a condensation reaction. They are separated by adding one back — hydrolysis, which is exactly what every digestive enzyme in Chapter 1.3 was doing. Check the atoms:

Do the check in the third column yourself once, on any row. It takes thirty seconds and it establishes the habit this whole chapter runs on: every atom on the left has to appear on the right. Matter is not created, destroyed or converted from one element into another by any chemical reaction. If an atom seems to have vanished from an equation, you have made a mistake — not discovered an exception.

"Enzymes break molecules apart" is only half the story. The word hydrolysis means splitting with water, and that is literal: a water molecule is consumed at every bond broken. Digesting a large starch molecule with a thousand glucose units uses up 999 water molecules. That is one of several reasons a body needs so much water, and it is invisible in a diagram that just draws an arrow.

Finding out what is in something

Four standard tests distinguish the food types. Each one relies on a chemical change you can see.

TestReagent and methodPositive resultDetects
Benedict'sAdd Benedict's solution and heat in a water bathBlue → green → yellow → orange → brick red, depending on how much is presentReducing sugars — glucose, fructose, maltose, lactose
IodineAdd a drop of iodine solution. No heatingOrange-brown → blue-blackStarch

Benedict's is the only one of the four that tells you how much as well as whether: the colour runs through a sequence as the concentration rises, so it is semi-quantitative. Note also what it does not detect. Sucrose is a sugar and gives a negative Benedict's result, because it is not a reducing sugar. A negative result never means "no sugar" — it means "no reducing sugar", and knowing the difference between those two statements is a large part of using evidence properly.

A claim built on a single negative result is much weaker than a claim built on a positive one, and saying so in your conclusion is a strength rather than a hedge.

Following the atoms

Now the question that HS-LS1-6 actually asks, and it is a better question than it looks: how do carbon, hydrogen and oxygen from a sugar molecule combine with other elements to make amino acids and other large carbon-based molecules?

Put it more concretely. You ate a bowl of rice, which is starch, which is glucose. A week later some of those atoms are in your muscle, which is protein. Muscle contains nitrogen. Glucose does not. So something else had to arrive — and working out what, and from where, is the whole of this section.

Where the nitrogen comes from

Nowhere in your body can make a nitrogen atom, and nowhere in your body can use the nitrogen gas you breathe. Nitrogen is about 78 per cent of every breath and you exhale essentially all of it, because N₂ is held together by a triple bond that no animal has an enzyme to break. Only certain bacteria can do it — and every nitrogen atom in you was pulled out of the air by one of them, then passed up through plants and animals into your food.

Inside your body, nitrogen is not made. It is moved. An enzyme transfers an amino group from one molecule to another in a reaction called transamination, usually via glutamate, which acts as a kind of nitrogen currency. Pyruvate receives an amino group and becomes alanine. That is the whole trick, and it is why eating protein matters even when your energy intake is fine.

Your body can build the carbon skeletons of eleven of the twenty amino acids from sugar. It cannot build the other nine, at all, from anything. Those nine are called essential amino acids — not because they matter more, but because they have to arrive already made.

That is what "you are what you eat" actually means, stated precisely. Everything you are made of is either rearranged food, or food that arrived in the right shape and stayed that way.

Energy and matter, into and out of a body

Chapter 1.7 modelled a body as a box with energy going in and out. Now that you know what the molecules are, that model can be stated properly — and it needs two separate accounts, because energy and matter are not the same thing and they do not behave the same way.

MatterEnergy
Comes in asFood, water, oxygenChemical energy in the bonds of food molecules
Inside, it isRearranged — taken apart and rebuilt into your moleculesTransferred from one form to another, and stored temporarily in ATP
Leaves asCarbon dioxide, water, urea, faecesMovement, and ultimately heat
Is it conserved?Yes — every atom is accounted forYes — but it becomes less useful each time it is transferred
Does it cycle?Yes — the atoms go round and round the biosphereNo — it flows one way and leaves as heat

The whole set of chemical reactions doing this is your metabolism, and it divides into two halves that run at the same time in the same cell:

  • Catabolism — breaking large molecules into small ones. These reactions release energy, and a reaction that releases energy is called exergonic. Digesting starch and breaking down glucose are catabolic.
  • Anabolism — building large molecules from small ones. These reactions require energy, and a reaction that requires energy is called endergonic. Building glycogen, fat, protein and DNA is anabolic.

The two are coupled through ATP, which acts as a rechargeable battery: catabolic reactions charge it, anabolic reactions spend it. A cell is not choosing between the two — it is doing both continuously, and the balance between them is what "metabolic rate" measures.

Here is the thought that ties this unit together. Every kilogram you have ever gained is rearranged food. Every kilogram you have ever lost left, mostly, as carbon dioxide through your lungs — a fat molecule broken down turns into CO₂ and water, and you breathe most of its mass away.

The energy is a different story. It did not accumulate and it did not leave with the atoms. It flowed through you, was used to do work, and ended up as heat — which is why a room full of people warms up, and why you are, in the most literal sense, a 100-watt heater that happens to be alive.

The process that releases the energy in glucose is cellular respiration, and it is the whole of Unit 2. This chapter deliberately stops at the bookkeeping — what goes in, what comes out, and that both are conserved. Unit 2 takes the machinery apart.

Where this goes next

Go back to the question the unit opened with. A child with cholera is given a sachet of salt and sugar in clean water. You can now answer every part of it.

The glucose is a monosaccharide, C₆H₁₂O₆ — small, polar, unable to dissolve through a membrane, so it needs a transport protein. That protein is SGLT1, and it carries glucose and sodium together or not at all. Moving sodium out of the gut makes the fluid behind the epithelium hypertonic. Water follows by osmosis through aquaporins, which carry water and nothing else. The absorbing surface is enormous because it is folded three times over, and it needs to be, because nine litres a day pass across it. And the reason the child was dying was that a feedback system holding water and salt in range had been overwhelmed.

That is Chapters 1.1 to 1.8 in a single paragraph, and there is not a single fact in it that stands alone.

Unit 2 picks up the one question this unit deliberately left open: what actually happens inside a mitochondrion when that glucose is finally broken down, where the energy goes, and how plants put it into the glucose in the first place.

Check your understanding

Fat contains about 9 kcal per gram; carbohydrate and protein about 4. Using the composition of the molecules, explain why — and say why a body stores surplus energy as fat rather than as glycogen.

Releasing energy from a food molecule means oxidising it — combining its carbon and hydrogen with oxygen. The more of a molecule is already combined with oxygen, the less energy is left to release.

A carbohydrate is roughly one oxygen for every carbon: glucose is C₆H₁₂O₆. It is already substantially oxidised. A fatty acid is almost entirely carbon and hydrogen with just two oxygens at one end — palmitic acid is C₁₆H₃₂O₂. Sixteen carbons and only two oxygens, so there is far more still available to be oxidised, and correspondingly more energy per gram.

The storage answer adds a second reason. Glycogen is stored with water bound to it — roughly three grams of water for every gram of glycogen — while fat is stored almost dry. So a gram of usable glycogen store weighs several times what its energy content suggests. Combining both effects, storing energy as fat is many times more mass-efficient, which matters a great deal for an animal that has to carry its own stores around.

A student eats a bowl of rice. Two days later, some of the carbon atoms from that rice are part of a protein in their bicep. Explain how, and identify the one thing in that protein that did not come from the rice.

Rice is mostly starch, a polymer of glucose. In the gut, amylase and then maltase hydrolyse it to glucose, which is absorbed through SGLT1 into the blood and taken to the liver and then to the muscle. Inside a cell, glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is split into two molecules of pyruvate (C₃H₄O₃), each keeping three of the original six carbons.

Pyruvate is one amino group away from the amino acid alanine. An enzyme transfers an amino group onto it from another amino acid — usually glutamate — in a reaction called transamination. Written out and balanced: C₃H₄O₃ + NH₃ + 2[H] → C₃H₇NO₂ + H₂O. All three carbons of the alanine came from half a glucose molecule that came from the rice. That alanine is then joined into a growing polypeptide chain by condensation, and the chain folds into a muscle protein.

The one thing that did not come from the rice is the nitrogen. Glucose contains only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. The nitrogen came from protein in something else the student ate, whose amino group was passed along; and before that, from a plant or animal that got it from a nitrogen-fixing bacterium. It could not have come from the nitrogen gas they breathed, because humans cannot break the triple bond in N₂.

Someone loses 5 kg over several months. Where did the mass physically go? Answer in terms of matter and of energy separately.

Matter. The stored fat was broken down and oxidised. A triglyceride is almost entirely carbon and hydrogen, so when it is oxidised the products are carbon dioxide and water. Most of the mass left through the lungs as CO₂ on the breath; the rest left as water, in urine, sweat and exhaled water vapour. Nothing was destroyed and nothing was converted into energy — every atom that made up those 5 kg is still somewhere, mostly now in the atmosphere.

Energy. Different account entirely. The chemical energy that had been stored in the bonds of those fat molecules was released as the bonds were broken and re-formed with oxygen. Some was captured in ATP and used to do work — muscle contraction, active transport, building molecules — and every one of those transfers ended, sooner or later, as heat lost to the surroundings. The energy did not accumulate anywhere and it did not leave with the atoms. It flowed through the body and left as heat.

That is the difference the crosscutting concept is pointing at: matter cycles, energy flows one way.

Quick check

These mark themselves, and point you back to the right section when an answer goes wrong.

Digesting a starch molecule made of 1000 glucose units requires 999 water molecules. Why?

  1. The water is needed to dissolve the pieces once they are cut off
  2. The water is used up by the enzyme, which is destroyed each time
  3. Every bond broken consumes a water molecule — that is what hydrolysis means, and it is literal
  4. Water is not really needed; it just happens to be present in the gut

"Enzymes break molecules apart" is only half the story. Hydrolysis means splitting with water, and the water is a reactant. It is one of several reasons a body needs so much of it — and it is completely invisible in a diagram that just draws an arrow.

Joining 500 amino acids into a single polypeptide chain releases 499 water molecules. Where does that water come from?

  1. From amino acids broken down to supply it, which is why 500 go in and 499 come out
  2. From the two monomers at each join — a condensation reaction removes the atoms of a water molecule to make the bond
  3. From the enzyme, which gives up a water molecule each time it forms a bond
  4. From the cytoplasm, which is drawn into the chain as it grows

Condensation and hydrolysis are one equation read in opposite directions, so the same counting works for both. Count bonds, not units: 500 monomers make 499 bonds, and each bond costs or yields exactly one water molecule.

Butter is solid at room temperature and olive oil is liquid. Both are fats. What explains the difference?

  1. Double bonds put kinks in unsaturated chains, so the molecules cannot pack closely together
  2. Butter is built from much longer fatty acid chains
  3. Butter uses a different backbone molecule instead of glycerol
  4. Olive oil contains more water, which keeps it liquid

In a saturated fat every carbon carries as many hydrogens as it can, so the chains are straight and stack tightly. One double bond is enough to bend the chain and stop that stacking. Shape decides state — which is the same principle that has decided everything else in this unit.

A fat sample taken out of a cupboard is liquid at room temperature. What can you infer about its fatty acid chains?

  1. That every carbon in them carries as many hydrogens as it can
  2. That fewer than three of them are attached to the glycerol
  3. That at least some of them carry double bonds between carbons, kinking the chains so the molecules cannot pack closely
  4. That they contain an element a solid fat does not

The words saturated and unsaturated describe hydrogen, but what you observe is packing. Straight chains stack like pencils in a box; one double bond bends a chain and the stack falls apart. State is read off shape, and shape is what this unit has been about from the first enzyme onwards.

A sample of sucrose solution gives a negative Benedict's result. What can you conclude?

  1. The sample contains no sugar
  2. The test must have been done wrong, since sucrose is a sugar
  3. The sample contains sugar, but only a very small amount
  4. The sample contains no reducing sugar — which is a different statement from containing no sugar

Knowing the difference between those two statements is a large part of using evidence properly. Benedict's detects glucose, fructose, maltose and lactose. Sucrose gives a clean negative, and it is still a sugar.

A glucose solution gives a negative iodine result. What can you conclude?

  1. That no starch is present — which is a different statement from saying no carbohydrate is present
  2. That the sample contains no carbohydrate
  3. That starch is present but too dilute for the test to show it
  4. That the iodine solution must have gone off and needs replacing

Each of the four tests names one thing and sees only that. Iodine finds starch, Benedict's finds reducing sugars, Biuret finds peptide bonds. To show that the glucose is there you need a different test altogether — Benedict's, which on this sample would run all the way to brick red.

In Figure 1.32 the bacon sample gives a pale protein well, even though bacon contains plenty of protein. What is the best conclusion?

  1. That bacon does not in fact contain protein
  2. That the result is inconclusive — the protein may be below the detection limit, or the test may not have worked
  3. That the Biuret reagent must have been faulty
  4. That the fat in bacon prevented the protein from reacting

A negative result has at least three possible causes: the substance is genuinely absent; it is present but below the detection limit; or the test did not work. That is why the water control is on the tray — it tells you what a negative looks like on this equipment, on this day. A claim built on a single negative is much weaker than one built on a positive, and saying so is a strength rather than a hedge.

One of the five tiles in Figure 1.32 is labelled "water" and holds nothing else. What is it doing there?

  1. Diluting the reagents to the right strength before they are used
  2. Confirming that each reagent can still produce a positive result
  3. Showing what a negative looks like on this equipment on this day, so a faint colour is not read as a weak positive
  4. Providing the standard against which the other colours are measured

A control fixes the meaning of every other reading on the tray. A tile rinsed and not quite dried, or a reagent that has discoloured slightly with age, will produce a tint in every well — and without something known to compare it against, that tint gets written down as a result.

You eat rice and two days later some of its carbon is in a muscle protein. Muscle contains nitrogen; rice starch does not. Where did the nitrogen come from?

  1. From the nitrogen gas breathed in, which is 78 per cent of air
  2. The body made it, by converting carbon atoms into nitrogen
  3. From protein in something else that was eaten — its amino group was transferred onto the carbon skeleton
  4. From the water drunk with the meal

Inside your body nitrogen is never made — only moved, by transamination, usually via glutamate acting as a kind of nitrogen currency. Pyruvate receives an amino group and becomes alanine. Every nitrogen atom in you was pulled out of the air by a bacterium and passed up through plants and animals into your food.

A diet supplies plenty of starch and no protein whatsoever. Why can the body not simply build the amino acids it needs?

  1. Because building an amino acid is anabolic, and there is no energy available to pay for it
  2. Because starch supplies only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen — the amino group has to be transferred in from an amino acid that is already there
  3. Because only nine of the twenty amino acids can be built from sugar in the first place
  4. Because nitrogen from the air cannot be used until it has dissolved into the blood

Two limits sit on top of each other. The carbon skeletons of eleven amino acids can be built from sugar, and even those are unfinished until an amino group is moved onto them; the other nine have to arrive already made. That is what "you are what you eat" means once it is stated precisely.

Someone loses 5 kg of fat over several months. Where did the mass physically go?

  1. Out through the lungs, mostly as carbon dioxide, with the rest leaving as water
  2. It was converted into energy, so the atoms no longer exist
  3. It left in the faeces, as undigested material
  4. It was sweated out through the skin

A triglyceride is almost entirely carbon and hydrogen, so oxidising it gives carbon dioxide and water. You breathe most of the mass away. Every kilogram you have ever gained is rearranged food; every kilogram lost left, mostly, on your breath.

Someone gains 4 kg of muscle over a year of training. Where did that mass physically come from?

  1. From the energy in their food, converted into matter and laid down as tissue
  2. Almost entirely from water held in the new muscle
  3. From the air breathed in during training, which is mostly nitrogen and oxygen
  4. From the atoms of food, rearranged into their own molecules — with the nitrogen coming from protein they ate

Matter has to balance in both directions. Mass gained is food atoms rebuilt into you; mass lost is those same atoms leaving as carbon dioxide, water and urea. Energy does neither — it flowed through, did some work, and left as heat.

Matter cycles round the biosphere; energy does not. Why the difference?

  1. Because energy is destroyed when it is used, while matter is not
  2. Because only matter is conserved; energy is not
  3. Because atoms are used up by the body and have to be replaced
  4. Because energy becomes less useful at every transfer and finally leaves as heat, while atoms are simply rearranged and used again

Catabolism releases energy and charges ATP; anabolism spends it. Every one of those transfers ends, sooner or later, as heat lost to the surroundings — which is why a room full of people warms up, and why you are, quite literally, a 100-watt heater that happens to be alive.

Over one day a body takes in 2000 kcal of chemical energy and ends the day at exactly the same mass. What happened to that energy?

  1. It was transferred into work and finally left as heat — which is why a room full of people warms up
  2. Most of it was destroyed as the body used it up
  3. It was stored in ATP, which is why the mass stayed the same
  4. It was returned to the environment to be taken up and used again

ATP is a rechargeable battery, not a tank: a cell holds only seconds' worth of it and recharges it continuously. That is the reason nothing accumulates. Energy entered as chemical bonds, was spent on contraction, transport and building, and left the body at a slightly higher temperature than it arrived.
