Unit 3 — Ecosystem Dynamics

3.6 Ecological Succession

Chapter 3.5 left ecosystems in every state of ruin and repair — whales climbing back, a park settling into something new, an island grazed to bare rock. What it never explained is how anything rebuilds. That process has a name, it runs in a recognisable order, and it is the last learning target in this unit.

Your learning-target sheet says: "I can explain the process of ecological succession and analyze the factors that cause succession." The word "succession" does not appear anywhere else in your anchor guide or your class slides. If you sat through every lesson and this feels new, that is because it is — and it is still on the list. This chapter was written to close that gap, and it is a full chapter, not a summary.

What succession is

Ecological succession is the gradual, predictable change in the community of species living in an area over time, following a disturbance or the appearance of new ground.

Three words there are doing work.

  • Community, not population. Succession is not one species growing — it is one set of species being replaced by another set, then another. The whole third ring of Figure 3.2 changes.
  • Predictable. Not identical every time, but recognisable. Bare ground anywhere on Earth is colonised in a comparable order, for reasons you can work out from first principles.
  • Gradual. Decades to millennia, which is why it has to be studied by comparing places of different ages rather than by watching one place. Ecologists call that a chronosequence — a row of sites at different stages, read as if it were a film.

The engine of the whole process is one idea, and if you take a single sentence from this chapter it should be this one:

Each community changes the conditions of the site, and the new conditions suit somebody else better than they suit the community that made them.

A lichen breaks down rock and makes soil — and soil lets a grass grow, and the grass smothers the lichen. Grasses build deeper soil that holds water — and shrubs use it, and shade the grasses out. Every stage builds the ramp that the next stage climbs, and is then displaced from the top of it. Ecologists call this facilitation, and it is why succession has a direction at all rather than being a random shuffle.

Succession starts when there is bare or cleared ground, which happens in two very different ways — and the difference between them is the main thing you are assessed on.

Primary and secondary succession

Primary succession starts on ground where there has never been soil — a cooled lava flow, a new volcanic island, rock exposed by a retreating glacier, a landslide scar down to bedrock. No soil, no seeds, no roots, no organisms of any kind.

Secondary succession starts on ground where the community has been destroyed but the soil survived — after a forest fire, a flood, a clear-fell, or the abandonment of a farm. It looks devastated and it is not: below the surface there is a bank of dormant seeds, living root systems, fungi, and the fertility built up over centuries.

The test is not how violent the disturbance looked. It is whether the soil survived.

A wildfire that incinerates a forest is secondary succession, because the soil and the seed bank are still there under the ash. A glacier quietly withdrawing a few metres a year leaves primary succession behind it, because what it exposes is scraped, sterile rock. Students lose marks on this constantly by classifying on drama rather than on substrate.

Why does making soil take so long? Because soil is not one thing. It is broken-down rock plus dead organisms, and both parts have to be manufactured on site:

  1. Weathering. Water gets into cracks, freezes, expands and splits the rock. Temperature swings crack it further.
  2. Biological attack. Lichens — a fungus and an alga living as one organism — grip bare rock and release weak acids that dissolve minerals out of it. They need no soil because they make their own foothold, which is why they are almost always first.
  3. Organic matter. Every lichen and moss that dies adds material that holds water and nutrients. This is the part that turns rock grit into something a root can live in.
  4. Nitrogen. Rock contains no usable nitrogen at all, and no plant can build protein without it. Primary succession usually depends on a nitrogen-fixing partner — bacteria in the roots of plants like mountain avens or alder — that pulls nitrogen out of the air.

All four are slow. Estimates for producing a single centimetre of new soil from rock run from a hundred to several hundred years depending on climate. That is the entire reason primary succession is measured in centuries and secondary succession in decades — and secondary succession simply skips it.

The organisms that arrive first, on either road, are called pioneer species. They share a recognisable set of traits, all of which follow from the job:

  • Tolerant of extremes — full sun, no shelter, wide temperature swings, thin or absent soil.
  • Excellent dispersal — tiny wind-blown spores or seeds, or seeds carried by birds and sea currents, because they have to reach the site before they can colonise it.
  • Fast reproduction, short life. Speed matters when the ground is empty.
  • Poor competitors. Every one of the traits above is a trade-off, and it is why pioneers are always the first to be displaced.

The evidence: three islands and a field

Succession is a claim about what happens over centuries, which raises an obvious problem — how would anyone know? Four lines of evidence, and it is worth seeing that they are genuinely independent of one another.

Krakatau, 1883 — an accidental experiment

The eruption of Krakatau in the Sunda Strait destroyed most of the island and buried what remained under tens of metres of hot ash. Everything living on it was killed. Because the eruption was one of the most famous events of the nineteenth century, botanists went repeatedly to see what came back, and they counted.

Read the order rather than just the numbers. First blue-green algae and ferns, whose spores are microscopic and travel enormous distances on the wind. Then grasses and beach plants with seeds that float in seawater. Then, once there was shade and soil, the trees — most of them with fleshy fruits, because by that stage birds and bats were arriving and bringing seeds in their guts. The order of arrival tracks how far and how easily a species could travel, which is exactly what the pioneer-traits list predicts.

Surtsey, 1963 — an experiment watched from the start

Surtsey rose out of the sea south of Iceland in a submarine eruption in 1963. This time scientists were there from the beginning, and Iceland closed the island to everyone except a small number of researchers — so it is the cleanest record of primary succession anywhere.

The interesting part of that graph is the bend. For twenty years the island gained species slowly. Then in 1985 gulls began nesting on the southern part of the island, and the rate of colonisation jumped. Seabird droppings are concentrated nitrogen and phosphorus — precisely what thin new volcanic soil lacks — and the gulls also carry seeds in on their feet and in their guts.

Two things follow. First, succession is not just plants arriving in a queue: animals are participants, and they can accelerate the whole process. Second, this is facilitation caught in the act. The gulls came because there was enough vegetation to nest in, and their arrival made much more vegetation possible.

Mount St Helens, 1980 — both kinds at once

The eruption of Mount St Helens is the best teaching example there is, because it produced primary and secondary succession side by side, a few kilometres apart.

  • The pumice plain north of the crater was buried under metres of sterile volcanic debris. No soil, no seed bank. Primary succession — and the pioneer that mattered most was the prairie lupine, a nitrogen fixer, which appeared within a year or two and began building fertility in patches that everything else then colonised.
  • The blowdown zone further out had every tree flattened by the lateral blast, but the soil beneath was intact and, crucially, much of it was still under late-spring snowpack when the mountain exploded. Secondary succession — and plants were coming up through the fallen trunks in the first summer.

Forty years on, the difference between the two zones is still visible from the air. Same eruption, same day, same climate, two completely different recovery rates — and the only variable is whether the soil survived.

Abandoned farmland — succession you can find near a town

The most-studied secondary succession anywhere is old-field succession in the Piedmont of the south-eastern United States, where cotton farms were abandoned in large numbers in the early twentieth century and ecologists have been tracking the fields ever since. The sequence is reliable enough to date a field by looking at it: annual weeds in year one, perennial grasses by year three or four, pine woodland by twenty, and an oak–hickory forest by a century.

The pines are the part worth understanding. They colonise the grassland and grow into a closed forest — and then they stop replacing themselves, because pine seedlings need full sun and there is now a pine canopy in the way. Oak and hickory seedlings, which tolerate shade, grow up underneath and inherit the site. The pines are removed by the conditions they created. It is exactly the mechanism from the alders and the spruce at Glacier Bay, in a different continent and a different climate — and that repeatability is what makes succession a general process rather than a collection of stories.

Seral stages, climax communities, and an honest caveat

The intermediate communities a site passes through have a name: each is a seral stage, and the whole sequence is a sere. The apparently stable community at the end is the climax community.

What "climax" means needs care, because the everyday sense of the word is misleading:

  • It does not mean nothing changes. Individual trees die and are replaced constantly; a gap opens and a small burst of succession runs inside it. What stops is directional change — the overall mix of species stays roughly the same.
  • It does not mean maximum diversity. Mid-succession is often the richest moment, because old and new species overlap. A closed conifer forest can hold fewer plant species than the shrubland it replaced.
  • It does not mean permanent. It means stable until the next disturbance — and in most real landscapes the next disturbance arrives long before the climax does.

The idea of a single inevitable endpoint is out of date, and you should know why.

The classical picture, from Frederic Clements in the early 1900s, treated a climax community as a destination: any site in a given climate would eventually arrive at one particular community, like an organism growing up. Later work showed this is too rigid. The same starting point can end up in different communities depending on which species happened to arrive first, on soil, slope and drainage, and on chance. Some systems never reach a climax at all because fire or flood resets them first — many grasslands and pine savannas are maintained permanently at an intermediate stage by regular burning.

Use "climax community" as a useful description of a relatively stable end state. Do not use it as a prediction of destiny. If an exam question asks you to evaluate the concept, that distinction is the answer.

Which brings the unit back to where Chapter 3.5 left it. Succession is the process by which a disturbed ecosystem can rebuild — and understanding it tells you exactly when it will not:

  • If the soil is gone, recovery drops from decades to centuries. Burn a slope hard enough that the soil washes off and you have converted secondary succession into primary succession.
  • If the seed source is gone, the species may never arrive. A cleared patch in the middle of a large forest recovers; the same patch a hundred kilometres from the nearest forest may not.
  • If the conditions themselves have changed, succession runs to a different endpoint. An invasive grass that burns every three years, a drier climate, a new grazing animal — any of these can hold a site permanently in a state the old community cannot re-enter.

That third case is HS-LS2-6, stated in one line: changing conditions may result in a new ecosystem. Succession is the mechanism behind both halves of the standard. In stable conditions it returns a disturbed site to something like what was there before, which is why ecosystems look resilient. When the conditions have moved, the same process runs to a different destination — and the ecosystem that comes back is genuinely a different one.

Quick check

Pines colonise an abandoned field, close into a forest, and then stop replacing themselves. Oaks take over. Why?

  1. The pines exhaust the soil, so nothing pine can grow there again
  2. Pines stop producing seed once they are fully grown
  3. Pine seedlings need full sun, and the pines' own canopy now shades the ground — while oak seedlings tolerate shade
  4. Oaks release a chemical that kills pine seedlings

This is the engine of the whole process, and it is worth being able to state in one line: each community changes the conditions of the site, and the new conditions suit somebody else better than they suit the community that made them. Ecologists call it facilitation, and it is why succession has a direction instead of being a random shuffle.

Lichens spread over a bare lava flow for decades. Grasses then appear, and within a few more decades the lichens have gone from the site. What happened?

  1. The lichens built the soil the grasses needed, and the grasses then took the light and space the lichens depended on
  2. The lichens stripped the last nutrients out of the rock and starved themselves out
  3. The climate shifted to one that suits grasses better than lichens
  4. The lichens reached the end of their natural lifespan together

Facilitation is the reason succession has a direction at all. A community that improves its site is not rewarded for it — it is displaced by whatever the improved site suits better, which is almost never itself.

A wildfire incinerates a forest. A glacier quietly withdraws a few metres. Which one leaves primary succession behind it?

  1. The fire, because the destruction was total
  2. The glacier, because what it exposes is scraped, sterile rock with no soil
  3. Both — anything that destroys a community starts primary succession
  4. Neither — in both cases the soil survives

The test is not how violent the disturbance looked. It is whether the soil survived. Students lose marks on this constantly by classifying on drama rather than on substrate — and Mount St Helens produced both kinds a few kilometres apart on the same morning.

A flood tears the vegetation out of a river valley but leaves the ground itself in place. On the same valley side, a landslide shears away everything down to bedrock. Which one begins primary succession?

  1. The flood, because moving water carried the whole community away
  2. Both, because each one stripped every plant from the site
  3. The landslide, because what it leaves behind is bare rock with no soil, seed bank or roots
  4. Neither, because both patches will grow back to the same community

Classify on substrate, not on how dramatic the event looked. The question to ask of any disturbance is a narrow one: is there soil left, and is there anything alive in it?

Pioneer species are almost always displaced by the species that follow them. Why?

  1. They use up the site's nutrients and then die out
  2. The animals that arrive later eat them
  3. Their lives are too short for the population to persist
  4. The traits that let them colonise bare ground — tolerance, speed, tiny wind-blown seeds — come at the cost of being poor competitors

Every pioneer trait is a trade-off. You cannot be the organism that survives full sun on bare rock and the organism that wins a fight for light under a closed canopy. So a pioneer builds the conditions in which it loses — that is a mechanism, not bad luck.

The first plant on Surtsey was a sea rocket, washed ashore as a seed. Why could an oak — a large, shade-tolerant, competitive tree — never have taken that role?

  1. There is not enough light on new volcanic ground for a tree to grow
  2. Its heavy seeds travel badly and its seedlings cannot take full sun, no shelter and almost no soil — it is built to compete, not to colonise
  3. Trees grow too slowly ever to establish on a new island
  4. Trees need nitrogen and pioneer species do not

Pioneer traits are not a superior design — they are the set of compromises that suit an empty, hostile site. Read the list the other way round and it tells you which species are excluded from the opening stage, and why.

On a secondary-succession track, the soil-depth line barely moves. Why is that the most important fact on the graph?

  1. The soil was never lost, so the slowest step of all — making soil out of rock — is skipped entirely
  2. It shows the soil is too thin for the site ever to reach a climax community
  3. It shows the disturbance was mild compared with a real catastrophe
  4. It shows soil depth has little effect once plants have arrived

Making a single centimetre of soil from bare rock takes somewhere between a hundred and several hundred years, depending on climate. That one step is the entire reason primary succession is measured in centuries and secondary succession in decades.

On a primary-succession track, the soil-depth line starts at zero and creeps upward for centuries. What does that line tell you?

  1. Nothing much happens for the first few centuries, and then succession starts
  2. The track will pass through completely different stages from a secondary one
  3. The site will finish at a richer climax community than a secondary track does
  4. Every later stage is waiting on soil the site is still manufacturing, and a single centimetre of it takes a hundred years or more

Soil is not one substance but two jobs done at once — rock broken down, and dead organisms added. Neither can be imported, which is why the line on a primary track is a rate limit rather than a measurement.

Colonisation on Surtsey was slow for twenty years, then jumped after 1985 when gulls began nesting. What does that show?

  1. The lava had finally cooled enough for plants to grow
  2. The gulls removed plants that were competing with the colonisers
  3. Animals take part in succession — droppings supply the nitrogen and phosphorus new volcanic soil lacks, and gulls carry seeds in
  4. The earlier surveys had missed species that were already there

This is facilitation caught in the act, and it runs both ways: the gulls came because there was finally enough vegetation to nest in, and their arrival made far more vegetation possible. Rock contains no usable nitrogen at all, and no plant builds protein without it.

On Rakata, wind and sea delivered species from the first year, but birds and bats delivered almost nothing at first and steadily more towards 1934. Why the delay on that route?

  1. Fleshy-fruited trees arrive only in an animal's gut, and the animals themselves could not settle until there was vegetation to live in
  2. Birds and bats could not cross that much open sea until later in the century
  3. Early botanists recorded only wind-blown and sea-borne arrivals
  4. The island's soil was too poor to support any large tree until the 1930s

A dispersal route is only as fast as the thing doing the dispersing. Wind and currents were there from the start; the animal route had to be built by succession before succession could use it.

A closed conifer forest holds fewer plant species than the shrubland it replaced. Has succession gone backwards?

  1. Yes — species richness always rises through succession
  2. Yes — the forest must have been disturbed at some point
  3. No — succession has no effect on diversity either way
  4. No — mid-succession is often the richest stage, because old and new species overlap there

"Climax" does not mean maximum diversity, and it does not mean nothing is happening — trees die and small bursts of succession run in the gaps they leave. What stops is directional change: the overall mix of species stays roughly the same.

An old tree falls in a mature forest and sun-loving, fast-growing species spring up in the gap. Does that mean the forest was not a climax community?

  1. Yes — in a true climax community nothing changes
  2. Yes — the gap resets that patch to an earlier seral stage
  3. No — what stops at the climax is directional change; gaps open and close while the overall mix of species stays roughly the same
  4. No — the gap species belong to the climax community, so no succession is happening

Stability at the community level is built out of constant turnover at the level of individuals. A forest that looks unchanged for two centuries has replaced nearly every tree in it.

"Any site in a given climate will eventually arrive at one particular climax community." Evaluate that claim.

  1. Correct — that is what a climax community means
  2. Too rigid — which species arrive first, plus soil, slope and chance, can lead the same starting point to different endpoints
  3. Correct, provided no disturbance ever interrupts it
  4. Wrong — succession never stops, so there is no such thing as a climax community

Some systems never reach a climax at all: many grasslands and pine savannas are held permanently at an intermediate stage by regular burning. Use "climax community" as a description of a relatively stable end state, not as a prediction of destiny — and if an exam asks you to evaluate the concept, that distinction is the answer.

Many grasslands and pine savannas burn every few years and never develop into forest. What does that tell you about the climax concept?

  1. The soil and climate of those places cannot support trees at all
  2. Those grasslands are themselves the climax community for that climate
  3. Succession cannot take place anywhere fire is frequent
  4. Succession does not always run to completion — regular disturbance can hold a site permanently at an intermediate stage, so a climax is not a destiny

In most real landscapes the next disturbance arrives long before the climax does. That makes the intermediate stages the normal condition of an ecosystem and the climax the special case, which is close to the reverse of how the term sounds.
