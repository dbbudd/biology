Unit 2 — Energy Flows & Matter Cycles

2.7 Energy Flows: Food Webs and Pyramids

Nothing new happens in this chapter. Photosynthesis still stores energy in sugar; respiration still releases it and loses some as heat. All that changes is the scale: instead of one cell we look at a whole ecosystem, and we ask what is left after the same two processes have run in one organism after another. The answer explains why predators are rare.

The same two processes, at the scale of a landscape

Here is the unit title drawn as a whole ecosystem. Compare it with the chloroplast-and-mitochondrion loop from 2.5 and you should recognise the shape immediately.

Three roles hold the diagram together, and each is defined by where its energy and carbon come from:

  • Producer — makes its own organic molecules from small inorganic ones, using light. Plants, algae, some bacteria. Everything else on the diagram is ultimately eating them.
  • Consumer — gets organic molecules by eating other organisms. Herbivores are primary consumers; things that eat herbivores are secondary; and so on.
  • Decomposer — gets organic molecules from dead matter, by secreting digestive enzymes into it and absorbing the products. Fungi and bacteria, mostly.

Decomposers are usually drawn as an afterthought and they are the most important box on the page. Think about a hundred-year-old forest: every autumn it drops a thick layer of leaves, and every year animals die in it. Walk into that forest and there is no hundred-year pile. Something ate it — and respired it, and breathed the carbon back into the air.

Without decomposers the carbon cycle stops within a season. Every nutrient in the ecosystem would end up locked in a pile of dead material and the producers would starve.

Chains, levels, and which way the arrows point

A food chain is a sequence of who eats whom. The position an organism holds in it is its trophic level — trophic simply means to do with feeding.

The arrows point the way the food goes, not the way the eating goes. Grass → grasshopper means the grasshopper eats the grass. Almost everyone draws it backwards at least once, usually reasoning "the grasshopper attacks the grass so the arrow points at the grass".

Read every arrow as "is eaten by", or better, "energy and matter flow this way". Once you read it that way the whole diagram becomes a map of where the energy is going, which is what it is for.

Not every chain starts with a living plant. There is a second kind, and it is bigger than the first in most ecosystems: dead organic matter → decomposers → animals that eat decomposers → and up from there. You do not notice it because it is small, brown and mostly underground. The majority of the energy captured by a forest's producers goes down that route rather than up through the herbivores.

Real ecosystems are not chains, though. They are food webs — every organism eating several things and being eaten by several others. And webs contain organisms that will not sit in a single band. An animal that eats from more than one trophic level is a trophic omnivore: a grizzly bear eating both berries and elk; a raven eating both carrion and small mammals. Any animal that eats a trophic omnivore is one too.

A trophic cascade is what you just built: a change in a predator population having knock-on effects two or more links away.

Wolves never eat willow. But more wolves means fewer elk, and fewer elk means less browsing, and less browsing means more willow. Two negatives make a positive, and the wolf population and the willow growth move in the same direction — through an animal that connects them.

This is why removing one species from an ecosystem so often has consequences nobody predicted. The effects do not stop at the neighbours.

A caution about this famous story. The elk–willow–beaver chain is well documented, and beaver colonies really did decline when the wolves went and recover when they returned. Some popular retellings go much further and claim wolves changed the courses of rivers. That overstates the evidence considerably, and other factors — climate, bears, human hunting outside the park — were at work too. A good scientific claim is proportionate to what the data can carry.

Where the other ninety per cent goes

Now the central question of the chapter. An elk eats a kilogram of willow. Nothing like a kilogram of elk results. Where does the rest go?

Three places, and none of them is mysterious:

  1. Not eaten. Elk do not eat every plant, and predators do not catch every elk or eat every part of the ones they catch. What is missed becomes dead organic matter and goes to the decomposers.
  2. Not digested. Some of what is eaten passes straight through and leaves as faeces. Cellulose is especially hard work; even a ruminant with a rumen full of helpful microbes cannot get all of it.
  3. Respired. This is much the biggest share. An elk spends most of what it digests simply staying alive — walking, keeping warm, pumping blood, running from wolves. Every joule spent that way ends as heat, and heat leaves the ecosystem.

Only what is left over builds new elk. Ecologists have names for this:

  • Gross rate of biomass production — everything a producer makes by photosynthesis, before it respires any of it.
  • Net rate of biomass production — what is left after respiration; the biomass the organism actually accumulates.

Only the net figure is available as food for the level above. That distinction is worth holding onto: a rainforest photosynthesises enormously and accumulates rather little, because a mature forest respires almost as much as it fixes.

This is also the honest answer to why you cannot escape the second law by eating more efficiently. The losses are not waste in the sense of bad management. Respiring is what being alive consists of, and respiring produces heat, and heat is gone. An elk that did not respire would not be a more efficient elk; it would be a dead one.

Draw the pyramid — to scale

Because the loss happens at every step, the amount of energy available shrinks fast as you go up. Drawn as a stack of bands with widths proportional to the energy, you get an energy pyramid.

The usual estimate is that about ten per cent of the energy at one level ends up in the level above. Work that through from a million kilocalories and see what it does to the picture — and then test whether the rule survives contact with real forest data.

A pyramid printed with equal bands and shrinking numbers is contradicting itself, and it is the picture students remember rather than the numbers. Drawn honestly, the top band is almost invisible — and that is the answer to "why are there so few wolves".

Chain length is limited by arithmetic, not by biology. Start with 1,000,000 kcal captured by producers and apply the ten per cent estimate three times and you are down to 1,000 kcal — one part in a thousand. A fifth level would be down to 100 kcal, which will not support an animal of any size over any useful area.

That is why food chains almost never run to more than four or five links, anywhere on Earth, in any habitat. There is nothing left to eat.

Two consequences: the ten per cent rule is only a rule of thumb, and steak is expensive

The third tab of the pyramid sim uses real measurements from a New Hampshire forest, and they do not obey the rule. Producers accumulated 1000 g/m²/year; primary consumers plus decomposers together accumulated 200. That is 20%, not 10%. The step above that, to secondary consumers, works out at 15%.

So the rule is an average across many ecosystems rather than a law, and in a real forest it can be out by a factor of two. What survives is the shape of the argument, and the shape is what matters: the fraction is well under 100%, it applies again at every step, and so the numbers collapse quickly no matter what the exact figure is.

The same caution applies to counting animals. The rule would suggest ten elk per wolf in Yellowstone; in practice there have been roughly a hundred. That is not a failure of the rule. The rule is about energy, not head-count — a wolf is far bigger than the ten per cent of an elk that the rule allows it, wolves are not the only thing eating elk, and they do not consume every scrap of what they kill.

The ecological cost of a steak

Now put the arithmetic to work on something you decide about every day.

Imagine two communities of the same size consuming the same amount of biomass. Community R eats only rice. Community S eats only beef, from cattle fed on grain.

Community R eats at the producer level: one step from sunlight. Community S eats at the primary consumer level: the grain is grown, cattle eat it, and roughly ninety per cent of that energy is lost to cattle respiration, faeces and uneaten parts before anyone sits down to eat.

To feed the same number of people the same amount of biomass, community S needs on the order of ten times as much land as community R.

That single fact drives an enormous amount of environmental argument — about land use, deforestation, water, and greenhouse gas emissions. You do not have to draw any particular conclusion from it. But it is not an opinion, and it is not complicated: it is the pyramid you just drew, applied to lunch.

Real agriculture complicates this in ways worth knowing. Much grazing land cannot grow crops at all, so cattle there are not competing with rice. Different animals convert at very different efficiencies — chicken and farmed fish are several times better than beef. And the ten per cent figure is, as you have just seen, a rough one. The direction of the effect, though, is not in doubt.

Quick check

Auto-marked. Get one wrong and it will point you back to the section that covers it.

A hundred-year-old forest drops a thick layer of leaves every autumn, yet there is no hundred-year pile on the floor. What does that tell you?

  1. The leaves blow away and accumulate somewhere else
  2. The forest photosynthesises the fallen leaves back into the trees
  3. Decomposers have eaten the dead material and respired most of its carbon back into the air
  4. The leaves decay into soil minerals, and no carbon is released

Decomposers are usually drawn as an afterthought and they are the most important box on the diagram. Without them every nutrient in the ecosystem ends up locked in a pile of dead material and the producers starve within a season.

Suppose every fungus and bacterium in a forest stopped decomposing tomorrow. What becomes of the producers?

  1. They are unaffected — trees take their carbon from the air, not from the litter
  2. They grow faster, because the thickening layer of litter shelters their roots
  3. They carry on until the incoming sunlight runs short, since energy is the thing an ecosystem must be supplied with
  4. They starve within a season or so, as every nutrient in the ecosystem ends up locked in dead material

The blue arrows in Figure 2.23 form a closed loop only because decomposers close it. Take that box out and the matter stops returning — which is why an ecosystem needs no supply of atoms from outside, but absolutely needs the organisms that recycle the ones it already has.

In the chain grass → grasshopper → mouse, what does each arrow mean?

  1. "Is eaten by" — the arrow points the way the energy and matter travel
  2. "Eats" — the grasshopper attacks the grass, so the arrow points at the grass
  3. "Lives near" — the arrows link organisms sharing a habitat
  4. "Competes with" — the arrow shows which organism wins

Get the arrows the wrong way round and the diagram stops meaning anything, because its whole purpose is to show the direction energy and matter move. Read every arrow as "energy and matter flow this way" and it fixes itself.

A student draws snake ← hawk in a food-web diagram, to show that the hawk hunts the snake. What is wrong with it?

  1. Nothing — the arrow correctly identifies which of the two is the predator
  2. The two are at the same trophic level, so no arrow belongs between them at all
  3. It should be snake → hawk, because the arrow points the way the matter and energy travel
  4. It needs arrowheads at both ends, because energy passes in both directions

Almost everyone reverses an arrow at least once, usually by reasoning about who attacks whom. Read each one as "is eaten by" and the diagram turns back into the thing it was drawn to be — a map of where the energy is going.

Wolves never eat willow, yet willow grew back when wolves returned to Yellowstone. How?

  1. Wolves fertilise the soil with the remains of their kills
  2. More wolves means fewer elk, fewer elk means less browsing, and less browsing means more willow
  3. Wolves and willows both do better in cold years, so they rise together
  4. Wolves changed the courses of the rivers, which watered the willows

A trophic cascade: two negatives making a positive across two links. This is why removing one species so often has consequences nobody predicted — the effects do not stop at the neighbours.

Wolves take few beavers, and beavers take no wolves at all — yet beaver colonies declined after the wolves were removed from Yellowstone. What is the documented route?

  1. No wolves meant more elk, more elk meant heavier browsing, and willow is what beavers build with and feed on
  2. Beavers and wolves competed for the same prey, so removing the wolves left the beavers more to eat
  3. Removing the wolves changed the courses of the rivers, and the beavers lost their dam sites
  4. The same hunters who removed the wolves trapped out the beavers alongside them

A cascade is counted in links, not in feeding relationships — wolf to elk to willow to beaver is three, and no two neighbours in that list need be predator and prey for the effect to travel along it. This is why the consequences of removing a species so rarely stop at its neighbours.

An elk eats a kilogram of willow, and nothing like a kilogram of elk results. Where does the rest go?

  1. It is destroyed — that is what the ninety per cent loss means
  2. It passes straight up to the wolf that eats the elk
  3. It is stored in the elk's body for later use
  4. Some passes through undigested as faeces, and much the biggest share is respired and lost as heat

These losses are not bad management. Respiring is what being alive consists of, respiring produces heat, and heat leaves. An elk that did not respire would not be a more efficient elk; it would be a dead one.

A mature rainforest photosynthesises more than almost any ecosystem on Earth, yet it accumulates very little new biomass each year. Why?

  1. Its producers capture far less light than the figures suggest, because the canopy shades itself
  2. It respires almost as much as it fixes, so its net rate of biomass production is small
  3. Herbivores eat nearly all of it before any of it can accumulate
  4. Biomass cannot accumulate in a mature ecosystem, because energy is conserved

Gross is what photosynthesis makes; net is what survives the plant's own respiration, and only the net figure is ever food for the level above. That is how a colossal rate of capture sits beside a tiny rate of accumulation without any contradiction.

A pyramid is printed with four equal-width bands labelled 1,000,000 / 100,000 / 10,000 / 1,000 kcal. Why is that drawing contradicting itself?

  1. The numbers should increase upwards, not decrease
  2. The numbers should all be equal, since energy is conserved
  3. The widths should be proportional to the numbers — drawn honestly, the top band would be almost invisible
  4. There should be five bands, because food chains run to five links

The picture is what students remember, not the numbers beside it — so a picture that disagrees with its own numbers teaches the wrong thing. Drawn to scale, the top band nearly disappears, and that is the answer to "why are there so few wolves".

A student draws a pyramid to scale by making each band half the width of the one below it. What has gone wrong?

  1. Nothing — narrowing by half at each step is what a pyramid shape means
  2. The bands should be equal, because the ten per cent estimate is only a rule of thumb
  3. It should narrow a hundredfold at each step, since energy is lost twice over — to respiration and to faeces
  4. Each band should be about a tenth of the one below, so the drawing makes the losses look far smaller than they are

A pyramid drawn to scale is an argument rather than a decoration, and the widths are what carry it — the picture is the part anyone remembers. At a tenth per step the fourth band is a thousandth of the base, and all but vanishes.

Why do food chains almost never run to more than four or five links, anywhere on Earth?

  1. Predators large enough to eat a top predator do not exist
  2. Only about a tenth of the energy passes on at each step, so by the fifth level there is almost nothing left to eat
  3. Animals that high up would have nowhere to drink
  4. Longer chains exist but have not been discovered yet

Chain length is limited by arithmetic rather than by biology. Apply the ten per cent estimate three times to a million kilocalories and you are down to a thousand — and there is nothing left for anyone above that.

An ecosystem's producers capture 2,000,000 kcal/m²/year. Using the ten per cent estimate, roughly how much is available to a tertiary consumer?

  1. 200,000 kcal — ten per cent of what the producers captured
  2. 20,000 kcal — the estimate applies twice, once for each consumer level above the herbivores
  3. 2,000 kcal — the estimate applies at all three transfers, leaving one part in a thousand
  4. 1,000,000 kcal — half of it, with the rest respired away

Applying a tenth three times is the same arithmetic that makes the top of a drawn pyramid so thin. Run it once more and a fourth consumer level is down to 200 kcal, which will not keep an animal of any size alive over any useful area.

Real forest data gave a transfer of 20% from producers to the level above, not 10%. Does that make the ten per cent rule useless?

  1. No — it is an average across many ecosystems, and what matters is that the fraction is small and applies again at every step
  2. Yes — a rule that is out by a factor of two is worthless
  3. No — the forest measurements must have been wrong
  4. Yes — it shows energy is not really lost between levels

The same caution applies to counting animals rather than joules. The rule would predict ten elk per wolf; in practice Yellowstone has had roughly a hundred. That is not a failure of the rule — the rule is about energy, and a wolf is far bigger than a tenth of an elk.

Chicken and farmed fish convert feed several times more efficiently than beef cattle. Does that undo the land argument for eating lower down?

  1. Yes — if conversion efficiency varies that much, the pyramid does not apply to farming at all
  2. No — a better converter still sits a level up, so it still takes more land than eating the crop directly
  3. No — chicken and farmed fish are producers, so they sit outside the argument
  4. Yes — it shows the comparison depends on the animal chosen, so no general claim survives

Every number in this section is approximate and the conclusion outlives all of them, because it depends only on the transferred fraction being well under 100%. That much grazing land cannot grow crops at all is a real qualification — but it changes where the argument bites, not whether it holds.
