Unit 4 — Reproduction

4.5 Halving the Deck: Meiosis

Everything in this unit so far has been about making exact copies. This chapter is about the one division in your body that deliberately does not — a division that produces four cells, each with half a set of chromosomes and none of them identical to anything. Without it, sexual reproduction is arithmetically impossible.

Why something has to halve

Start with a problem that has nothing to do with biology and everything to do with counting.

You have 46 chromosomes in every body cell. So does each of your parents. If a sperm and an egg each carried a full set of 46, the zygote would have 92. That zygote would grow into a person with 92 chromosomes in every cell, whose children would have 184, whose children would have 368. Four generations and the number is unmanageable; in twenty it is astronomical.

Since this obviously does not happen, one of two things must be true: either fertilisation throws half the chromosomes away, or gametes are made with half a set in the first place. It is the second, and the division that does it is called meiosis.

This gives you the cleanest possible statement of what meiosis is for, and it is worth being able to say without hesitating:

Meiosis exists so that fertilisation can happen without doubling the chromosome number every generation.

Everything else about meiosis — the two divisions, the pairing, the shuffling — is machinery in service of that requirement, plus one enormous bonus that Chapter 4.6 is about.

Haploid, diploid, and the loop a human life runs on

Two words carry this chapter, and both are counts of sets, not of chromosomes.

A diploid cell has two complete sets of chromosomes — one inherited from each parent — so its chromosomes come in homologous pairs. It is written 2n. In humans 2n = 46, arranged as 23 pairs.

A haploid cell has one complete set: one chromosome from each pair, no pairs at all. It is written n. In humans n = 23.

Haploid does not mean "half the DNA". It means "one set". The difference matters enormously, and it is the single most common place people lose marks in this unit.

A cell that has just finished meiosis I is haploid — 23 chromosomes, one from each pair — but each of those chromosomes is still two sister chromatids, so the cell contains the same amount of DNA as an unreplicated diploid cell. Ploidy counts sets of chromosomes. It says nothing about how many chromatids each chromosome is made of. Keep the two counts separate and this chapter is straightforward.

Meiosis happens in very few places. In humans, only in the gonads — the testes and the ovaries — and only in the particular cell lineage that makes gametes. Every other cell in your body, in every other tissue, only ever divides by mitosis. That is why a mutation in a skin cell cannot be inherited and a mutation in a cell of the testis or ovary can, which is a point Unit 5 made and this chapter now explains.

What 23 pairs actually look like

A karyotype is a photograph of all the chromosomes from one cell, arrested at metaphase when they are most condensed, cut out and arranged in order of size with the homologous partners placed side by side.

Before you read one, build one. The karyotype below starts half-finished: the 23 chromosomes that came from the egg, in pink, are already in place, and the 23 from the sperm, in blue, are jumbled underneath at the same scale. Pair each blue chromosome with its partner. Partners are the same length and have their centromere, the narrow waist, in the same place. Drag a chromosome, or tap it and then tap its slot. When every pair is complete, the finished karyotype appears.

That gives you the two categories, and the difference between them is worth taking seriously.

  • Pairs 1 to 22 are autosomes. Both members of each pair are the same size and carry the same genes in the same order. Everybody has them, in the same 22 pairs.
  • Pair 23 are the sex chromosomes. A person with two X chromosomes is chromosomally female; a person with an X and a Y is chromosomally male. The Y is much smaller than the X and carries far fewer genes — but among them is SRY, which starts development down the male pathway.

Every gamete you produce gets one chromosome from each of the 23 positions — one of pair 1, one of pair 2, and so on down to one sex chromosome. That is what "haploid" means in practice, and it is the sentence that Chapter 4.6 turns into a very large number.

Notice the consequence for the sex chromosomes: every egg carries an X, because the person making it has only X chromosomes to give. A sperm carries either an X or a Y. So it is the sperm that determines the chromosomal sex of the zygote, and it does so with a probability of very nearly one half.

The three things that make meiosis different

Meiosis uses the same stage names as mitosis — prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase — because the machinery is largely the same. Three things are different, and every consequence in this unit comes from one of them.

1. There are two divisions, and the DNA is only copied once

Interphase happens once, before meiosis I. Then the cell divides twice with no copying in between. That is the arithmetic of halving: one round of copying and two rounds of separating, so a cell that started with 46 chromosomes ends up with four cells of 23.

Say it as a chain and it is easy to remember: copy once, divide twice. Mitosis copies once and divides once, so the number stays the same.

2. In prophase I, homologous chromosomes pair up

This is the step mitosis never takes, and it is the origin of almost everything interesting about meiosis. Each chromosome finds its homologous partner and they lie alongside each other along their entire length — a process called synapsis. A paired-up homologous pair is four chromatids lying together and is called a tetrad.

While they are pressed together, matching segments break and swap between non-sister chromatids. This is crossing over.

Crossing over usually happens once or twice per chromosome pair, every time meiosis runs. It is not a rare event or an accident — it is a normal, required part of the process, and cells that fail to do it often fail to separate their chromosomes correctly.

3. Anaphase I separates pairs, not sisters

In mitotic anaphase, the centromeres split and sister chromatids are pulled apart. In anaphase I the centromeres do not split. Whole chromosomes — each still two sister chromatids — are pulled apart from their homologous partners.

That is why meiosis I is called the reduction division: it is the division that takes the cell from diploid to haploid, because each daughter receives one member of each pair rather than one copy of each chromosome. Meiosis II then does what mitosis does — splits sisters — but starting from a haploid cell, so the number does not fall again.

If you remember one line from this chapter, make it this one:

Meiosis I separates homologous pairs. Meiosis II separates sister chromatids.

Almost every question about meiosis can be answered by working out which of those two divisions is being asked about.

Mitosis and meiosis, side by side

Your learning target asks you to distinguish these two by purpose, number of divisions and the characteristics of the resulting cells — so the table is organised that way rather than as a list of stages.

The two rows that carry the most weight are the last two. Everything above them is machinery; those two are what the machinery is for.

"Meiosis is just mitosis twice" is wrong, and it is wrong in a specific way. Meiosis II is essentially mitosis — sisters separate, nothing pairs up. Meiosis I is not mitosis at all: homologues pair, they cross over, and they separate as whole chromosomes. If someone can only tell you about one of the two divisions, it is almost always meiosis I they have missed, and meiosis I is the one that does the actual work.

Check your understanding

Why must gametes be haploid?

  1. Because they are smaller than body cells
  2. Because half the genes are not needed
  3. Because two of them fuse, so a full set in each would double the chromosome number every generation
  4. Because they do not need to divide again

It is an arithmetic requirement. Fertilisation adds two gametes together, so each must carry half a set for the total to come out at the species number.

A species has 2n = 12. Suppose its gametes were made without any halving, so each carried 12 chromosomes. What would follow?

  1. The two gametes would be unable to fuse at fertilisation
  2. Every generation would carry twice the chromosome number of the one before — 24, then 48, then 96
  3. The zygote would discard the spare set, so the number would stay at 12
  4. The number would stay at 12, because the zygote only uses one set

A species number stays constant because two steps change it in opposite directions and cancel exactly: meiosis halves it, fertilisation doubles it. Remove either one and the count runs away within a few generations.

A human cell has just completed meiosis I. How many chromosomes and chromatids does each daughter cell contain?

  1. 46 chromosomes, 92 chromatids
  2. 23 chromosomes, 46 chromatids
  3. 23 chromosomes, 23 chromatids
  4. 46 chromosomes, 46 chromatids

Meiosis I separates homologous pairs, so each cell gets one chromosome from each of the 23 pairs. Sister chromatids have not separated yet, so each of those 23 chromosomes is still two chromatids. The cell is haploid and holds 46 chromatids at the same time.

An organism has 2n = 8. One of its cells has finished meiosis I and is now part-way through meiosis II, with its chromosomes lined up on the equator. How many chromosomes and chromatids does that cell contain?

  1. 8 chromosomes, 16 chromatids
  2. 8 chromosomes, 8 chromatids
  3. 4 chromosomes, 4 chromatids
  4. 4 chromosomes, 8 chromatids

Run the two counts separately and the stage falls out on its own. The chromosome count halves once, at anaphase I, and never again. The chromatid count halves once, at anaphase II, and never before it.

What is separated during anaphase I?

  1. Homologous chromosomes
  2. Sister chromatids
  3. Individual chromatids from different pairs
  4. The two daughter cells

Anaphase I pulls homologous pairs apart while sister chromatids stay joined. It is anaphase II — and mitotic anaphase — that separates sisters.

During meiosis, at which point do sister chromatids finally come apart from each other?

  1. Anaphase I only
  2. Both anaphase I and anaphase II
  3. Anaphase II only
  4. Prophase I, while the homologues are paired

Meiosis II does what mitosis does — split the centromeres and separate sisters. The difference is only that it starts from a haploid cell, so the chromosome number does not fall a second time.

What happens in prophase I that never happens in mitosis?

  1. Chromosomes condense
  2. The nuclear membrane breaks down
  3. Spindle fibres form
  4. Homologous chromosomes pair up and exchange segments

The first three happen in both. Synapsis and crossing over happen only in meiosis, and they are the origin of most of the genetic variation this unit is about.

A dividing cell is viewed under a microscope and tetrads are visible — groups of four chromatids lying alongside one another. What can you conclude?

  1. The cell is in meiosis I, because homologues lie alongside their partners in no other division
  2. The cell is in mitosis, since every mitotic chromosome is also two chromatids
  3. The cell is in meiosis II, since both divisions pair chromosomes up
  4. The cell has copied its DNA twice, which is why there are four chromatids

Synapsis is the one thing visible down a microscope that identifies meiosis I with certainty. Condensing, spindle fibres and a dissolving nuclear membrane would all look the same in either division.

Which statement about human sex chromosomes is correct?

  1. Eggs carry either an X or a Y with equal probability
  2. Every egg carries an X; a sperm carries either an X or a Y
  3. The Y chromosome is larger than the X
  4. Sex chromosomes are pair 22

A person producing eggs has only X chromosomes to pass on, so every egg gets one. The sperm supplies either an X or a Y, which is why the sperm determines chromosomal sex. The sex chromosomes are pair 23, and the Y is much the smaller of the two.

Which statement about the autosomes on a human karyotype is correct?

  1. They are numbered in exact order of size, 1 down to 22
  2. Each one appears as a single strand, because the cell has not yet copied its DNA
  3. The centromere sits at the midpoint of every one of them
  4. Each pair holds two chromosomes of matching size that carry the same genes in the same order

Matching a karyotype is done on two features alone: total length, and where along that length the centromere sits. Everything the 22 autosome pairs have in common follows from both members of a pair being copies of the same chromosome, one from each parent.

One diploid cell undergoes meiosis. What is produced?

  1. Two identical diploid cells
  2. Two different haploid cells
  3. Four genetically different haploid cells
  4. Four identical haploid cells

Copy once, divide twice, so four cells. Each has one chromosome from each pair, so haploid. And crossing over plus the random orientation of each pair means no two are the same — which is Chapter 4.6.

A body cell of an organism with 2n = 12 divides by mitosis. What is produced?

  1. Two cells of 12 chromosomes each, genetically identical to the parent cell
  2. Two cells of 6 chromosomes each
  3. Four cells of 12 chromosomes each
  4. Two cells of 12 chromosomes each, genetically different from one another

Purpose sets everything else. Mitosis exists to replace a cell with a copy of itself, so one division and no pairing is all it needs. Meiosis exists to make gametes, which is why it takes two divisions and a round of crossing over to get there.

Where this goes next

This chapter has justified meiosis entirely on arithmetic: something has to halve, and this is how. On that argument alone, meiosis could have been a simple sorting process that put one chromosome from each pair into each gamete and left it at that.

It is not simple. It pairs the homologues up first and lets them exchange pieces. It chooses which member of each pair goes to which pole at random, independently, twenty-three times over. Those two features are not needed for the halving at all — and they are the reason you are not identical to your brother or sister.

Chapter 4.6 counts exactly how much variation they generate. The answer is a number most people find hard to believe, and it is the point where this unit connects to the whole of genetics.
