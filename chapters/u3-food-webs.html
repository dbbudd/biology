Unit 3 — Ecosystem Dynamics

3.4 Food Webs, Keystones and Cascades

Chapters 3.1 to 3.3 followed one population at a time. That was a simplification, and here is where it breaks: in Borneo in the 1950s, spraying insecticide to kill mosquitoes ended with the roofs of houses collapsing and rats in the grain stores. Nobody predicted it, because nobody had drawn the food web. This chapter is about drawing the food web.

Chains, webs and trophic levels

A food chain is a single line of who-eats-whom: grass → mouse → snake → hawk. It is easy to draw and it is almost always wrong, because nothing in nature eats only one thing.

A food web is what you get when you draw all the chains at once and let them cross.

Inside that web there are several chains. Grass → mouse → hawk is one. Wildflowers → rabbit → snake → hawk is another. Both are true at once.

Each organism sits at a trophic level — a position counted in steps from the sunlight:

LevelNameWhat it doesIn Figure 3.13
1ProducerMakes its own food from sunlight by photosynthesisgrass, wildflowers
2Primary consumerEats producers — a herbivorerabbit, mouse
3Secondary consumerEats primary consumerssnake; the hawk, when it eats a mouse
4Tertiary consumerEats secondary consumersthe hawk, when it eats the snake

Notice that the hawk appears twice. An animal that eats at more than one level does not have a single trophic level — it has an average one, and it changes with the season. This is normal, not a special case, and it is the first sign that a real web is more tangled than a chain suggests.

One group is missing from that table and from most food-web diagrams: decomposers — the fungi and bacteria that break down every organism in the web when it dies and return the nutrients to the soil. They are left off for space, not because they are optional. Nothing in this chapter works without them.

Why webs are shaped like a pyramid

Here is a question with a surprising answer: why is there no animal that eats hawks, and no animal that eats that? Why do food chains stop at four or five links, everywhere on Earth?

The answer is energy. When a rabbit eats grass, only a small fraction of the grass's energy ends up as rabbit. Most of it is spent on staying alive — moving, breathing, keeping warm, pumping ions — and leaves as heat. More is never absorbed at all and passes straight through. A useful rule of thumb is that roughly ten per cent of the energy at one trophic level becomes body mass at the next.

Follow the arithmetic and two facts fall out that you can use in almost any answer about food webs:

  • Chains are short. After four steps you have a thousandth of what you started with. A fifth-level predator would need such an enormous area to feed itself that it becomes impossible to be one. This is why top predators are rare, large-ranging, and the first thing to disappear when a habitat is broken up.
  • There must be far more prey than predators. That is why the lynx axis in Figure 3.10 ran to nine thousand while the hare axis ran to a hundred and sixty thousand. It is not a quirk of Canadian trapping — it is thermodynamics.

Put a number on it. To build 1 kg of hawk at the fourth level you need roughly 10 kg of snake, 100 kg of mouse, and 1,000 kg of plants. A single hawk represents about a tonne of grass, standing somewhere, growing all season.

Some things worth pulling out of the diagram, in case they are not obvious:

  • Competition is visible in the arrows. The harvester ants and the grasshopper both eat mesquite: that is interspecific competition, between species. Two grasshoppers eating the same brittlebush are in intraspecific competition, within a species — which you cannot see on this diagram at all, and which is usually the fiercer of the two, because members of the same species need exactly the same things.
  • Multiple paths make a web resilient. If the harvester ants vanished, the praying mantis would not starve — it also eats grasshoppers. Every alternative arrow is a piece of insurance.
  • But not every species has a spare. Look for the ones where a single arrow carries everything. Those are where the web is fragile, and the next section is about what happens when one of them is cut.

Competition, and why a web is more stable than a chain

Arrows in a food web show who eats whom. They also, indirectly, show who is competing — and competition is a limiting factor you met in Chapter 3.2 without seeing where it comes from.

Who is competingHow to spot it in Figure 3.15Why it matters
Intraspecific Members of the same species Invisible — a diagram of species cannot show it Usually the fiercer of the two, because two individuals of one species need exactly the same food, water, space and mates. This is the competition that produces the density-dependent regulation behind every S-curve.
Interspecific Different species Two consumers with arrows from the same producer, or two predators sharing a prey species Shapes which species can coexist at all, and where each of them lives. It is why removing one species can make a completely different one more abundant.

Now use that to answer a question that runs through the rest of the unit: why does a web survive damage better than a chain?

Take the chain brittlebush → grasshopper → praying mantis on its own. Remove the brittlebush and every organism above it starves. There is no alternative, because a chain has no alternatives — that is what makes it a chain.

Now put it back in the web of Figure 3.15. The grasshopper also eats mesquite and prickly pear. The praying mantis also eats harvester ants. Remove the brittlebush and the numbers wobble, but nothing collapses, because the energy simply flows along other arrows. Every extra connection is a piece of insurance.

This is where the first half of HS-LS2-6 comes from. The standard says complex interactions in ecosystems maintain relatively consistent numbers and types of organisms in stable conditions. The complexity is the mechanism: a densely connected web has many routes for energy, many alternatives for each consumer, and many density-dependent feedbacks pulling in different directions. All that redundancy is what makes an ecosystem look steady.

Which sets up the obvious follow-up question, and it is the one the rest of this chapter answers: are all the connections equally important?

Paine's crowbar, and the idea it produced

In 1963 a young ecologist named Robert Paine went down to a rocky shore at Mukkaw Bay on the coast of Washington State, marked out a stretch of tide pool, and began throwing starfish into the sea.

The starfish was Pisaster ochraceus, the ochre sea star, and it eats mussels. Paine removed every one from his experimental plot and kept removing them, every few weeks, for years. Alongside it he left an identical stretch of shore alone as a control. Then he counted how many species lived in each.

A citation correction worth making. Several versions of this figure in circulation — including the one on your class slides and in the anchor guide — date Paine's paper to 1996. Volume 100 of The American Naturalist was published in 1966. It is a thirty-year error on the founding paper of the whole keystone-species idea, and it matters for more than pedantry: 1966 places the work at the start of experimental field ecology, before anyone had established that you could test an ecosystem by breaking a piece of it. In 1996 it would be an unremarkable follow-up study. Check the date if you cite it.

What happened, and why. Without Pisaster eating them, the mussels grew, spread, and took over the rock. Mussels are extremely good competitors for space — they simply cover everything — and space on a rock is the limiting resource for barnacles, limpets, anemones, algae and chitons. One by one they were crowded out. Paine had not removed those species. He had removed the thing that was stopping the mussels from removing them.

Paine's conclusion was that some species have an influence on their community far out of proportion to their abundance. He called such a species a keystone species, borrowing an image from architecture.

A keystone species has an effect on its community that is disproportionately large compared with its abundance. Remove it and the structure of the community changes, often losing a great deal of diversity.

Two traps in that definition. First, keystone is not the same as top predator — some keystones are herbivores (the sea otter's kelp, elephants pushing over trees to keep savanna open) and some are plants. Second, keystone is not the same as abundant. Pisaster was never the commonest animal on that shore; that is precisely the point. The commonest species is often called a dominant species, which is a different idea entirely.

Hear it from the scientists themselves. In this short film from HHMI BioInteractive, Robert Paine describes the starfish experiment, and James Estes shows how sea otters do the same job in the kelp forests of the North Pacific — by eating the sea urchins that would otherwise strip the kelp bare. Watch for the moment the idea gets its name.

Now use the definition properly. Your guide asks you to identify a possible keystone species in the desert web of Figure 3.15 and back the claim with evidence and reasoning. A good answer looks like this:

Claim. The saguaro cactus is a keystone species in this web.

Evidence. In Figure 3.15, arrows run from the saguaro to the antelope squirrel and to the Gila woodpecker. In the real desert it does far more: the woodpecker excavates nest holes in the saguaro's trunk, and when it abandons them the elf owl moves in. The elf owl has no other nest site in this web.

Reasoning. Remove the saguaro and you do not only remove a food source. You remove the only nesting structure for two of the four secondary consumers and, through the elf owl, cut a path to a tertiary consumer. The saguaro is not the most numerous plant in the desert — brittlebush and mesquite far outnumber it — so its influence is out of all proportion to its abundance, which is the definition.

What would test it. Paine's method: find two comparable areas, remove saguaros from one, leave the other alone, and count species in both for several years. Note how much better that is than arguing from the diagram — and note that nobody would get permission to do it, which is why most keystone evidence comes from removals that happened by accident.

Quick check

A student draws an arrow from the hawk to the mouse, saying it shows that the hawk eats the mouse. What is wrong?

  1. Nothing — arrows in a web can point either way
  2. Hawks eat snakes, not mice
  3. The arrow should run mouse to hawk, because it follows the energy rather than pointing at the food
  4. It should have arrowheads at both ends

This trips people up constantly, because "the hawk eats the mouse" naturally points the other way in English. The arrow is not a sentence — it is a route for energy, and energy travels out of the eaten and into the eater.

Figure 3.13 draws an arrow running from the rabbit to the snake. What does that arrow claim?

  1. That the snake eats the rabbit — the arrow runs the way the energy travels
  2. That the rabbit eats the snake, since the arrow starts at the rabbit
  3. That the rabbit and the snake compete for the same food
  4. That the rabbit and the snake sit on the same trophic level

Every arrow in a web carries the same single piece of information, and it is carried by the direction alone. Read it out loud as "eaten by" rather than "eats" and the whole diagram stops flipping around.

A lake holds 50,000 kcal of algae. Using the ten-per-cent rule, roughly how much reaches a fish that eats the animals that eat the algae?

  1. 5,000 kcal
  2. 500 kcal
  3. 45,000 kcal
  4. 50 kcal

50,000 → 5,000 → 500. Two steps, two divisions by ten. Counting the steps carefully is where most of the marks are in this kind of question; the arithmetic is the easy part.

A hawk at the fourth trophic level puts on 8 kcal of body mass. Running the ten-per-cent rule backwards, roughly how much energy did the producers underneath it have to capture?

  1. 800 kcal
  2. 80 kcal
  3. 8,000 kcal
  4. 80,000 kcal

Going down the pyramid you multiply by ten at each step, because roughly nine tenths of what the lower level held never arrived. This is the arithmetic behind the tonne of grass standing somewhere to support one hawk.

In the Sonoran web, if the harvester ants disappeared, the praying mantis would probably survive. Why?

  1. It would start eating plants instead
  2. It reproduces fast enough to replace its losses
  3. It sits at the top of the web, so nothing below affects it
  4. It also eats grasshoppers, and every alternative arrow is a piece of insurance

That redundancy is why a web survives damage that would destroy a chain. Now go the other way and look for species in Figure 3.15 where a single arrow carries everything — those are where the web is fragile, and cutting one of those is what the rest of the chapter is about.

The praying mantis can lose the harvester ants because it has somewhere else to feed. Which of these consumers in Figure 3.15 has no such alternative?

  1. The kangaroo rat
  2. The Gila woodpecker — the saguaro is the only arrow reaching it
  3. The grasshopper
  4. The elf owl

Counting the arrows into a species is how you find where a web is thin. One arrow in means one thing standing between that species and starvation, and those are the threads worth worrying about before any of the others.

Which kind of competition cannot be seen anywhere on a food-web diagram, even though it is usually the fiercer of the two?

  1. Intraspecific — two members of one species need exactly the same things, and a diagram of species cannot show it
  2. Interspecific, because different species are drawn in different places
  3. Competition with decomposers, which are left off the diagram
  4. None — every kind of competition shows up as a shared arrow

Two grasshoppers on the same brittlebush need identical food, water, space and mates — nothing else in the desert overlaps with a grasshopper that exactly. This is the competition that produces the density-dependent regulation behind every S-curve in Chapter 3.2.

In Figure 3.15 both the elf owl and the diamondback have arrows reaching them from the kangaroo rat and from the grasshopper mouse. What does that pattern show?

  1. Intraspecific competition, since both are predators of the same kind
  2. That the diamondback preys on the elf owl
  3. That the kangaroo rat is a keystone species
  4. Interspecific competition — two predators drawing on the same prey are in competition, although no arrow joins them

Competition is never drawn in a food web. You infer it from arrows that share an end, which is why it is easy to read a web for a year and never notice it. It is also why removing one species can make a completely different one more abundant.

Remove the hawk, and the model refuses to say whether rabbit numbers go up or down. Why?

  1. Rabbits are not affected by hawks at all
  2. Removing a predator always increases its prey, so the model must be wrong
  3. Two effects pull opposite ways — one predator gone, but the snakes booming — and arrows cannot say which is bigger
  4. The web is too small, and a bigger one would give an answer

That refusal is a strength, not a limitation. Two opposing effects of unknown size cannot be settled by drawing arrows, and pretending otherwise is how confident predictions turn into Borneo. Somebody has to do the removal for real and count what happens — which is exactly what Paine did.

Take the snake out of the web in Figure 3.13. What happens to the mouse?

  1. It cannot be settled from the diagram — one predator is gone, but the hawk has lost a prey species and may take more mice
  2. It must rise, because one of its predators has been removed
  3. It must fall, because the hawk will now hunt mice much harder
  4. It must fall, because there is less grass to go round

An arrow records that a relationship exists. It records nothing about how strong it is, and two effects of unknown strength pointing opposite ways have no answer on paper. Somebody has to remove the snake and count the mice.

Pisaster was never the commonest animal on Paine's shore. Does that count against calling it a keystone species?

  1. Yes — a keystone species has to be the most abundant one present
  2. Yes — a keystone species has to be the top predator
  3. It depends — only predators can be keystone species
  4. No — the definition is an effect out of proportion to abundance, so being uncommon is the point

The commonest species has its own name — a dominant species — and it is a different idea entirely. Think of the arch: the keystone carries no more weight than any other block, and there is nothing special about the stone. Take it out and the rest fall, because it was holding them in position.

Brittlebush and mesquite far outnumber the saguaro in the Sonoran Desert. Which is the better candidate for a keystone species, and why?

  1. Brittlebush, because the most abundant producer supports the most consumers
  2. The saguaro, because it is uncommon and yet supplies the only nest sites for the Gila woodpecker and the elf owl
  3. Neither, because a plant cannot be a keystone species
  4. The diamondback, because it preys on five species and so touches most of the web

Abundance and influence are two different measurements, and a keystone is defined by the gap between them. The saguaro's influence is largely structural rather than nutritional — it is what the desert nests in, not only what it eats.

Paine left an identical stretch of shore untouched alongside his removal plot. What would have been unknowable without it?

  1. How many sea stars were there before he started
  2. Whether the species loss was caused by removing the sea stars, or by something affecting the whole coast at that time
  3. Whether mussels can outcompete barnacles for space
  4. How many years the experiment needed to run

Without a control, "half the species disappeared" is only a correlation with the removal — a storm, a warm year or a disease would produce the same result. The control makes the comparison fair, and it is why one experiment on twenty metres of rock is about as clean as ecology ever gets.

Suppose you could test the claim that the saguaro is a keystone species. Which design would let you say the saguaro caused any species loss you found?

  1. Count the species in one desert plot, remove the saguaros, and count again five years later
  2. Survey many desert sites and check whether those with more saguaros hold more species
  3. Remove the saguaros from one plot, leave a comparable plot untouched, and count species in both for several years
  4. Watch one untouched plot for several years and record every species that disappears

A control plot has to carry everything the experimental plot carries except the one thing you changed — same shore, same weather, same years. Which is also why most keystone evidence comes from removals that happened by accident: nobody would be given permission to clear the saguaros.
