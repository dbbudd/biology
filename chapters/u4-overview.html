Unit 4 — Reproduction

Unit 4 Overview

You were once a single cell, about a tenth of a millimetre across. Every neuron, red blood cell and bone cell you have now was built by that one cell dividing again and again, and by its descendants becoming different from one another. This unit explains how that happens, and how your body makes the cells that could start the process again.

Two sentences hold this unit together. They pull in opposite directions, and that tension is the whole subject:

To build a body, cells must copy themselves exactly.
To make the next generation, cells must do the opposite.

Chapters 4.1 to 4.4 are about copying, and about what goes wrong when the copying is not controlled. Chapters 4.5 to 4.8 are about the machinery that deliberately breaks the copy, and the loops that decide when to run it.

The thing this unit has to explain

Start with a fact that is easy to state and hard to explain.

You began as one cell, a zygote, formed when a sperm met an egg. An adult human body contains somewhere around 37 trillion cells. If a cell divides into two, and both of those divide, and so on, then getting from 1 to 37 trillion takes about 45 rounds of division, because 245 is roughly 35 trillion. That is only forty-five doublings.

The picture raises these questions, and the unit answers them in this order.

  • How does one cell become two identical cells? Not roughly identical: each new cell needs an exact copy of all its DNA, about six billion base pairs, three billion from each parent. That has to happen forty-five times over. See 4.1.
  • If every cell has the same DNA, why is a neuron not a red blood cell? The picture says "identical DNA" and "200 different cell types" in the same box. Both are true, and 4.4 explains how.
  • What happens if a cell divides when it should not? Forty-five doublings build a body. A few extra doublings in the wrong place make a tumour. See 4.3.
  • How do you get from 2n back to n? Every cell in your body has 46 chromosomes. So did the zygote. So the sperm and the egg must each have had 23. Somewhere, a division halved the set instead of copying it. See 4.5.
  • Why are you not identical to your siblings? You have the same two parents and draw on the same two sets of chromosomes. 4.6 explains why, and the answer is a much bigger number than most people expect.
  • Where does all this happen, and what decides when? See 4.7 and 4.8.

Write down your best answer to each question now. Rough answers are fine. Come back at the end of the unit and rewrite them.

  1. Your skin cells and your muscle cells contain exactly the same genes. What makes them different?
  2. A cut on your finger heals. A tumour does not stop. What is different about the cells?
  3. Your body makes millions of new cells every second without becoming a tumour. What stops it?
  4. You have 46 chromosomes and so does everyone else in your family. How can you all be different?
  5. Why do humans bother with two parents at all, when a bacterium manages perfectly well with none?

How the four standards fit together

This unit is built on four NGSS performance expectations. Each is a different thing you have to be able to do, and the unit builds them in order. The highlighted words show each skill.

  • HS-LS1-4: How does one cell become a whole body, by copying itself and then becoming different?
  • HS-LS1-2: How do the systems of a body work together to make, carry and support new life?
  • HS-LS3-2: Where does the variation between children of the same parents come from?
  • HS-LS1-3: How do hormone feedback loops keep a body steady, and how would you test that they do?

Look closely at HS-LS1-4: it says use a model, not create one. You are not asked to invent a diagram of mitosis. You are asked to take a model you have been given and use it to explain something: trace a consequence through it, say what a stage does, or predict what happens if a step is missed. Every interactive in this unit works that way. HS-LS1-3 is the one people forget. It asks for an investigation, and 4.8 covers designing one: variables, controls, a control group and a data table.

Two of these standards are about building and running a body: LS1-4 and LS1-2. One is about passing information to the next generation: LS3-2. One is about keeping conditions steady while all of that happens: LS1-3. The unit moves from the first group to the second in 4.5, when a cell divides in a way that does not produce a copy.

Learning targets for the unit

These are the unit's "I can" statements, grouped by standard. Use them as a checklist: can you do each one without notes? The number after each one is the chapter that teaches it.

Cell division and mitosis

  • I can name and describe the stages of mitosis.
  • I can identify cells in the various stages of mitosis.
  • I can describe controls of the cell cycle and its relationship to cancer.

Building and maintaining an organism

  • I can differentiate between sexual and asexual reproduction, and describe the benefits of each.
  • I can explain stem cells and the process of cell differentiation and specialization.
  • I can compare the structure and function of the human male and female reproductive systems.

Meiosis and new genetic combinations

  • I can differentiate mitosis and meiosis in terms of purpose, number of divisions, and characteristics of the resulting cells.
  • I can describe the ways in which new genetic combinations are created through meiosis.

Feedback and homeostasis

  • I can use a model to explain how negative feedback between hormones maintains homeostasis in the reproductive and growth systems.

What you will actually do

Each chapter has at least one interactive, and they are working models rather than illustrations. Each one asks you to commit to an answer before it explains anything. Because the result is calculated from the model, changing the model changes the result, and that is how you find out what each part does.

  • 4.1 Identify real dividing cells down a microscope, then count a whole field and work out how long each stage lasts.
  • 4.2 Run a clone population and a sexual population through the same four disasters, and watch the advantage flip.
  • 4.3 Switch off a checkpoint and follow the lineage until it becomes a tumour.
  • 4.4 Build six different cell types from one identical set of genes.
  • 4.5 Drive a cell through meiosis, predicting the chromosome and chromatid count at every stage.
  • 4.6 Count every possible gamete (all 8,388,608 of them), then break a division and read the karyotype.
  • 4.7 Label both systems, then account for why one process makes four gametes and the other makes one.
  • 4.8 Assemble a hormone feedback loop, then cut a link and predict what every hormone does.
