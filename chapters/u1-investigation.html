Unit 1 — Biochemistry & Homeostasis

1.7 Planning an Investigation

Chapter 1.6 made a great many claims about how bodies behave. This chapter is about the only thing that turns a claim into knowledge — and about how easy it is to produce data that looks convincing and means nothing.

From 1.6: breathing rate is controlled by a negative feedback loop that responds mainly to carbon dioxide. Exercise makes muscles produce more carbon dioxide, so the loop should push breathing rate up.

That is a prediction with a mechanism behind it, which is the best kind. It is still only a prediction, and this chapter is about the difference.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to

  • Name the independent, dependent and controlled variables in an investigation, and choose a control.
  • Decide how much data you need, and explain what repeats actually buy you.
  • Read error bars, and say when a difference between two means is worth believing.
  • Translate data from a graph into a table, and say what is lost and gained.
  • Distinguish correlation from causation on real data.
  • Use an energy-balance model to predict how diet and exercise change body mass — including why the change stops.

What an investigation actually is

An investigation is not "doing an experiment". It is a deliberate attempt to answer a question in a way that could have come out the other way. If your method cannot produce a result that would change your mind, you have not designed an investigation — you have designed a demonstration.

Everything else follows from that. The reason for controlling variables is that an uncontrolled one gives you a second possible explanation for your result. The reason for a control group is that a measurement means nothing without something to compare it to. The reason for repeats is that a single number tells you nothing about how much your numbers wobble.

The three kinds of variable

KindWhat it isHow many
Independent variableThe one thing you deliberately changeExactly one
Dependent variableThe thing you measure, which you think depends on the independent variableUsually one
Controlled variableAnything else that could plausibly affect the dependent variable, held the same across every testAs many as you can manage

A useful sentence for checking a design: "I am changing ___, measuring ___, and keeping ___ the same." If you cannot fill in the first blank with exactly one thing, the investigation cannot tell you what caused the result.

Listing a variable as "controlled" is not the same as controlling it, and listing irrelevant ones does not help. "We controlled the colour of the participants' shoes" is not rigour — nothing connects shoe colour to breathing rate, so there was nothing to control. A controlled variable is one that could have changed your result if you had let it vary. Ask "what would happen to my dependent variable if this changed?" If the answer is "nothing", leave it out.

The control

A control group is the condition in which the independent variable is absent. It is what your treated group is compared against. Two designs are both legitimate and the choice between them matters:

  • Each person is their own control. Measure everyone at rest first, then after exercise. Differences in fitness, lung size and age between people cancel out, because you are comparing each person with themselves.
  • A separate control group. One group exercises, another does not. Necessary when the first measurement would change the participant — but it means the two groups differ in every other way as well, so you need more people to see the effect through that variation.

Note what is not a control: another level of the independent variable. A group exercising for ten minutes instead of three is a second treatment, not a control.

Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a testable prediction with a reason attached. Compare these:

  • "Exercise affects breathing rate." — Testable, but so vague that almost any result confirms it.
  • "Breathing rate will increase with exercise." — Better: it specifies a direction and could be wrong.
  • "Breathing rate will increase with the duration of exercise, because working muscles produce more carbon dioxide, and the feedback loop controlling breathing responds to blood carbon dioxide." — Best. It predicts a direction, and it commits to a mechanism, so a result that goes the other way tells you something specific is wrong.

Now design one. Assign every variable a role, choose a control, choose how many people to measure — and commit before anything is marked.

Reading the data honestly

Error bars, and the only question worth asking of two bars

When you measure ten people you do not get ten identical numbers. Real measurements scatter, so a mean on its own hides the thing you most need to know: how much would this mean have moved if I had measured ten different people?

That is what an error bar shows. The bar most often drawn is one standard error either side of the mean, and the standard error is the standard deviation divided by the square root of the number of measurements. Two consequences follow immediately, and both matter:

  • More scatter in your data means longer error bars — you know your mean less precisely.
  • More repeats means shorter error bars — but only as the square root. Going from 4 measurements to 16 halves the error bar; going from 16 to 64 halves it again. Precision gets expensive fast.

The G10 mean is higher. But its error bar runs from roughly 50 to roughly 98, and the G9 error bar sits entirely inside that span. The bars overlap, so the difference is not convincing. A gap that size could easily appear by chance between two groups that are really the same, given how much the G10 scores vary.

The right conclusion is not "G10 is worse" and it is certainly not "G10 is better". It is: on this evidence you cannot tell, and the G10 group is far more variable than the G9 group — which is itself an interesting result and probably the more important one.

The single most useful question you can ask of any pair of bars with error bars on them: do the bars overlap?

If they clearly do not overlap, the difference is probably real. If they overlap substantially, you cannot claim a difference from this data. If they just barely touch, you need a proper statistical test rather than an eyeball. And in every case, "the difference is unlikely to be chance" is a completely different claim from "the difference was caused by what I changed" — a badly controlled experiment can produce beautifully separated error bars around the wrong cause.

Correlation is not causation

Here is real clinical data. The ACR test — albumin to creatinine ratio — measures how much protein is leaking into the urine, which indicates kidney damage. Three groups of ten patients were tested.

Group 129.729.527331.9

The clinical bands are: below 30 mg/g is normal, 30 to 300 is moderately increased, and above 300 is severely increased. So Group 1 is normal, Group 2 is moderately affected and Group 3 is severely affected, and the three groups are clearly different — those means are nowhere near each other relative to their spread.

Now be careful about what that does and does not establish.

  • It does establish that the three groups differ in kidney protein leakage, and that ACR separates them cleanly.
  • It does not establish what caused the difference, because we were not told what distinguishes the groups other than their results. If Group 3 turned out to be older, or to have had diabetes for longer, or to be taking a different medicine, any of those could be the real cause.
  • Look at the spread as well as the middle. Group 1's values cluster within 6 mg/g of each other; Group 2's span 270. A group with a tight spread and a group with a wide one are not just different in level — something different is going on in them.

Two variables show correlation when they change together. That is a statement about the data. Causation is a statement about the world, and it needs more than data — it needs a mechanism, and ideally an experiment where you changed the suspected cause yourself and watched the effect follow. Ice-cream sales and drowning deaths are strongly correlated; the cause of both is summer.

This is also the difference between the two designs at the start of this chapter. An experiment, where you set the independent variable, can support a causal claim. An observation of groups that already differ can only support a correlation, however clean the numbers look.

Manipulating variables: diet, exercise and body mass

Here is an investigation you cannot run in a lesson, because it takes three years. So model it instead — which is exactly what the crosscutting concept of Systems and System Models is for.

The system is a human body. The matter and energy going in is food; the energy going out is everything the body spends; and anything left over is stored, mostly as fat. That is the whole model:

Putting numbers on it

Your basal metabolic rate (BMR) is the energy you spend doing nothing at all — running your heart, brain, kidneys and the repair of every tissue. It is normally the largest single item on the list, around 60 to 70 per cent of the total for most people. There are several equations for estimating it; the Mifflin-St Jeor equation is the one most widely used:

BMR = 10 × mass(kg) + 6.25 × height(cm) − 5 × age(years) + s
where s = +5 for males and −161 for females

For a 16-year-old of 60 kg and 170 cm, using s = +5:

10 × 60 + 6.25 × 170 − 5 × 16 + 5 = 600 + 1062.5 − 80 + 5 = 1587.5 kcal per day

That is what they burn lying completely still for 24 hours. Multiply by an activity factor for everything else they do:

Everyday activity levelMultiplierTotal energy out (kcal/day)
Sedentary — mostly sitting× 1.21905
Lightly active× 1.3752183
Moderately active× 1.552461
Very active× 1.7252738

What most people get wrong

Suppose this person eats 2400 kcal a day and is sedentary. Their balance is 2400 − 1905 = +495 kcal a day. Since about 7700 kcal are stored in a kilogram of body tissue, the obvious calculation is:

495 × 365 ÷ 7700 = 23.5 kg gained in one year

That is the calculation almost everybody does, and it is wrong — not slightly, but structurally. It ignores the purple feedback arrow in Figure 1.30. As mass rises, BMR rises with it, because 10 × mass is in the equation. A heavier body costs more to run, so the daily surplus shrinks by itself.

Recompute BMR every day as the mass changes and the picture is completely different:

TimeBody mass (kg)Energy out now (kcal/day)Daily surplus remaining
Start60.01905+495
3 months65.41969+431
6 months70.12026+374
1 year77.72117+283
2 years87.82239+161
3 years93.82310+90
Eventually101.324000 — steady

The gain in year one is about 17.7 kg, not 23.5. The gain slows every year. And it does not go on forever: there is a mass at which the surplus reaches exactly zero and the body stops changing. You can solve for it directly — the steady mass is the one where energy in equals energy out — and for this person eating 2400 kcal a day while sedentary, that is 101.3 kg.

This is negative feedback, and you met it in Chapter 1.6. A body that gains mass burns more energy, which reduces the surplus that was causing the gain. The response opposes the change, so the system settles at a new steady state instead of running away.

It is also why the two most common beliefs about body mass are both wrong. "If I keep eating this much I will keep gaining forever" is wrong — you will level off. And "I ate one extra biscuit a day for a year so I should have gained 5 kg" is wrong for the same reason — the arithmetic ignores the feedback.

Now change the other variable

Exercise is measured in METs — multiples of resting energy use. The energy cost of a session is roughly

kcal per minute = MET × 3.5 × mass(kg) ÷ 200

For our 60 kg person, a 30-minute session costs:

ActivityMETkcal in 30 minMinutes to burn one 250 kcal snack
Brisk walking4.313555
Swimming, steady5.818341
Basketball6.520537
Cycling, moderate7.523632
Running at 8 km/h8.326129

Look at the last column, because it is the honest and slightly deflating finding of this whole model. Half an hour of running, which is hard work, costs about as much as one snack. Changing what goes in moves the balance further, for less effort, than changing what comes out — which is why "you cannot outrun a bad diet" is not a slogan but an arithmetic result.

That does not make exercise pointless. It raises the "out" arrow permanently rather than once, it preserves muscle so that BMR stays high while mass falls, and every one of its benefits for the heart, lungs, bones and mood is real and independent of body mass.

Every model has a boundary and this one has several. It assumes a fixed intake, which nobody has. It treats all stored tissue as identical, when gaining muscle and gaining fat are different. It ignores the well-documented finding that people who eat less tend to move less without noticing, which flattens the curve further. And it says nothing about health, which is not the same variable as mass.

How much data, how good, and knowing when to stop

The learning target for this chapter asks you to decide on the "types, how much, and accuracy of data needed to produce reliable measurements", and to consider the limitations on precision. Three words in that sentence are used loosely in everyday speech and precisely in science.

WordWhat it meansHow you improve it
AccuracyHow close a measurement is to the true valueCalibrate the instrument; remove systematic errors
PrecisionHow closely repeated measurements agree with each otherBetter technique; a finer instrument; more repeats
ReliabilityWhether you would get the same result if you did it all againRepeats, and a method described tightly enough for someone else to follow

A stopwatch read to 0.01 s is precise. If you start it late every time, it is precise and inaccurate — and no number of repeats will fix that, because repeats only reduce random scatter, never a bias that happens every time.

Two errors, two different remedies, and telling them apart is most of experimental design.

Random error scatters your results either side of the truth. It is what error bars measure, and repeats shrink it — by the square root of the number of repeats.

Systematic error shifts every result the same way. Error bars cannot see it at all, and repeats make it worse, not better, because you become more and more confident about a wrong number. Only a change of method or instrument removes it.

Deciding how many repeats

There is no universal number, and "three because we always do three" is not a reason. What you actually need depends on two things you can estimate before you start: how big an effect you are looking for, and how much your measurements scatter. If the effect is large and the scatter is small — breathing rate at rest versus after three minutes of step-ups — a handful of people will show it clearly. If the effect is small and the scatter is large, you may need hundreds.

The practical approach for a lesson-length investigation: run a few measurements, look at how much they vary, and use that to decide whether your planned number is enough. That is called refining the design, and it is what the "refine the design accordingly" clause in the learning target means.

Naming your limitations properly

A limitation is not an apology and it is not "we could have been more careful". It is a specific, named reason why your data might be wrong, ideally with a direction. Compare:

  • Weak: "Human error may have affected the results."
  • Better: "Breathing rate was counted by eye, and participants were aware they were being watched — people tend to breathe more deliberately when they know someone is counting, which would raise the recorded rate in both conditions."
  • Also good: "Only 6 people were measured, all from the same class and the same age, so the result may not extend to other ages or fitness levels."

The second and third tell a reader something. They also tell you what to change next time, which is the point.

Check your understanding

A student measures one person's breathing rate at rest (14 breaths/min) and after exercise (26 breaths/min) and concludes that exercise raises breathing rate by 12 breaths per minute. Give two separate reasons why that conclusion is not supported, and say what you would do about each.

Reason 1 — no measure of variability. With a single person there is no spread, so there is no way to know whether a difference of 12 is large or small compared with the natural wobble in the measurement. If the same person's resting rate varies between 11 and 18 on different days, a jump to 26 means much less than it looks. The remedy is repeats, either of the same person or across people, so that a standard error can be calculated and an error bar drawn.

Reason 2 — no control of other variables. The two readings were taken at different times, so anything that changed in between is a rival explanation: excitement, a warmer room, having just walked upstairs, or simply knowing they were about to be measured. The remedy is to hold those constant and state that you did — same room, same time of day, same counting method, same rest interval before counting.

A third answer worth credit: the result cannot be generalised to anyone else, because a sample of one tells you about one person.

Two groups of plants are grown with and without a fertiliser. Treated plants average 24 cm ± 5 cm; untreated average 19 cm ± 4 cm (error bars are one standard error). A student writes "the fertiliser increased growth by 5 cm." Evaluate that statement, and say what would strengthen the investigation.

The treated range is 19 to 29 cm and the untreated range is 15 to 23 cm. Those overlap between 19 and 23, so the difference of 5 cm is not convincing on this evidence — a gap this size could arise by chance between two groups that are really the same, given the scatter. The correct statement is that the treated plants were taller on average, but the difference is within the uncertainty of the measurements, so no effect can be claimed.

To strengthen it: increase the number of plants in each group, since the standard error falls as the square root of the number — quadrupling the plants would halve both error bars and could easily separate them. Also check that the two groups differ only in fertiliser: same species, same seed batch, same soil, same pot size, same light, same water volume and schedule. And measure the same way each time, from the soil surface to the highest growing point, ideally by someone who does not know which group they are measuring.

A 60 kg, 170 cm, 16-year-old is sedentary and eats 2400 kcal a day. Their BMR is about 1590 kcal/day. Calculate their daily energy balance, predict the mass gained in the first year using the simple calculation, and then explain why the real answer is smaller.

Energy out = BMR × activity factor = 1587.5 × 1.2 = 1905 kcal/day.
Balance = 2400 − 1905 = +495 kcal/day.
Simple prediction = 495 × 365 ÷ 7700 = 23.5 kg in a year.

The real answer is closer to 17.7 kg, because the simple calculation treats energy out as a constant when it is not. Body mass appears directly in the BMR equation — the term 10 × mass — so every kilogram gained raises BMR by about 10 kcal a day, and raises total energy out by 12 kcal a day once the activity factor is applied. The surplus therefore shrinks continuously as the mass rises: it starts at 495 kcal a day and has fallen to about 283 by the end of the first year.

The same reasoning shows the gain must eventually stop altogether. Mass keeps rising until energy out equals 2400 kcal a day, which happens at about 101 kg. This is a negative feedback loop of exactly the kind in Chapter 1.6: the response — a larger body burning more energy — opposes the change that produced it, so the system settles at a new steady state rather than running away.

Quick check

These mark themselves, and point you back to the right section when an answer goes wrong.

A class investigates breathing rate after exercise. Group A does three minutes of step-ups; Group B does ten minutes. A student calls Group B "the control". Are they right?

  1. Yes — any group that differs from the treated group is a control
  2. No — Group B is a second level of the independent variable, so it is another treatment, not a control
  3. Yes, because ten minutes is long enough to act as a baseline
  4. No — an investigation like this cannot have a control at all

A control group is the condition in which the independent variable is absent. Two different amounts of exercise is a comparison between treatments, which is a perfectly good design — it just does not answer the question "compared with what?" that a control answers.

A student investigating fertiliser waters one set of pots with 5 mL of feed and another with 10 mL, and calls the 5 mL pots the control. What is missing?

  1. Nothing — the lower dose counts as the control because it is the smaller amount
  2. A third dose, so that the trend across the amounts can be seen
  3. A set of pots given no fertiliser at all — that is the condition in which the independent variable is absent
  4. Nothing — a design that compares two amounts cannot have a control

Comparing two doses tells you whether more feed is better. Only a no-feed group tells you whether the feed does anything at all. Both comparisons can sit in the same investigation, and it is the second one that answers the question a control exists to answer — compared with what?

A method states: "We controlled the room temperature, the time of day, and the colour of the participants' shoes." What is wrong with that list?

  1. Room temperature should not have been controlled
  2. Three controlled variables is too many to manage
  3. Controlled variables should not be named in a method
  4. Shoe colour could not have affected breathing rate, so listing it is not rigour — there was nothing to control

The test is a question: what would happen to my dependent variable if this changed? If the answer is "nothing", leave it out. A controlled variable is one that could genuinely have changed your result if you had let it vary.

A method for the step-up investigation controls the room temperature and the time of day, but lets each participant choose their own step height. Apply the test — what would happen to my dependent variable if this changed? — and say what should be done.

  1. Fix one step height for everyone — a taller step is harder work, so letting it vary would change breathing rate
  2. Leave it, since participants differ in height anyway and the effects even out
  3. Leave it, since step height is part of the exercise rather than a variable
  4. Record each participant's step height and name it as a limitation afterwards

The test runs in both directions. It strikes shoe colour off a list, and it puts step height on to one — the variables that matter are the ones with a plausible route to the thing you are measuring. Listing a variable is not controlling it, and forgetting one is the more expensive mistake of the two.

Treated plants average 24 cm ± 5 cm; untreated average 19 cm ± 4 cm, with error bars of one standard error. What can you claim?

  1. That the fertiliser increased growth by 5 cm
  2. That the fertiliser reduced growth, since the bars overlap
  3. That the treated plants were taller on average, but the difference is within the uncertainty, so no effect can be claimed
  4. That the difference is real, because the treated group had the fertiliser applied

The single most useful question you can ask of two bars is whether they overlap. To strengthen this: more plants in each group — the standard error falls as the square root, so quadrupling them halves both bars and might separate them.

Breathing rate at rest averages 15 ± 1 breaths per minute; after three minutes of step-ups it averages 30 ± 2, with error bars of one standard error. What can you claim?

  1. Nothing, because the second mean carries a longer error bar than the first
  2. That the difference is real and therefore caused by the step-ups
  3. Nothing until at least thirty people have been measured
  4. That the difference is much larger than the uncertainty in the two means, so it is unlikely to be chance

Bars that sit well apart and bars that overlap substantially are the two easy cases. Bars that just touch are the case where an eyeball is not enough and a proper statistical test is needed. In every case, separation is a claim about chance — never, on its own, a claim about cause.

Three patient groups have clearly different mean ACR values, well separated relative to their spread. What have you established?

  1. That the groups differ in kidney protein leakage — but nothing at all about what caused the difference
  2. That whatever distinguishes the groups is causing the kidney damage
  3. Nothing, because three groups of ten is far too small a sample
  4. That the ACR test is unreliable, given how much Group 2 varies

Correlation is a statement about the data; causation is a statement about the world, and it needs a mechanism and ideally an experiment where you changed the suspected cause yourself. Observing groups that already differ can only ever support a correlation, however clean the numbers look.

The three ACR groups are cleanly separated, but nothing in the data says what distinguishes the patients. What would be needed before the difference could be called causal?

  1. More patients in each group, since ten is too few to support a causal claim
  2. A mechanism linking the suspected cause to kidney damage, and ideally a study in which the investigator set that cause
  3. A stronger correlation between the groups and the suspected cause
  4. The ACR test repeated on the same patients, to confirm the values

Setting the independent variable yourself is what closes the gap, because groups that already differ carry every other difference along with them. That is exactly the split between the two designs at the start of this chapter: an experiment can support a cause, an observation of existing groups cannot.

A 60 kg person has a daily surplus of 495 kcal. The obvious calculation, 495 × 365 ÷ 7700, predicts 23.5 kg gained in a year. The real figure is about 17.7 kg. Why is the simple calculation wrong?

  1. Because a kilogram of tissue actually stores more than 7700 kcal
  2. Because not all the food eaten is actually absorbed
  3. Because people eat less on some days than others
  4. Because it treats energy out as a constant — but BMR contains 10 × mass, so a heavier body burns more and the surplus shrinks as it goes

Every kilogram gained raises BMR by about 10 kcal a day, and total energy out by about 12 once the activity factor is applied. The surplus falls from 495 to about 283 over the first year — and it keeps falling until it reaches zero, at about 101 kg. This is negative feedback from Chapter 1.6, in a system that is not a body part at all.

The same 60 kg person instead cuts their intake to 1410 kcal a day, a deficit of 495. The simple calculation again predicts a change of 23.5 kg in the first year. Why will the real loss be smaller than that?

  1. Because BMR contains 10 × mass — as mass falls the body costs less to run, so the deficit shrinks and the loss levels off
  2. Because a kilogram of lost tissue releases less energy than a kilogram gained stores
  3. Because the body stops drawing on fat once the store falls past a certain point
  4. Because exercise raises BMR, which cancels out most of the deficit

The feedback runs in both directions. A surplus closes itself as the body grows, and a deficit closes itself as the body shrinks, because the same term — 10 × mass — sits in the BMR equation either way. That is why a fixed cut in intake produces a fast first month and a slow twelfth, and why it ends at a mass rather than continuing indefinitely.

For our 60 kg person, half an hour of running costs about 261 kcal — roughly one snack. What does that arithmetic support?

  1. That exercise is pointless for health
  2. That changing intake moves the energy balance further, for less effort, than changing output does
  3. That only exercise, never diet, can change the steady mass
  4. That body mass cannot be changed at all, since the feedback cancels everything

"You cannot outrun a bad diet" turns out to be an arithmetic result rather than a slogan. That is a useful example of what a model is for: it produced a conclusion most people would not have guessed, from four lines of arithmetic.

Reading the last column of the MET table — 55 minutes of brisk walking to burn one 250 kcal snack — a student argues that exercise is barely worth the time. What is wrong with that conclusion?

  1. Nothing — the arithmetic shows exercise hardly moves the balance at all
  2. The MET values are too low, so the real energy cost of exercise is much higher
  3. Exercise moves the steady mass further than intake does, so it matters more
  4. The column only covers energy balance — exercise also raises the out arrow permanently, preserves muscle so BMR stays high, and benefits the heart, lungs, bones and mood

A model answers the question it was built to answer and no other. This one contains energy in, energy out and a store, so energy is all it can speak about — asking it whether exercise is worthwhile is asking it about variables it does not contain.

A student always starts the stopwatch a little late. They repeat every measurement twenty times. What happens to the error?

  1. It shrinks, because repeats reduce error
  2. It shows up clearly in the error bars, so it can be corrected
  3. It stays exactly as large — and the tight error bars now make a wrong number look more trustworthy
  4. It cancels out, because some readings will be early instead

Two kinds of error, two different remedies. Random error scatters results either side of the truth, and repeats shrink it. Systematic error shifts every result the same way; repeats make it worse, because you become more and more confident about a wrong number. Only a change of method or instrument removes it.

Two students counting the same person's breathing rate record 15 and 18, because they judge shallow breaths differently. Across many counts their readings fall either side of the true value. What will twenty repeats do?

  1. Nothing — this is a systematic error, and repeats cannot touch it
  2. Shrink the error bar on the mean, though only as the square root — quadrupling the repeats halves it
  3. Make the mean accurate, by averaging away the disagreement between the two students
  4. Make the result worse, since more readings means more chances to miscount

Precision is cheap at first and expensive later: 4 measurements to 16 halves the error bar, and it takes 64 to halve it again. Which is why the number of repeats is a judgement about the size of the effect against the scatter, rather than a habit like three.
